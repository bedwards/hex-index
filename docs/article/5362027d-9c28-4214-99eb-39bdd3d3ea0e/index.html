The Ukrainian work-protest balance

Tim Mak · The Counteroffensive ·Jul 16, 2026 ·10 min read
Commentary by Hex Index staff
Original article →

Tim Mak captures a surreal moment in wartime Kyiv where the line between civic duty and professional obligation dissolves into a single act of resistance. This piece is notable not for the political shake-up itself, but for the specific, quiet absurdity of citizens setting up portable desks and power banks in the shadow of the President's office to work while demanding accountability. It reveals a society where the war has fundamentally rewired the relationship between the individual, the state, and the concept of a "workday."

The Co-Working Revolution

Mak's reporting centers on the visual and philosophical dissonance of the scene: protesters chanting for a dismissed Defense Minister while simultaneously answering emails. The author introduces Denys Pidhornyi, a 27-year-old hardware project manager who brought a camping table to the protest. "I decided yesterday that I had to come, but I also couldn't skip work. So I packed everything I needed to work and came here," Mak quotes him saying. This framing is crucial; it suggests that for this generation of Ukrainians, the revolution is not a pause in life, but an extension of it.

The narrative effectively uses the physical setting to underscore the stakes. These are not holiday protesters; they are people who cannot afford to stop their economic engine even as they fight for their political future. Mak notes that Denys even brought bananas and a "BlueYeti" power supply to share, turning the square into a makeshift co-working space. "As a citizen, I can simply see the difference between how things were before and how things are now," Denys tells the reporter. This observation lands with particular weight because it comes from someone who is literally working while speaking it. The article argues that the dismissal of Mykhailo Fedorov was perceived not just as a personnel change, but as a regression in the very efficiency these citizens are trying to maintain.

"We're moving away from fighting with people and toward fighting with technology. I can see this trend and this approach being actively pushed since Fedorov came in."

This quote from product designer Oleksandr Bovlianko, cited by Mak, encapsulates the core grievance of the crowd. The protest wasn't merely about loyalty to a person, but about a specific methodology of war: digitalization, drone innovation, and anti-corruption procurement. Mak highlights that Fedorov's six-month tenure was marked by the disruption of Russian logistics in Crimea and the cutting off of Starlink access for Russian forces. Critics might argue that focusing on a single minister overlooks the broader, collective effort of the military, but Mak's coverage makes a compelling case that the perception of competence is a vital resource in a democracy under martial law. When the public sees a "tech innovator" replaced by a general favoring "classic warfare means," the anxiety is palpable.

The Weight of History and the Absence of Votes

The piece deepens its analysis by connecting the current unrest to the 2013–2014 Revolution of Dignity. Mak profiles Anastasiia Drapaliuk, who carried a sign demanding competence over "Soviet mentality," and notes her participation in the Maidan protests a decade prior. "We've already mastered it—and now we can teach them," she says, referring to the rest of Europe watching Ukrainian civil society. This historical thread adds necessary gravity; it is not merely a policy dispute, but a continuation of a struggle for a specific type of statehood.

However, the article also exposes a stark democratic deficit. Under martial law, there are no elections, leaving the streets as the only lever of influence. "At the very least, he needs to be given more time so he can finish what he started," Oleksandr argues, highlighting the frustration of a populace with no ballot box to turn to. Mak writes that the dismissal occurred without explicit reasoning, leaving a vacuum of transparency that fueled the outrage. "When the only person in the government who is working effectively is dismissed, it raises questions about the country's top leadership—where they're heading and what they're trying to achieve," Drapaliuk observes. This is the article's most uncomfortable truth: in a war for survival, the executive branch's opaque decision-making feels like a luxury the nation cannot afford.

The coverage also touches on the internal friction between the civilian leadership and the military command. Fedorov publicly criticized the Commander-in-Chief, Oleksandr Syrskyi, for blocking reforms. "We found that every initiative we proposed started being blocked... [Syrskyi] is willing to attend private meetings, engage in intrigue," Fedorov stated during a press briefing. Mak presents this not as a petty squabble, but as a fundamental clash of doctrine between asymmetric warfare and traditional ground assaults. While the administration has yet to name a successor, with the parliament on break until August, the uncertainty hangs heavy over the front lines.

Bottom Line

Tim Mak's reporting succeeds by refusing to treat the protest as a standard political rally, instead framing it as a testament to a society that has fused its economic life with its civic resistance. The strongest element is the vivid depiction of the "co-working protest," which humanizes the abstract concept of democratic accountability. The piece's vulnerability lies in its reliance on the protesters' perspective, which, while compelling, offers limited insight into the strategic rationale of the administration's decision to reshuffle its defense leadership. Readers should watch for how the new Minister of Defense navigates the delicate balance between the digital innovation demanded by the public and the traditional military strategy favored by the general staff.

Editor’s Note:

Today, Kyiv is buzzing with political uncertainty as protests unfold over the government’s latest shake-up. While headlines will tell you what happened, our team is on the ground talking to the people living through it.

Will you support us?

OUR LEAD STORY:.

KYIV, Ukraine – Amidst over a thousand protesters in the center of Kyiv, Denys Pidhornyi sat behind a fold-up table, staring intently at his laptop and answering calls as they came in. He was hard at work, as usual, but today, he was working outside of President Zelenskyy’s office.

Denys, 27, believed he had a duty to his work managing projects at an unnamed R&D hardware company, but also a civil duty as a Ukrainian citizen to tell his government that they made a mistake. He brought his own portable power supply, a desk, a computer, food and water.

“I decided yesterday that I had to come, but I also couldn’t skip work. So I packed everything I needed to work and came here.”

Denys has a remote job, which means he can work from anywhere — including smack in the center of a massive protest over Zelenskyy dismissing Minister of Defense Mykhailo Fedorov.

Zelenskyy’s decision to replace Fedorov — though we don’t yet know with whom — sparked mass protests across Ukraine on the morning of July 16, with more than 1,000 protesters near the Office of the President in Kyiv.

Ukrainian protesters chant “Fedorov” near the President’s Office in Kyiv on July 16. Video by The Counteroffensive.

Fedorov’s term lasted less than 6 months and was marked by the development of Ukrainian middle- and deep-strike capabilities, as well as the digitalization of various military processes. Many are frustrated that Zelenskyy, without giving an explicit reasoning, dismissed a politician they deemed an effective wartime MoD. This is not just any position, but one making decisions about defense in a country where successful defense can be life or death for everyday citizens.

Despite facing some criticism, including of his army reforms and his failure to impact mobilization methods, on Thursday, a workday, Ukrainians took to the streets in his defense.

While some marched, others brought their laptops and turned one of the biggest protests during the full-scale invasion into a co-working space of sorts — their obligations to their jobs could not trump the pull to fight for a functional democracy in Ukraine.
