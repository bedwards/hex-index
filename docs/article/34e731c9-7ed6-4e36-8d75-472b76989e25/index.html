Sarah Griffith, writing for Scot McKnight, offers a rare and necessary corrective to the lazy caricature of conservative Christian women as merely "brainwashed" or passive followers of their husbands. Rather than dismissing the political choices of these women, Griffith uses Katie Gaddini's new book, Esther's Army, to argue that their support for a specific political movement is rooted in a complex, decades-long history of female agency, theological conviction, and organized activism. This is not a political endorsement, but a sociological deep dive that forces readers to confront the reality that these women are educated, strategic, and deeply convinced they are protecting their nation.

The Architecture of Belief

The piece begins by dismantling the assumption that conservative women lack intellectual depth or political autonomy. Griffith notes that she initially struggled to understand how women she respected could support a figure so at odds with the values they claimed to cherish. However, Gaddini's research reveals that these women are not reacting in a vacuum; they are the product of institutions that have spent decades training them for leadership. As Griffith observes, "Rather than caricaturing conservative Christian women as brainwashed, uneducated, or simply voting the way their husbands told them to, the author invites us to look deeper."

This reframing is crucial. It shifts the conversation from "why are they so wrong?" to "how did they get here?" The book categorizes these women into six distinct groups, ranging from the "Liberty Flames and Regent Royals"—graduates of conservative universities who are actively entering law and politics—to the "Mama Bears" who organize around school boards. Griffith highlights that these institutions are no longer "holding tanks for women who are 'still in want of a husband,'" but are instead producing capable women who see education as a tool for national influence. This is a significant departure from the mainstream narrative that often portrays evangelical women as intellectually disengaged.

"They are preparing for careers in law, politics, media, and public policy because they genuinely believe they can shape the country's future."

The strength of this section lies in its refusal to simplify. Griffith admits that while she may disagree with their conclusions, the book forces her to recognize their competence. A counterargument worth considering is whether this focus on agency risks excusing harmful political outcomes by attributing too much rationality to decisions driven by fear or misinformation. However, Griffith's point is that understanding the logic of their support is the only path to meaningful dialogue, even if that logic leads to conclusions she finds dangerous.

The Tension of Identity and Power

The commentary then moves to the more contentious groups, particularly the "Powerhouses" and "Influencers." Here, Griffith grapples with the apparent contradiction of women who advocate for traditional domestic roles while simultaneously pursuing high-powered public careers. She notes that Gaddini does not dismiss this as hypocrisy but explores how these women reconcile the two. Griffith confesses her own struggle with this, admitting that her chapter "produced more than a few eye rolls" because of her own history with church leadership that discouraged her from scholarship.

The piece shines when it addresses the role of digital media in amplifying these voices. The "Influencers" chapter demonstrates how distrust of traditional journalism has allowed women to build massive audiences without editorial gatekeepers. Griffith is critical of the commercial aspect, noting, "I couldn't get past the fact that many seem to find a niche that makes women feel like they are falling short, only to offer a product, a class, or a program that promises to help them become better wives, mothers, or Christians." This critique of the algorithmic economy is vital context that the book touches on but perhaps doesn't fully dissect.

"The fears expressed by many of these women are real to them, but I kept asking what lies beneath those fears, who benefits from continually reinforcing them, and how much today's algorithms reward the voices that keep those fears alive."

This questioning of the underlying economic and technological incentives is where Griffith's commentary adds the most value. She suggests that the rise of these voices is not just about theology or politics, but about a system that rewards fear and division. Critics might argue that this analysis overlooks the genuine, non-financial motivations of these women, but the point stands: the ecosystem in which they operate is designed to amplify their anxieties.

The Suburban Fear Factor

Finally, the piece turns to the "White Suburbanites," a group mobilized by the perception that their way of life is under threat. Griffith, who lives in a similar environment, admits to feeling "embarrassment" as she recognized her own community in the book's interviews. She argues that the administration's success with this demographic came from speaking directly to their fears, while opponents dismissed those fears outright. The result, she notes, was that "the rhetoric of fear seemed to crowd out the kind of love, hospitality, and welcome I see Jesus calling his followers to embody."

This is the emotional core of the commentary. It moves beyond political strategy to the spiritual and human cost of a politics built on fear. Griffith's personal reflection adds a layer of authenticity that academic analysis often lacks. She acknowledges that while the fears may be perceived rather than real, they are undeniably powerful drivers of political behavior. The book's greatest strength, she concludes, is that it "reminds us that meaningful conversations can only happen with human beings, not stereotypes."

"By replacing caricatures with people, she reminds us that meaningful conversations can only happen with human beings, not stereotypes."

Bottom Line

Sarah Griffith's commentary on Esther's Army succeeds because it refuses to look away from the complexity of conservative Christian women's political engagement. The strongest part of the argument is its insistence on seeing these women as agents of their own destiny, shaped by institutions and fears that are often misunderstood by outsiders. The biggest vulnerability lies in the difficulty of translating this understanding into political change when the underlying fears are so deeply entrenched. Readers should watch for how these dynamics evolve as the digital media landscape continues to reshape the relationship between faith, fear, and public policy.

By Sarah Griffith

A few years ago, I found myself at a loss to understand how so many of my Christian sisters could support Donald Trump.

I don’t ask that question as someone who has always stood outside conservative politics, throwing stones. I ask it as someone who grew up in a conservative military family, voted for Trump in 2016, and genuinely believed I was supporting the values I had been taught to cherish.

Today, I no longer support Trump because it has become too difficult to reconcile those values with the man who claimed to represent them. What surprised me even more, however, was watching many of the women I love and respect continue to support him with enthusiasm. I wasn’t looking for a political argument. I was trying to understand why they supported a man who seemed so at odds with the very things we claimed to hold dear. They had their reasons—I simply couldn’t see them.

I picked up Katie Gaddini’s book, Esther’s Army, hoping to understand what I couldn’t explain. By the final page, I understood the people I loved a little better as well.

Rather than caricaturing conservative Christian women as brainwashed, uneducated, or simply voting the way their husbands told them to, the author invites us to look deeper. She argues that the only way forward is to understand who these women are, what has shaped them, and what they fear losing.

Tracing the history of the conservative movement, particularly as it developed alongside American evangelicalism, the author demonstrates that many of its defining convictions were not simply handed down by influential men. They were cultivated, organized, and advanced by Christian women who believed they were protecting their families, their faith, and ultimately their nation. A particular vision of “biblical” womanhood and motherhood becomes the thread tying these women together.

To present her decade-long research, Gaddini organizes conservative women into six broad groups: Liberty Flames and Regent Royals, Mama Bears, Blexiters, Powerhouses, Influencers, and White Suburbanites. Each represents a different pathway into modern conservative activism.
