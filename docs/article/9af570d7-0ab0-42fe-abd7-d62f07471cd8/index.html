Announcing our new series: Unlikely spiritual practices

Sarah Bessey · Sarah Bessey's Field Notes ·Jul 13, 2026 ·10 min read
Commentary by Hex Index staff
Deep dives· Original article →

In a cultural moment defined by burnout and rigid religious performance, Sarah Bessey challenges the very architecture of modern faith formation. She argues that the path to spiritual vitality for the disillusioned isn't a return to stricter discipline, but a radical reimagining of what counts as a spiritual practice at all.

The Architecture of Exhaustion

Bessey opens by diagnosing a specific phenomenon: the collapse of the "guaranteed" spiritual life. She writes, "If we did those things, we were promised we would experience spiritual growth, complete certainty in all things, success, a clear complexion, healthy relationships, sturdy mental health, and a cheerful disposition! Guaranteed!" This framing is effective because it names the transactional nature of much contemporary piety, where faith is treated as a behavior chart rather than a relationship.

The author suggests that for many, the traditional toolkit—early morning devotions, rigid Bible reading plans, and performative service—has become not just ineffective, but actively painful. She notes, "We need to find that elusive spot between ancient practices and new experiences, sturdy order and a bit of healthy chaos too." This is a crucial pivot. Bessey isn't advocating for spiritual anarchy; she is calling for a synthesis that honors the need for structure without demanding the rigidity that caused the fracture.

Critics might argue that dismantling established disciplines leaves believers without a compass, potentially leading to a subjective faith that lacks historical grounding. However, Bessey counters this by anchoring her proposal in deep tradition rather than novelty. She invokes the 14th-century mystic Julian of Norwich, noting that Julian described God as "completely relaxed and courteous." By invoking a medieval theologian to justify relaxation, Bessey effectively sidesteps the accusation that she is lowering the bar for holiness; instead, she is raising the bar for authenticity.

This series is for the drop-outs of Quiet Time. It's for the ones who burnt out on carefully-completed Bible studies.

Rebuilding Without the Blueprint

The core of Bessey's argument is that the current global climate of trauma and intensity makes the old methods not just obsolete, but dangerous. She observes, "Basically, it is taking A LOT to stay a person right now." In this context, the demand for high-performance spirituality feels like an insult to the human condition. Bessey posits that "there is no better time to cultivate a rich, resilient, beautiful interior life than when everything else is on fire."

This reframing is powerful. It shifts the goal of spiritual practice from "earning favor" to "survival and flourishing." She writes, "This series isn't about earning stars on some mythical God Behaviour Chart that God is secretly keeping in heaven." The language here is deliberately unburdened. She rejects the idea of a divine scorecard, replacing it with a vision of faith that honors "your own need for gentleness, faithfulness, and flourishing in these very days."

Bessey also addresses the specific demographic of those who were once the "Faithful Ones"—the leaders, the Bible nerds, the small group hosts. She acknowledges the unique grief of those whose faith evolved precisely because they took it too seriously. "The ones whose faith began evolving not because they were losing faith but because they were so incredibly serious about their faith," she writes. This nuance is vital; it validates the intellectual and emotional rigor of deconstruction, rather than dismissing it as a failure of will.

A counterargument worth considering is whether this approach risks creating a faith that is too comfortable, lacking the friction necessary for genuine transformation. If spiritual practice is solely about "relaxing," does it lose its prophetic edge? Bessey seems to anticipate this by emphasizing that the goal is not to do "absolutely nothing," but to find practices that are "joyful, loving, beautiful, playful, and light." The inclusion of "playful" suggests an active, engaged spirituality, even if it looks different from the somber discipline of the past.

The Methodology of the Unexpected

The series is structured not as a rigid curriculum, but as a "spiritual scavenger hunt." Bessey promises to write essays in real-time, admitting, "I may be as surprised as you at what develops over the coming weeks." This lack of a pre-written syllabus is a deliberate stylistic choice that mirrors the content: faith as an unfolding discovery rather than a static set of doctrines.

She plans to explore the boundaries of what constitutes a practice, starting with the question of definition itself. "As we work through this series, I think half of what we'll do here is simply name, honour, expand, and bless the ways you are already experiencing a spiritual practice." This is perhaps the most generous move in the piece. It suggests that the reader is already doing the work, and the series is merely a lens to help them see it.

Bessey also integrates the medium into the message by returning to audio formats, acknowledging that "a lot of us prefer listening to reading these days." By offering the essays as audio, she lowers the barrier to entry for those who may be too exhausted to read but still crave connection. It is a practical application of her theology: meeting people where they are, in the format that best serves their current capacity.

Bottom Line

Sarah Bessey's argument succeeds because it refuses to pathologize the exhaustion of the modern believer, instead treating it as a rational response to a world on fire. Her strongest move is reframing "relaxation" not as spiritual laziness, but as a return to the ancient, radical truth of a God who is "completely relaxed and courteous." The series' biggest vulnerability lies in its reliance on the reader's willingness to trust a process that feels unstructured, but for those who have already burned their old maps, that unstructured path may be the only one left to walk.

Why Unlikely Spiritual Practices?.

Like a lot of us, I was taught that if I wanted to be close to God that I should definitely:

go to church every time the doors were open,

get up early in the morning for “devotions” AKA devos,

read my Bible,

pray in an acceptable fashion, and

read a faith-based book and

maybe with a bit of “service” or “tithing 10%” tossed in as well.

If we did those things, we were promised we would experience spiritual growth, complete certainty in all things, success, a clear complexion, healthy relationships, sturdy mental health, and a cheerful disposition! Guaranteed!

Annnnnnnd cut to burning it all down in deconstruction.

(That part can take years.)

So now we’re here.

As we live into our evolving faith on the other side of that clarifying experience, we’re ready to start rebuilding. We’re ready to reimagine a faith that works, not only for us, but for this world that God so loves. A lot of us are experiencing a yearning to purposely reconnect with God that surprises us but somehow those old familiar pathways - read your Bible, pray this way using an acronym, read approved books - feel a bit hollow at best, maybe painful at worst. We need to find that elusive spot between ancient practices and new experiences, sturdy order and a bit of healthy chaos too.

We’re left wondering, how do I purposely make room in my life for the pursuit or experience of God without doing the same things I used to do? How do I feel “close to God” now? What spiritual practices are still available to me? Are there other ways to connect with God than the ones I was taught? There has to be something between Performing All The Spiritual Disciplines As Before and Doing Absolutely Nothing.
