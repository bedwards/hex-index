The artist who colored ghibli

Various · Animation Obsessive ·Jul 26, 2026 ·16 min read
Commentary by Hex Index staff
Original article →

In an era where artificial intelligence can mimic a style in seconds, this piece makes a startling claim: the soul of Studio Ghibli's animation cannot be replicated because it relies on a human sensibility that predates digital tools. The article argues that the studio's signature look isn't a formula, but the result of a single, often overlooked artist, Michiyo Yasuda, whose decisions turned flat drawings into living, breathing worlds. For busy readers seeking depth in a saturated media landscape, this is a reminder that true innovation often hides in the quiet, technical choices of unsung collaborators.

The Architecture of Light

The piece centers on a fundamental truth about Ghibli's success: its visual language is built on texture and subtlety, not just line work. Animation Obsessive reports that even when machine-generated images vaguely recall the studio's aesthetic, "it's not so simple to paint light like Kazuo Oga did in My Neighbor Totoro, or to animate a smile like Makiko Futaki did across the '80s." This distinction is crucial. While modern algorithms excel at pattern recognition, they struggle with the intentionality behind a specific hue. The article posits that Yasuda's genius lay in her ability to choose colors that conveyed physical reality—weight, fabric thickness, and age—rather than just aesthetic appeal.

Isao Takahata, the director of Grave of the Fireflies, is quoted in the piece noting that Yasuda's choices went beyond simple color selection. He observed, "She chose a color that looked faded from many washings. Although I say 'chose a color,' in reality, she was choosing texture. Whether the fabric is thick or flimsy depends on the color chosen." This insight reframes the role of a color designer from a decorator to a storyteller. By linking color to the physical history of an object, Yasuda grounded the fantastical elements of Ghibli's films in a tangible reality. Critics might argue that this focus on realism limits the scope of animation as a medium for pure abstraction, but the article suggests that this grounding is precisely what makes Ghibli's fantasy feel accessible.

"The reason animation films suffer creatively has to do with the fact that they all use the same colors... I believe that Ghibli is the only studio that has really managed to develop such unique colors."

The Rejection of the Flashy

The commentary dives into the historical context of the 1980s, a time when the industry was shifting toward brighter, more artificial palettes. Animation Obsessive notes that Yasuda actively resisted this trend, creating her own paints to achieve a "feeling of transparency" that allowed viewers to "sense the air" in a scene. She explained her motivation by stating, "At the time, society was becoming busier and busier, and there were lots of strong, flashy plastic colors. So, looking for a little more gentleness, I set out to create my own paints." This decision was not merely artistic but cultural; it was a deliberate pushback against the noise of modern life.

The piece draws a parallel to the evolution of traditional animation, where the transition from hand-painted cels to digital tools often flattened the image. Yasuda's work on Princess Mononoke and My Neighbors the Yamadas demonstrates her ability to adapt her philosophy to new technology without losing the "restrained" quality that defined her earlier work. The article highlights her superhuman memory for color and her understanding of how paints interact with film stock, noting that she could create an impression of depth where others saw only flatness. This aligns with the broader history of animation, where the shift from the rigid structures of early ink-and-paint to the fluidity of digital often required a rethinking of light and shadow. Yasuda's realization that "there is color even in shadows" revolutionized how the studio approached darkness, moving away from default blacks to deep, vivid tones.

The Human Cost of Perfection

While the article celebrates Yasuda's achievements, it does not shy away from the physical toll of her craft. The piece describes a grueling work culture where Yasuda lived at the office, her writing arm swelling from overuse and her weight dropping to 88 pounds during the production of Anne of Green Gables. This context adds a layer of gravity to the discussion of artistic excellence. The editors note that her dedication was fueled by a deep observation of the world, from the way light hits a dead leaf in a puddle to the blurred patterns on distant clothing. "I started observing things constantly," she recalled, detailing how she used these real-world observations to inform her palette. This relentless pursuit of truth, however, came at a personal cost that the industry often overlooks in favor of the final product.

The article also touches on the collaborative friction between Yasuda and directors like Miyazaki, portraying them as "old friends" who fought over the nuances of color. In one instance, Miyazaki insisted on a high-contrast pairing that Yasuda found "unthinkable," leading to a compromise that ultimately served the film better. This dynamic underscores the piece's central argument: Ghibli's magic was not the result of a single visionary, but a constant negotiation between different artistic sensibilities. As the piece concludes, "Even Studio Ghibli hasn't come up with a defined color scheme because it's always evolving according to the approaches and..." the specific needs of each film.

Bottom Line

The strongest element of this coverage is its recentering of the narrative from the famous directors to the technical artist who gave their worlds life, proving that the "Ghibli look" is a human achievement, not a software preset. Its biggest vulnerability is a slight romanticization of the grueling work conditions, though it does acknowledge the physical toll. Readers should watch for how contemporary digital tools attempt to replicate this human nuance, a challenge that remains as relevant today as it was in the 1980s.

1. Ghibli’s colors.

The art of Studio Ghibli isn’t really duplicable. People have tried: the internet is full of attempts, the bulk of them machine-generated. Even when those images vaguely recall Ghibli, though, it’s not so simple to paint light like Kazuo Oga did in My Neighbor Totoro, or to animate a smile like Makiko Futaki did across the ‘80s.

Many tiny and hyper-specific choices, down to the individual frames, made Ghibli films what they are. These choices came from stars like Hayao Miyazaki and Isao Takahata, but also, critically, from the way less famous people on their teams.

Among the most central of the less-famous people, for most of Ghibli’s existence, was Michiyo Yasuda. She’s far from a household name. Yet the one-of-a-kind colors of Ghibli’s animation, which shape the whole look and feel of its films, were selected mainly by her.

Miyazaki once called Yasuda “three times sharper and more particular than me.”1 Takahata, who worked with her on Grave of the Fireflies (1988), said glowing things about her abilities. He pointed to the worn-out pants of one character in that film:

[S]he chose a color that looked faded from many washings. Although I say ‘chose a color,’ in reality, she was choosing texture. Whether the fabric is thick or flimsy depends on the color chosen. Yasuda-san has a subtle perception that allows her to grasp such things.2

As Ghibli got established, its colors stood apart. They were more nuanced, more subdued, richer than the norm. That was Yasuda’s area. “The reason animation films suffer creatively has to do with the fact that they all use the same colors,” Miyazaki argued in the ‘80s. “It’s like, ‘Oh, the color of animation.’ … I believe that Ghibli is the only studio that has really managed to develop such unique colors.”3
