Gerontocracy's failure mode

Cory Doctorow · Pluralistic ·Jul 14, 2026 ·12 min read
Commentary by Hex Index staff
Deep dives· Original article →

Cory Doctorow reframes the American political crisis not as a clash of ideologies, but as a catastrophic failure of basic risk management. While most observers fixate on policy disputes, Doctorow argues that the true existential threat to the republic is the sheer statistical probability of death and incapacity among its ruling class. This is not a partisan critique; it is an actuarial one, suggesting that the entire system is running on a logic that ignores the most fundamental laws of biology.

The Designated Survivor Paradox

Doctorow opens by highlighting the absurdity of the "designated survivor" protocol—a Cold War relic where a single official is hidden in a bunker during the State of the Union to ensure continuity of government after a decapitation strike. He contrasts this paranoia with the current reality of American governance, noting that while the procedure was designed for a singular, spectacular disaster, the actual threat is a slow, grinding erosion of capacity.

"The problem is that once the atavistic, impulsive elements of your project escape containment, the resultant turbulence sucks everyone else into their chaotic vortex," Doctorow writes. He argues that the administration and legislative bodies are currently too brittle to withstand the chaos of daily stunts because they lack the depth to plan for the long term. This framing is compelling because it shifts the blame from individual malice to systemic fragility.

The author draws a sharp parallel between political succession and literary estate planning. Just as a writer must ensure their work survives them, a nation must ensure its leadership does not vanish unexpectedly. Doctorow points out that while he has arranged for a younger executor to handle his estate in case his primary choice passes first, the political class refuses to make similar calculations.

"The question here isn't whether old people can be good at those jobs. They obviously can be. The problem is actuarial: old people are far more likely to die, or suffer severe medical episodes, than younger people."

This observation lands with visceral force. It strips away the polite fictions of political campaigns and focuses on the cold math of mortality. The argument is strengthened by the historical context of the "designated survivor" itself, a procedure initiated in the 1950s that assumed a technocratic, long-termist approach to governance—an approach that has since been replaced by what Doctorow calls "vibes-based" chaos.

Critics might argue that focusing on age distracts from policy failures, but Doctorow counters that the inability to plan is the root cause of the policy failures. When leadership is perpetually on the brink of a medical crisis, long-term strategy becomes impossible.

The Cost of Hubris

The commentary deepens as Doctorow examines specific historical failures where the refusal to plan for succession led to disaster. He cites the case of Ruth Bader Ginsburg, whose refusal to step down while still capable of serving was a strategic miscalculation that altered the trajectory of the Supreme Court.

"The amazing thing here is that RBG made her name as a master strategist, but when it came to this incredibly consequential matter, she set strategy aside for hubris."

This is a devastating critique of institutional preservation. Doctorow suggests that the desire to hand-pick a successor often overrides the need for a stable transition, a dynamic that has played out repeatedly in recent years. The reference to Dianne Feinstein, who held onto power long after her capacity had clearly diminished, serves as a grim reminder of how special elections triggered by sudden deaths can have "wild consequences" that upend legislative majorities.

The author also touches on the broader cultural failure to accept mortality. He notes that in the Senate, where majorities are often razor-thin, the presence of nonagenarians and octogenarians creates a "failure mode" where the entire political project hangs on the health of a few individuals.

"Anyone who pretends this isn't true is setting themselves and the people around them up for terrible things."

This statement cuts through the noise of political punditry. It suggests that the current political environment is not just inefficient, but actively dangerous because it relies on the improbable assumption that the elderly will remain fit for office indefinitely. The comparison to Stephen James Joyce, who notoriously stifled the legacy of his grandfather James Joyce, serves as a cautionary tale about what happens when the wrong person inherits power without proper vetting or succession planning.

"If you're part of a political project that's laying and executing long-term plans whose outcomes turn on hair-fine majorities, this should factor into your thinking."

The Actuarial Reality

Doctorow concludes by returning to the concept of security. In cybersecurity, the biggest vulnerability is often the belief that one is immune to attack. He argues that politicians in their 70s and 80s suffer from a similar delusion, believing they are immune to the biological realities that affect everyone else.

"By the same token, any politician in their 70s or 80s who thinks that they can't suffer a stroke or heart attack or the kind of lapse that makes you freeze up during a presidential debate is a danger to their party, their politics and their nation."

This section effectively synthesizes the article's themes: the danger of hubris, the necessity of contingency planning, and the high cost of ignoring actuarial tables. The argument is that the "gerontocracy" is not just an aesthetic problem; it is a structural flaw that threatens the continuity of the state.

"The failure to do so can throw everything you've worked for into disarray."

The piece avoids the trap of ageism by clarifying that age itself is not the enemy, but the refusal to acknowledge the risks associated with it is. The argument holds up because it relies on data and historical precedent rather than personal attacks. It forces the reader to consider the stability of institutions that are, in essence, gambling with the lives of their leaders.

Bottom Line

Doctorow's most potent contribution is the shift from asking "who is winning?" to "what happens when the leaders can no longer lead?" The argument is strongest in its use of actuarial logic to expose the fragility of a system built on the endurance of the elderly. Its biggest vulnerability is that it offers no clear political path to fix the demographic imbalance, leaving the reader with a diagnosis of a terminal condition rather than a cure. The immediate takeaway is that until the political class treats succession planning with the same seriousness as national security, the risk of sudden, catastrophic disruption remains unacceptably high.

Gerontocracy's failure mode (permalink).

The "designated survivor" is one of the weirder aspects of America's (very, very weird) political system.

Each year, during the State of the Union address, when both houses of Congress and the President are all under one roof, a single political figure, in the line of succession for the presidency, is spirited away to a hidden bunker, just in case the US legislative and administrative branches are decapitated in a single, spectacular terrorist strike:

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Designated_survivor

Initiated during the 1950s, designated survivors are a paranoid relic of the Cold War, but they're also a relic of an era when America was a less chud-dominated, more technocratic land. It's a longtermist sort of procedure, in stark opposition to vibes-based MAGA chaos in which the Mad King makes daily announcements of new wars, tariffs, monuments, and existential threats to the nation.

America's ruling class have always sought an equilibrium between its pure Id of hatred for labor, autocratic yearnings and apocalyptic fantasies, and its patient, scheming Ego, the author of endless FedSoc judicial nominee listings, Projects 2025, and decades-long schemes to overturn Roe and reverse the New Deal.

(Democrats have their own version of this, of course – the endless contest between the McKinsey wing of the party's right and its infinitely embroidered Machin-Synematic Universe.)

The problem is that once the atavistic, impulsive elements of your project escape containment, the resultant turbulence sucks everyone else into their chaotic vortex. How can you plan for anything when you're buffeted by endless stunts, feints, and distractions?

Nowhere is this failure to plan more vivid than in the age distribution of both chambers of the US legislature, its presidential candidates, and its judicial appointments. What's more, this is equally true of the Democrats and the Republicans.
