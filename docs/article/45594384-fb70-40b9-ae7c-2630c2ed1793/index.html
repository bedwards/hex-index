I have never been working class

Noah Smith · Noahpinion ·Jul 12, 2026 ·15 min read
Commentary by Hex Index staff
Original article →

Noah Smith challenges a foundational assumption of modern political discourse: that class is defined by your current bank account or your zip code. Instead, he argues that in America, class is fundamentally about future potential and human capital, a distinction that explains why the political left is losing its traditional base. This is not just a semantic debate; it is a diagnosis of a widening cultural chasm that threatens the viability of progressive coalitions.

The Illusion of Shared Struggle

Smith begins by dismantling his own biography to make a broader point. He recounts growing up in a "small house with only one bathroom shared between four people," driving a "beat-up used Toyota Tercel," and even "picking cotton by hand" for a research project. Yet, despite these material hardships, he asserts, "I have never once considered myself part of the working class." The reason, he explains, is that he viewed class not as a "present snapshot of someone's lifestyle" but as a measure of "potential."

This framing is crucial because it aligns with economic theories like the Permanent Income Hypothesis, which suggests that people spend based on their expected lifetime earnings rather than their current cash flow. Smith writes, "If you're a shabby grad student living off of cup ramen, your current income is low, but your permanent income is high, because you know you're probably going to make a lot of money in the future." He contrasts this with the reality of millions of Americans for whom high-paying careers were "just never an option, and never will be." The argument holds water because it explains why a struggling graduate student might feel culturally distinct from a truck driver, even if their current paychecks are similar.

However, this perspective risks overlooking the psychological toll of precariousness. Critics might argue that defining class solely by potential ignores the immediate, crushing reality of debt and instability that prevents many from acting on that potential, regardless of their education level.

The Disconnect of the Professional-Managerial Class

The piece takes a sharper turn when analyzing why the Democratic Party and the socialist left are failing to connect with low-income voters. Smith points out that while the working class has drifted toward the executive branch's populist appeals, the left has become dominated by the "professional-managerial class" (PMC). He cites the Ehrenreichs' 1977 essay, noting that the PMC now forms the Democratic Party's "most important voter base," with DSA members holding degrees at twice the national average.

Smith observes that this elite often projects their own struggles onto the working class, creating a distorted view of who belongs where. He recalls a law student who defined the working class as "a sex worker who had a bunch of student loans and a humanities degree that she wasn't able to use." This anecdote illustrates a profound cultural blindness. As Smith puts it, "The integrating institutions that once pushed us together across class lines — church, the military, school — are gone." Without these shared spaces, the educated elite cannot imagine the lives of those without degrees, leading to a political strategy based on "vibes" rather than reality.

"If Trump is 'a poor person's idea of a rich person,' Platner is basically the opposite — a rich person's idea of a working-class person."

The Graham Platner saga serves as Smith's primary evidence of this disconnect. He describes how recruiters, including a sociology PhD student and the heir to the Toys "R" Us fortune, recruited an oyster farmer and Marine veteran who they felt "seemed rough and tough." Smith argues they picked him based entirely on stereotypes, treating him as a "messianic figure" who could win back blue-collar voters. The result was a campaign that collapsed under the weight of its own inauthenticity. This suggests that the left's attempt to co-opt working-class identity is not just ineffective; it is patronizing.

The Future of Class Politics

Smith concludes that the cultural gap is now so wide that Americans seem "unable to even imagine what Americans from other social classes are like." He warns that bridging this divide will require more than just finding a new candidate who fits a stereotype. "It would probably require moderating on social issues — DEI, immigration, etc. — instead of just running candidates that pattern-match to the kind of people that bullied rich lefty kids back in junior high." This is a stark admission that the current progressive coalition may be incompatible with the working-class base it claims to represent.

The argument is compelling because it moves beyond policy prescriptions to address the deep-seated cultural alienation that drives political behavior. It suggests that the failure of the left is not just about economics, but about a failure of empathy and imagination. Yet, one could counter that the demand to moderate on social issues may simply alienate the very progressive base that funds and organizes these movements, creating a political catch-22.

Bottom Line

Noah Smith's most powerful insight is that the American working class is defined by a lack of upward mobility, not just by current income, and the political left has failed to recognize this distinction. The piece's greatest vulnerability is its potential to justify a retreat from social justice issues as a purely transactional political move, which could further erode the moral foundation of the movement. Readers should watch for whether the Democratic Party attempts to address this cultural gap through policy or simply through candidate selection.

I have never been working class

by Noah Smith · Noahpinion · Read full article

I grew up in the 1980s in a small house with only one bathroom shared between four people. The floor was linoleum. There was a carport instead of a garage, and we had one beat-up used Toyota Tercel hatchback. I don’t remember when we got our first color TV, but when I was young we had a black-and-white one that my grandmother gave us. Our furniture was all second-hand and we kept the couches covered up with worn old blankets.

When I was young, I mowed lawns for money. As a high school kid, I signed up to pick cotton by hand (!!) for an agricultural research project at Texas A&M University, for minimum wage1. I have also worked as a cashier. Twice in my life, I have been a member of a labor union, and I have marched in a strike.

I have never once considered myself part of the working class.

Why not? Because I have never thought of class as being defined by a present snapshot of someone’s lifestyle or material circumstances. Instead, I always thought of class as being about someone’s potential. And I grew up always knowing that my economic potential went far beyond the rather humble circumstances of my early childhood.

For one thing, my family was upwardly mobile. My grandparents could probably be called “working class” in their youth — my grandmother worked in a sweatshop as a teenager, my grandfather wore cardboard in his soles because his family couldn’t afford shoes. But after World War 2, thanks to the GI Bill and rapid economic growth, my ancestors advanced into the middle class, with jobs like optometrist, athletic coach, and registered nurse. My father had a PhD and a tenure-track academic job that promised to pay a lot more after a decade of work. We weren’t rocketing up the income distribution, but we were clearly climbing.

Our humble lifestyle in the early and mid 1980s reflected this future orientation. Our family income was probably around the 35th-38th percentile,2 but this was because we were a one-earner family. My mother chose to spend the first seven years of my life as a housewife — which she did in order to make sure my sister and I got a thorough, accelerated education. We lived an abstemious life in part because we saved as much money as we could.
