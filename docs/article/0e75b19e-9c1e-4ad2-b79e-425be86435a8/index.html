We must save sociology from itself

Yascha Mounk · Persuasion ·Aug 10, 2026 ·10 min read
Commentary by Hex Index staff
Deep dives· Original article →

Yascha Mounk delivers a startling diagnosis for a discipline that has quietly lost its way: sociology is no longer a tool for understanding the world, but a vehicle for confirming the biases of its practitioners. In an era where the field faces a 50-to-1 liberal-to-conservative imbalance and plummeting enrollment, Mounk argues that the only path to survival is a return to rigorous, falsifiable science rather than ideological activism.

The Crisis of Credibility

Mounk opens by recounting his own introduction to the field in the early 1990s, where concepts like "white supremacy" were novel lenses that illuminated hidden social dynamics. He notes that while this perspective was necessary then, the discipline has since calcified. "Sociology has been a field of ideologically progressive concepts in search of supporting evidence for so long now that it is in a serious crisis," Mounk writes. The evidence he presents is stark: membership in the American Sociological Association has dropped by a third since its 1972 peak, and undergraduate majors have plummeted even faster.

The author suggests that this loss of trust is self-inflicted. When a field becomes an echo chamber, it fails its primary function. "Employers don't trust sociology enough to hire its graduates. Students don't trust the professors enough to major in the field. Professors don't trust each other's research," Mounk observes. This erosion of credibility has invited external intervention, with the Ron DeSantis administration in Florida removing introductory sociology from general education curricula, claiming the discipline had been "hijacked by left-wing activists." Mounk concedes a hard truth here: "The administration was not wrong."

Critics might argue that the removal of a major from a curriculum is a political overreach that ignores the field's historical role in highlighting systemic inequities that other disciplines miss. However, Mounk's point is not that the field should be silenced, but that its current inability to self-correct has made it vulnerable to external censorship.

The cardinal sin of the social researcher is squeezing the world into his worldview.

The Personal Failure of Theory

To illustrate the danger of forcing data to fit a narrative, Mounk shares a revealing anecdote from his doctoral work at Oxford. He entered his research intending to prove that a specific group of South Asian Muslims was primarily shaped by the invisible hand of racism. Instead, his subjects told him a different story. "I can't seem to be present at the moments where racism occurs... Maybe they don't see racism impacting their lives as much as you expected—or as much as you want racism to have impacted their lives," his advisor, Geoffrey Walford, told him.

This moment of dissonance is the crux of Mounk's argument. He realized that his subjects were not defined by victimhood but by their religious inspiration. "Sociology is meant to explore how your subjects understand their own lives, not how you want them to understand their lives," he writes. By adhering to the dogma of the day, he nearly missed the actual sociological insight: the profound role of faith in modern identity. This mirrors the broader issue in the field, where the "antiracist, decolonial, feminist, queer and transnational theorizing" championed by the American Sociological Association has become a rigid filter that excludes other vital truths.

Mounk contrasts this with the rigor of Karl Popper's "falsifiability" test, urging the discipline to "proactively seeking examples that challenge the theory rather than simply looking for more cases that confirm it." The field has drifted so far that, as Christian Smith, a prominent sociologist of religion, noted, wielding sociology as an activist tool "tends to produce sectarian narrowness, exclusion, uniformity, silencing, self-censorship, lack of debate, the negative sanctioning of non-conformists, and the debilitation of rigorous thought."

The Path Forward

The author warns that without a return to scientific integrity, sociology risks becoming indistinguishable from the "guy on the next bar stool spouting off opinions." The stakes are high because the discipline is uniquely positioned to identify patterns that are not part of mainstream discourse. "Only then can we hope to improve it for the better," Mounk concludes, emphasizing that in an era of tectonic changes, society needs a discipline that genuinely explains the world, not one that merely validates a specific political worldview.

Bottom Line

Mounk's most compelling argument is that the field's ideological rigidity is not just a moral failing but a practical suicide pact that has already driven away students, employers, and even the academic establishment. The piece's greatest vulnerability lies in its potential to be co-opted by those seeking to dismiss all critical race analysis as mere dogma, yet its core demand—that sociology must prioritize empirical truth over political utility—is an urgent and necessary call to action.

by Yascha Mounk · Persuasion · Read full article

It was in sociology classes that I first heard the phrase “white supremacy.” Quite naturally, the first thing I thought of was people in hoods. But my sociology professor defined white supremacy as something quite different: the assumption that the cultural patterns associated with white people—from clothes to food to family structure—are normal, alongside the assumption that the patterns associated with ethnic and racial minorities are inferior.

A light bulb went off in my head. The concept of white supremacy helped illuminate whole dimensions of my life that I had hitherto hidden. For example, it reminded me of a specific event during my sophomore year in high school. My mom offered to make biryani for Thanksgiving instead of turkey. Her reasoning was sound: Thanksgiving was a holiday about gratitude celebrated with feast foods. Turkey, cranberry sauce, and stuffing were the feast foods of a particular ethnic and religious culture (white Protestant Americans). But we came from a different ethnic and religious culture (South Asian Ismaili Muslim Americans). We had feast foods too, biryani being a main one.

The choice was easy. Turkey is tough and flavorless, no matter how much cranberry sauce you put on it. Biryani is proof of God’s existence.

I did not tell a soul in my high school that I ate biryani for Thanksgiving. Sociology gave me the conceptual language to explain why: Because American society views the cultures associated with white people as superior to the cultures associated with ethnic minorities.

Sociology was like an alternate universe full of concepts that helped me see parts of my life experience I had not fully explored, and professors who validated me for sharing them. My term papers as an undergraduate basically involved writing an opening paragraph about power, privilege, and oppression as it related to this or that minority identity, and then finding the data to support my thesis.

It is important to say that this was 1993. It’s not like there wasn’t real racism. I had grown up in the white western suburbs of Chicago during the Reagan-Bush era, where racist tropes (welfare queens, Willie Horton) were commonplace. I do not remember any of the teachers in my high school in Glen Ellyn, Illinois so much as mentioning the Rodney King beating when it happened.
