Court upholds N.Y. Law mandating platforms post terms of service and report how content moderation…

Various · Reason ·Aug 26, 2026 ·10 min read
Commentary by Hex Index staff
Original article →

In a legal landscape often dominated by abstract First Amendment absolutism, a federal judge in New York has delivered a pragmatic verdict that treats social media platforms not as sovereign speakers, but as commercial entities with a duty to inform. The ruling in X Corp. v. James suggests that when a company sells a service, the government can compel it to disclose the fine print of how that service operates, without violating free speech rights. This decision offers a rare, concrete application of consumer protection logic to the opaque world of algorithmic moderation.

The Commercial Nature of Moderation

The core of the piece's analysis rests on a distinction that often gets lost in the culture wars: the difference between a platform's opinions and its business terms. Reason reports that Judge John Cronan of the Southern District of New York found that "businesses might not always want to share every detail about the products or services they offer. But when the government requires those businesses to speak truthfully about their offerings, the First Amendment typically allows for that kind of disclosure requirement." This framing is crucial because it sidesteps the endless debate over whether a platform is a public square or a private club, focusing instead on the transaction between user and provider.

The court applied a specific legal standard known as Zauderer scrutiny, a doctrine rooted in a 1985 Supreme Court case involving attorney advertising. Just as a lawyer cannot hide their fee structure, and a fast-food chain must list calorie counts, the court argues that platforms must reveal their content moderation rules. The piece notes that under this standard, "mandated disclosure of accurate, factual, commercial information does not offend the core First Amendment values of promoting efficient exchange of information or protecting individual liberty interests." This is a powerful, if dry, argument that reframes transparency not as censorship, but as market efficiency. It echoes the logic of Zauderer v. Office of Disciplinary Counsel, where the Supreme Court held that compelled disclosures furthers the "discovery of truth" in the marketplace.

Critics might argue that content moderation is inherently political and that forcing a company to define "hate speech" or "misinformation" on a government form is an unconstitutional entanglement. However, the piece highlights the judge's rejection of this fear, noting that the law is "truly agnostic about the substance of the content moderation policy." The state isn't dictating the rules; it is simply demanding that the rules, whatever they are, be visible.

"More speech—like sunlight—is the best of disinfectants."

The Burden of Opacity

The New York law in question requires platforms to post their terms of service in a user-friendly manner and submit semiannual reports to the Attorney General detailing exactly how they enforce those rules. These reports must include data on flagged content, actions taken, and appeals processed. Reason argues that X Corp.'s attempt to block this was a strategic error, as the company "wants to share less of its policies than New York requires, so it invokes the First Amendment to avoid New York's requirement." The court saw through this, ruling that the First Amendment "poses no obstacle to New York's law."

The piece emphasizes that the law does not force platforms to moderate anything they don't want to. "X Corp. can adopt whatever policies [it] choose[s], including none at all; under the Content Category Report Provisions, X Corp. just ha[s] to disclose any policies it adopts." This distinction is vital. It separates the act of governing speech from the act of reporting on the governance of speech. By requiring data on how many posts were removed for "foreign political interference" or "extremism," the law aims to prevent the "non-disclosure of information material to the consumer." In an era where users are often unaware of why their content is suppressed, this transparency is a consumer right, not a speech restriction.

The court also addressed the concern that this might violate Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act, which protects platforms from liability for user content. The ruling found no conflict, because the law "doesn't actually forbid or mandate any content moderation." It merely mandates the reporting of what is already happening. This aligns with the broader legal principle that commercial disclosures are "reasonably related to the State's interest in preventing deception of consumers." The argument holds that just as a calorie count on a burger doesn't change the burger, a report on moderation policies doesn't change the speech on the platform.

Bottom Line

The strongest part of this coverage is its refusal to treat social media platforms as special cases immune to standard commercial regulation. By anchoring the argument in Zauderer scrutiny, the piece demonstrates that the First Amendment is not a shield for corporate secrecy. The biggest vulnerability of the ruling, however, remains the practical burden on smaller platforms and the potential for the reporting requirements to become a tool for political harassment if the data is misused. Readers should watch to see if this decision inspires similar legislation in other states or if it faces an appeal that challenges the definition of "commercial speech" in the digital age.

Businesses might not always want to share every detail about the products or services they offer. But when the government requires those businesses to speak truthfully about their offerings, the First Amendment typically allows for that kind of disclosure requirement. "Such disclosure furthers, rather than hinders, the First Amendment goal of the discovery of truth and contributes to the efficiency of the 'marketplace of ideas.'"

That principle resolves this case. The State of New York requires social-media companies to disclose certain aspects of their content-moderation policies. X Corp. wants to share less of its policies than New York requires, so it invokes the First Amendment to avoid New York's requirement. But the First Amendment poses no obstacle to New York's law. Here, more speech—like sunlight—is "the best of disinfectants." …

The [challenged] Act … it requires a "social media company" to "post terms of service for each social media platform owned or operated by the company in a manner reasonably designed to inform all users of the social media platform of the existence and contents of the terms of service." X Corp. refers to this requirement as the "Terms of Service Requirement." Under the Terms of Service Requirement, the "terms of service" publicly posted must "include … contact information for the purpose of allowing users to ask the social media company questions about the terms of service; a description of the process that users must follow to flag content, groups, or other users that they believe violate the terms of service, and the social media company's commitments on response and resolution time; and a list of potential actions the social media company may take against an item of content or a user." …

[T]he Act [also] requires social-media companies to semiannually "submit" to New York's "attorney general a terms of service report," which X Corp. refers to as the "Terms of Service Report." The Terms of Service Report, which the "attorney general shall make … available to the public in a searchable repository on its official internet website," must include several items, such as a platform's "current version of the terms of service" and "a complete and detailed description of any changes to the terms of service since the previous report."
