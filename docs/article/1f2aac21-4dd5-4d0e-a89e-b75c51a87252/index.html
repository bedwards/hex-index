Why China’s AI bubble is also industrial policy

Jordan Schneider · ChinaTalk ·Aug 18, 2026 ·21 min read
Commentary by Hex Index staff
Deep dives· Original article →

Jordan Schneider reframes the narrative of China's AI "bubble" not as a market failure, but as the inevitable friction of a massive, state-directed industrial policy. While most observers see 80,000 dissolved companies as a sign of a bursting speculative mania, Schneider argues this is actually the sound of the state forcing capital into hard technology sectors, regardless of short-term profitability. This distinction is vital for anyone trying to understand the true trajectory of the global AI race, as it suggests the "bubble" is a feature, not a bug, of Beijing's strategy.

The Mechanics of a Policy-Driven Bubble

Schneider begins by invoking a Chinese idiom, "beat the drum, pass the flower," to describe the current frenzy. He writes, "Each investor races to pass along overvalued AI companies before the 'beat' of the capital-raising cycle stops." This vivid metaphor captures the panic of a market where risk is being offloaded faster than value is being created. However, Schneider quickly pivots to the core thesis: "While they may be right that these are indicators of a market about to correct itself, it is more likely that they are signs that Chinese industrial policy is at work."

The author meticulously traces the shift in funding sources. Before 2018, Western venture capital drove the sector, but geopolitical tensions and US export controls have severed those ties. As Schneider notes, "Dollar-denominated funds, which accounted for nearly 14% of China VC fundraising in 2021, accounted for barely 1% by early 2025." In this vacuum, the state stepped in with unprecedented force. The scale is staggering: "GGFs have amassed 7.7 trillion yuan ($1.1 trillion) in committed capital... enough to fund DARPA at its current budget for two centuries."

This massive influx of state money is the engine behind the bubble dynamics. Schneider explains that the government uses these funds to build priority industries, just as it did with solar panels and electric vehicles. The result is a market where "funding rushes in faster than the industry can absorb it." Critics might argue that this ignores the fundamental inefficiencies of state-directed investment, but Schneider's data on the sheer volume of capital suggests that the market is being artificially sustained to achieve strategic goals, not market returns.

The result is a capital chain that is 'patient' at the national level and impatient at the layers actually making investment decisions.

The Paradox of Impatient Patience

The most insightful part of Schneider's analysis is his deconstruction of "patient capital." Theoretically, these government funds are designed to support long-term research in semiconductors and AI. In practice, however, the structure of the funding creates a paradox. Schneider writes, "Local officials, among the most active stakeholders in the process, operate on cadre rotation cycles... They therefore optimize their funding decisions to meet their promotion targets within that time frame."

Because officials rotate every three to five years, they prioritize projects that show immediate, measurable results rather than long-term breakthroughs. This pressure is compounded by private venture capital firms that are forced to enforce "redemption clauses," requiring companies to buy back stakes if they miss IPO deadlines. Schneider points out that "roughly two-thirds of the time, that liability extends to the founder personally." This creates a toxic environment where founders are forced to chase short-term exits rather than build sustainable businesses.

The system is designed to be "patient" at the top, but the capital becomes "impatient" as it flows down the chain. Schneider argues, "By the time capital finally reaches companies, the 20-year horizon at the top has often compressed into a demand for returns within just three to five." This structural contradiction explains why the market looks like a bubble: it is a speculative frenzy driven by the need to satisfy the short-term metrics of local officials and private fund managers, even as the national strategy aims for decades-long dominance.

Institutionalized Herding and Geographic Convergence

The final piece of Schneider's puzzle is the phenomenon of "institutionalized herding." When Beijing signals a priority sector, the entire system realigns, leading to a dangerous concentration of capital. Schneider observes, "Regional governments have historically specialized in distinct industrial sectors... AI investments nominally reflect this specialization, but capital is increasingly pushing localities into the same subsectors."

He cites the "Hundred Model War" as a prime example, where over 130 large language models were launched in China by mid-2023, many by established tech giants. This is not organic market differentiation; it is a coordinated rush to capture state funding. Schneider notes that "the 2025 national venture capital guidance fund is meant to mobilize a trillion yuan ($140 billion) in regional and private capital into state-designated priority sectors." The danger here is that this concentration leads to massive duplication of effort and wasted resources, creating a "bubble" of redundant companies that will eventually fail.

Yet, Schneider suggests this is the intended outcome of the policy. The state is willing to tolerate a high failure rate if it ensures that the few survivors become global leaders. As he puts it, "The dynamics that look like a financial bubble at a surface level are the same mechanisms that allow Beijing to achieve its industrial policy goals." This is a chillingly pragmatic view of the AI race, where the cost is measured in billions of wasted dollars and thousands of failed startups, but the prize is technological sovereignty.

Bottom Line

Schneider's argument is a powerful corrective to the simplistic narrative of a Chinese AI bubble about to burst. The strongest part of his analysis is the detailed breakdown of how the funding structure itself creates the very instability it seeks to solve, turning "patient capital" into a high-pressure, short-term gamble. The biggest vulnerability is the assumption that the state can successfully pick winners amidst such chaotic incentives; history suggests that industrial policy often fails to identify the right technologies until it is too late. Readers should watch for how the Chinese government attempts to reform these redemption clauses and cadre incentives, as that will determine whether the "bubble" corrects or hardens into a new, state-controlled reality.

Why China’s AI bubble is also industrial policy

by Jordan Schneider · ChinaTalk · Read full article

Leia Wang is a is a nonresident scholar in the Technology and International Affairs Program at the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, as well as an associate at the Centre for the Governance of AI (GovAI).

击鼓传花 (ji gu chuan hua), roughly translated as “beat the drum, pass the flower,” is a Chinese children’s game in which players pass a flower around while a drum sounds. When the drum stops, whoever is left holding the flower loses the round.

The phrase has recently acquired a secondary use in Chinese financial and policy commentary as a shorthand for a speculative chain in which risk is passed between participants. From anonymous investor forums to listed-company risk disclosures, it has been applied to the 2015 A-share bubble, property speculation, local government debt rollovers, P2P lending, and, most recently, the venture capital (VC) market. As one Chinese venture capitalist explained to me,1 the game is China’s AI market, and investors are the players. Each investor races to pass along overvalued AI companies before the “beat” of the capital-raising cycle stops.

Many domestic financial actors caution that this dynamic indicates China’s early-stage AI market is becoming a financial bubble. Valuations have inflated faster than revenues, capital is concentrated in a handful of hot subsectors, and money keeps flowing toward companies with no clear path to profitability. They cite the roughly 80,000 Chinese AI companies that have dissolved or deregistered in the past two years.

While they may be right that these are indicators of a market about to correct itself, it is more likely that they are signs that Chinese industrial policy is at work. Since 2017, when AI was designated a strategic technology, Beijing has taken an active role in promoting the industry’s growth. Over the past eighteen months, the state has prioritized funding early-stage companies, launching dedicated national AI funds and committing hundreds of billions of yuan toward AI ventures. This influx of concentrated capital has contributed to bubble-like dynamics as funding rushes in faster than the industry can absorb it.

Yet the strategy of financing new ventures in strategic sectors is one Beijing has used before. From solar panels to electric vehicles (EVs), the government used state capital to build other priority industries. Whether AI follows a similar trajectory depends on how the funding is deployed, including the mechanisms and stakeholders involved.
