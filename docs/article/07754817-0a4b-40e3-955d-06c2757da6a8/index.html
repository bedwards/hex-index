When she becomes esther

In a landscape saturated with partisan shouting, this piece offers a rare, chilling diagnosis of how biblical storytelling can be weaponized to turn neighbors into existential threats. Elizabeth Neumann, a former counterterrorism official, does not merely critique political strategy; she exposes the psychological architecture that transforms civic disagreement into a sacred mandate for hostility. This is essential listening for anyone trying to understand why the American right has mobilized so effectively, and more importantly, why that mobilization is drifting toward dangerous extremism.

The Anatomy of a Dangerous Story

The article's central insight is that the Christian right is not a monolith, but a collection of distinct groups united by a shared emotional wound. Neumann draws heavily on Katie Gaddini's ethnographic research in Esther's Army, noting that while the women profiled range from "College Idealists" to "Mama Bears," they are all driven by "recurring experiences of humiliation, uncertainty, fear, and loss of control." This framing is crucial because it shifts the conversation from ideology to psychology. It suggests that the drive for power is a symptom of a deeper need for belonging and protection.

Neumann argues that the movement succeeds by offering a narrative that heals these wounds, but at a terrible cost. She writes, "The critical question is what kind of story organizes those experiences. Who is cast as the hero? Who becomes the villain? And what actions begin to appear necessary when an ordinary political conflict is transformed into a sacred struggle for survival?" This is a profound observation. It moves the debate beyond policy disputes to the very stories people tell themselves to make sense of their lives. By identifying the emotional hooks, Neumann explains why logic often fails to persuade these voters.

"When we falsely cast political opponents as evil, existential threats, we move dangerously close to extremism."

The author's background in national security lends unique weight to her warning. She clarifies that she is not accusing these women of imminent violence, but rather identifying the "fertile ground for extremist beliefs." She defines extremism not just as violence, but as "the belief that an in-group's success or survival can never be separated from the need for hostile action against an out-group." This definition is vital. It captures the spectrum of harm, from bullying to genocide, and shows how the former can logically lead to the latter if the narrative is left unchecked. Critics might argue that this definition is too broad and risks conflating passionate advocacy with genuine extremism, but Neumann's distinction between holding polarizing views and believing hostile action is necessary is a necessary nuance.

The Theology of the Haman

The piece takes a sharp turn when it analyzes the specific biblical metaphor driving this movement: the story of Esther. Neumann points out that while the story of Esther is a common tool in evangelical teaching, its application here is theologically and politically perilous. In the biblical text, Esther saves her people from genocide. In the modern political context, Neumann argues, "There is no evidence of a coordinated effort to annihilate Christians as a people." Yet, by casting themselves as Esther, these women must inevitably cast their opponents as Haman, the genocidal villain.

This reframing is where the argument becomes most urgent. Neumann writes, "The analogy does more than encourage women to act courageously. It transforms fellow citizens, co-workers, and neighbors with different political views into the equivalent of a genocidal enemy." This is a devastating critique of how religious language can dehumanize political opponents. It explains why the rhetoric has become so vitriolic; if your neighbor is Haman, then any action taken against them is not just political, but righteous.

"When we mistake a fellow human being, made in the image of God, for one of 'the powers of this dark world,' and spiritualize our political quest as a fight for God, we move dangerously close not only to extremism, but to religious extremism, which has the grim distinction of being among its bloodiest forms."

The author connects this to the broader ecosystem of the Christian right, noting that this dynamic is not unique to this moment but echoes historical patterns. Just as the "Mama Grizzly" phenomenon of 2010 mobilized women around perceived threats to their children, the current "Esther's Army" mobilizes women around a perceived threat to their very existence. The parallel is striking: in both cases, the narrative of victimhood is used to justify aggressive action. Neumann suggests that this is not a bug in the system, but a feature of how the movement maintains cohesion.

Beyond Winning: A Call for Integrity

The final section of the piece pivots from diagnosis to prescription. Neumann challenges the idea that political success is the ultimate goal for Christians. She argues, "When winning becomes paramount, it becomes tempting to tolerate leaders on our side who speak in half-truths, dehumanize opponents, disregard the rule of law, or treat cruelty as strength." This is a direct challenge to the "ends justify the means" mentality that has permeated much of modern political discourse.

She asks a series of difficult questions: "What kind of people are we becoming? Does our engagement bear the fruit of the Spirit?" These questions cut through the noise of policy debates to address the moral cost of political participation. Neumann suggests that the solution is not retreat, but a return to a "distinctly Christian way of inhabiting public life." She writes, "Faithful political engagement cannot be measured only by whether we prevail, or even by whether our cause is just. It must also be measured by how we pursue that cause."

"Christians are not called to fight fire with fire. We are called to do justice, love mercy, walk humbly, speak truth, love our enemies, and overcome evil with good."

This call for integrity is perhaps the most radical part of the argument. In an era where political loyalty often demands silence in the face of wrongdoing, Neumann urges believers to "refuse to excuse wrongdoing within their own coalition." This is a difficult path, one that requires courage and discernment. It is a call to be peacemakers, not just warriors. The author acknowledges that this is a long-term project, requiring "patient ministry within the church: sound teaching, loving correction, deeper discipleship, and communities that form women to participate with courage and conviction without being ruled by fear, grievance, or the pursuit of power."

Bottom Line

Neumann's argument is strongest in its ability to connect the dots between personal trauma, theological distortion, and political extremism, offering a roadmap for understanding rather than just condemnation. Its biggest vulnerability lies in the difficulty of its prescription; asking a movement driven by fear and grievance to embrace humility and truth is a tall order in the current political climate. Readers should watch for how this call for integrity resonates within the very communities Neumann describes, and whether a new generation of Christian women can be formed by a story that prioritizes love over power.

Since writing Kingdom of Rage, I have been wrestling with what it looks like in practice to “seek the peace and prosperity of the city” (Jer. 29:7, NIV). It is how I closed the book, challenging readers to be peacemakers and love their neighbors in the public square.

Christians already bless their communities in countless ways: tutoring youth, fostering and adopting children, serving people experiencing homelessness, and caring for the sick. But I have been pondering a more specific question: Is there a way to engage faithfully in civic and political life without succumbing to the temptations of power and control? Can we participate without being motivated by fear, but instead by genuine love for our neighbors, including those we regard as the “other”? And if there is such a way, who are the women who might respond to that call?

Gaddini, like me, grew up in conservative Christian culture.
