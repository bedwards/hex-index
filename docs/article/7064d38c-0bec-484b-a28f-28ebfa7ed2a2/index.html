Reading: The duc de Saint-Simon on the battle of blenheim

Commentary by Hex Index staff
Deep dives· Original article →

Brad DeLong does not merely recount a 1704 battle; he dissects how a single afternoon of catastrophic mismanagement shattered a century of French ambition and reshaped the global balance of power. By channeling the visceral, confused memoirs of the Duc de Saint-Simon, DeLong reveals that history's turning points are often not the result of grand strategy, but of "blindness without example" in the field. This is a rare lens that forces the modern reader to confront the fragility of hegemony and the human cost of administrative failure.

The Anatomy of a Blunder

DeLong anchors his analysis in the primary source voice of Louis de Rouvroy, the Duc de Saint-Simon, whose memoirs capture the sheer bewilderment of the French court. The core of the argument is that the Franco-Bavarian defeat was not inevitable; it was manufactured by a series of avoidable tactical errors. DeLong highlights the absurdity of the French deployment, noting that the commander, Marshal Tallard, made a fateful choice to station a massive force inside a single village. "At his extreme right was the large village of Blenheim, in which, by a blindness without example, he had placed twenty-six battalions of infantry, six regiments of dragoons, and a brigade of cavalry," DeLong writes. This decision effectively removed a third of his army from the main engagement, creating a fatal gap that the Allies exploited.

The commentary effectively uses Saint-Simon's narrative to illustrate the paralysis of command. The French generals, despite having the day to prepare, chose a position that left their lines separated by a quagmire and their flanks exposed. DeLong points out the tragic irony that the French had fought a similar battle at Hochstedt just a year prior, yet failed to learn the lesson: "The first battle of Hochstedt afforded a lesson which ought to have been studied on this occasion. There were many officers present, too, who had been at that battle; but they were not consulted." This oversight underscores a recurring theme in the War of the Spanish Succession: the rigidity of the French command structure, which would later plague them in the War of the Quadruple Alliance.

"It was a strange situation for a general to take up, who is master of a vast plain; and it became, as will be seen, a very sad one."

The human dimension of this strategic failure is brought into sharp relief through the chaos of the retreat. DeLong does not shy away from the panic that ensued, describing how the French cavalry, unable to rally due to the terrain, was swept aside. The narrative details the capture of Marshal Tallard himself, who rode out to countermand orders only to be surrounded and taken prisoner. The scene of the trapped troops in Blenheim is particularly harrowing; cut off and leaderless, they were forced to surrender en masse. DeLong notes that the decision to capitulate was made after a British officer demonstrated the total collapse of the French lines, leaving Blansac, the local commander, with no choice but to save his men from annihilation. "The number of prisoners that fell to the enemy in this battle was infinite," DeLong observes, emphasizing the scale of the loss.

The Shock of Defeat

The aftermath of the battle, as described by DeLong, offers a profound look at the psychological impact of sudden military collapse on a superpower. The French court, accustomed to victory, was plunged into a state of denial and confusion. For six days, King Louis XIV remained in the dark, as officials feared to deliver the full truth. "The King received the cruel news of this battle on the 21st of August... For six days the King remained in this uncertainty as to the real losses that had been sustained," DeLong writes. This delay highlights the disconnect between the reality on the ground and the perception in the capital, a dynamic that often exacerbates the trauma of defeat.

When the truth finally emerged, the reaction was one of universal grief and indignation. DeLong captures the depth of the national humiliation, noting that "scarcely an illustrious family that had not had one of their members killed, wounded, or taken prisoner." The public outcry was so severe that it led to a scapegoating of lower-ranking officers and regiments, while the high command was largely let off the hook. "All the punishment fell upon certain regiments, which were broken, and upon certain unimportant officers—the guilty and innocent mixed together," DeLong explains. This selective justice reflects the political necessity of preserving the prestige of the monarchy even in the face of undeniable failure.

Critics might note that DeLong's focus on the tactical blunders of the French command slightly underplays the strategic brilliance of the Allied coalition, particularly the coordination between Marlborough and Prince Eugene. While the French errors were glaring, the ability of the Allies to execute a complex crossing and simultaneous attack against a prepared position was itself a remarkable feat of logistics and leadership. However, DeLong's argument remains compelling because it centers the narrative on the human element of command failure rather than abstract military theory.

The Long Shadow of Blenheim

Ultimately, DeLong uses the battle to illustrate the contingent nature of history. The piece suggests that the rise of British global hegemony and the containment of French power were not preordained but hinged on the events of a single day. "In the end, the politico-military story from 1624 to 1815 is that France did not become the hegemonic power inside Europe, while Britain did become the hegemonic power across the world's oceans," DeLong writes. He posits that the battle of Blenheim was the "butterfly-wingflap hinge" of this historical shift. The loss forced France into a defensive posture from which it never fully recovered, altering the trajectory of European politics for over a century.

The commentary concludes by reflecting on the enduring legacy of the defeat. The "terrible capitulation" at Blenheim was not just a military loss but a blow to the national psyche that resonated through the generations. DeLong's use of Saint-Simon's memoirs allows the reader to feel the weight of that moment, where the confidence of the Sun King's France was irrevocably shattered. "We were not accustomed to misfortunes. This one, very reasonably, was utterly unexpected," the text recalls, capturing the disorientation of a nation that believed itself invincible.

"It seemed in every way the result of bad generalship, of an unjustifiable disposition of troops, and of a series of gross and incredible errors."

Bottom Line

Brad DeLong's piece is a masterful exercise in historical storytelling, using the raw, emotional testimony of the Duc de Saint-Simon to reveal how a single day of poor decisions can alter the course of civilization. Its greatest strength lies in its refusal to romanticize war, instead focusing on the confusion, fear, and human cost of a catastrophic defeat. The argument's only vulnerability is its heavy reliance on a single French perspective, which, while vivid, may overlook the broader strategic context that also contributed to the Allied victory. Readers should watch for how this analysis of command failure echoes in modern geopolitical shifts, where the margin between hegemony and decline remains as thin as it was in 1704.

But both of these were close-run things. And perhaps the butterfly-wingflap hinge of this aspect of history was the 1704 battle of Blenheim.

In his memoirs, Louis de Rouvroy, Duc de Saint‑Simon, reports from Paris:

Louis de Rouvroy (1830): Memoirs of the Duc de Saint-Simon <>: ‘I have already said that a grand alliance, with the Emperor at its head, had been formed against France, and that our troops were opposing the Allies in various parts of Europe. The Elector of Bavaria had joined his forces to ours, and had already done us some service. On the 12th of August he led his men into the plain of Hochstedt, where, during the previous year, he had gained a victory over the Imperialists. In this plain he was joined by our troops, who took up positions right and left of him, under the command of Tallard and Marsin. The Elector himself had command of all.

Soon after their arrival at Hochstedt, they received intelligence that Prince Eugene, with the Imperialist forces, and the Duke of Marlborough with the English were coming to meet them. Our generals had, however, all the day before them to choose their ground, and to make their dispositions. It would have been difficult to succeed worse, both with the one and the other. A brook, by no means of a miry kind, ran parallel to our army; and in front of it a spring, which formed a long and large quagmire, nearly separated the two lines of Marshal Tallard. It was a strange situation for a general to take up, who is master of a vast plain; and it became, as will be seen, a very sad one.
