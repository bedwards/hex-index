143: Why 4 in 10 new moms end up in the er with issues that could have been treated in primary care

Various · Two Truths ·Jul 26, 2026 ·10 min read
Commentary by Hex Index staff
Deep dives· Original article →

This piece cuts through the noise of maternal health statistics to expose a terrifyingly simple failure: the healthcare system is designed to drop new mothers the moment the baby is born. Two Truths reports a study finding that "about 4 in 10 new mothers ended up in the ER or urgent care in the year after delivery for issues that could have been treated in primary care," a staggering figure that suggests our current model of care is not just inefficient, but dangerous. The article's most distinctive claim is that this isn't a failure of patient compliance, but a structural collapse in the transition from obstetrics to general medicine, a gap that kills hundreds of women annually.

The Architecture of the Cliff

The editors define the problem with surgical precision, describing the "postpartum cliff" as "the timeframe after the immediate postpartum period, when there's not always an easy transition between your OB/GYN and your primary care, and many women miss out on medical care." This framing is crucial because it shifts the blame from exhausted new parents to the system itself. Historically, the industry relied on a "lone follow-up appointment six to eight weeks after delivery," a relic of an era when maternal mortality was accepted as an unfortunate risk rather than a preventable tragedy. The piece notes that while the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists now recommends ongoing care, "guidance and reality are two different things."

The human cost of this disconnect is laid bare with chilling clarity. Two Truths highlights that "about half of those deaths happen more than a week after delivery, which is exactly when care can fall away." This timing is not coincidental; it is the result of a system that treats pregnancy as a temporary condition with a hard stop date, ignoring the long-term physiological realities of childbirth. The article correctly identifies that the leading cause of pregnancy-related death is mental health conditions, yet the infrastructure to treat them vanishes once the six-week checkup is over.

The leading cause of pregnancy-related death in this country: mental health conditions, which some one in five to seven new moms experience—all of which are treatable.

Critics might argue that the sheer volume of postpartum complications is simply a biological reality that medicine cannot fully mitigate. However, the piece counters this by noting that "upward of 84%" of these deaths are preventable, suggesting that the barrier is not medical science, but logistical access.

The Gray Area of Responsibility

The commentary then pivots to the institutional confusion that fuels this crisis. Dr. Mark Clapp, a maternal-fetal medicine specialist quoted in the piece, identifies the core ambiguity: "Who is responsible for the patient's health after the first six to 12 weeks postpartum?" This question exposes a critical flaw in modern healthcare: the lack of a formal handoff. Two Truths reports that "often there's no formal 'handoff,' things are just communicated through electronic notes or record transfers, but information can get lost."

This fragmentation is particularly acute for common but complex issues like mastitis or lingering hypertension. The article points out that "breastfeeding, in particular, can be a tricky issue in medicine to begin with, as many physicians lack training in it." When primary care doctors are untrained in lactation support and obstetricians are focused on reproductive health rather than general wellness, new mothers fall into the cracks. The piece argues that without support structures like paid leave or affordable childcare, "medical appointments for yourself are hard to make happen," turning the ER into the only viable option for urgent care.

The Power of Proactive Intervention

Perhaps the most compelling section of the coverage is the evidence that a simple, low-tech solution can dramatically alter outcomes. The JAMA Network Open study cited by Two Truths found that when researchers "called and scheduled primary care appointments for people, with their consent, and sent text message reminders about appointments," the results were transformative. The piece emphasizes that "researchers took the onus off moms," and as a result, unnecessary ER visits dropped significantly.

This finding challenges the prevailing narrative that patients are simply "hard to reach." Instead, it suggests that the system is too passive. Dr. Clapp notes that interventions like this "more than doubled the number of people who got back to their primary care provider in that first year postpartum." The argument here is that trust is the missing variable; as the piece observes, patients report "healthier behaviors, fewer symptoms, a higher quality of life, and greater satisfaction with treatment when they had more trust in their provider." Seeing a doctor you actually know, rather than a stranger in an emergency room, changes the trajectory of care.

Reconnecting moms directly to care, then, works—and it isn't nearly enough.

Bottom Line

The strongest part of this argument is its refusal to treat the "postpartum cliff" as an individual failure, instead pinpointing the systemic lack of a formal handoff between obstetric and primary care. However, the piece's biggest vulnerability is its reliance on small-scale interventions; while scheduling nudges work, they cannot fix the fundamental lack of paid leave or the shrinking supply of primary care physicians. The reader should watch for how policymakers respond to the call for structural changes, as the data proves that the current model is actively costing lives.

The bottom line: Reconnecting moms directly to care, then, works—and it isn't nearly enough. That's the part that requires bigger fixes: paid leave so that we can have time to rest and adjust to everything that comes with a new phase of life, accessible childcare so that we can actually get to our own medical appointments (maybe alone), real investments in maternal health and women's health, and a commitment to studying it, understanding it, and treating it seriously.

As a health reporter, a lot of new studies come across my inbox. Recently, one that plainly stated the hurdles so many new moms face in finding care for themselves caught my attention.

The study was small, 353 people, but it examined what researchers called a “postpartum cliff,” painting a real-world picture of the barriers to care so many new mothers face. Published in JAMA Network Open this past May, the study found that about 4 in 10 new mothers ended up in the ER or urgent care in the year after delivery for issues that could have been treated in primary care (things like headaches, urinary tract infections, mastitis, and more). The moms in the study all already had a primary care doctor, and they had ongoing health concerns such as depression or high blood pressure.

To understand a little bit more about the “postpartum cliff”—why it matters, what needs to change so that we can get the care we need, and when and where we need it—Two Truths recently spoke with Mark Clapp, MD, MPH, one of the study’s authors. Dr. Clapp is a maternal-fetal medicine specialist at Mass General Hospital in Boston and an assistant professor at Harvard Medical School. He’s worked on—and is currently working on—a whole bunch of interesting, important studies related to plugging moms back into the healthcare system after birth. Here’s some of what he told us.
