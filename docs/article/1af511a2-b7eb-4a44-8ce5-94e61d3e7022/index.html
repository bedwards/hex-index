Ukraine's Southern offensive, what's next for Putin & what if mobilisation fails to help Russia in…

Commentary by Hex Index staff
Deep dives· Original article →

Mick Ryan delivers a sobering assessment of a conflict that has settled into a brutal, high-stakes attrition, arguing that the war in Ukraine is no longer just about holding lines but about fundamentally altering the strategic calculus of a regime that refuses to accept defeat. What makes this analysis urgent is its rejection of the idea that recent Ukrainian gains are merely tactical; Ryan frames them as a deliberate, evolving test of new combined arms doctrines that could redefine modern warfare for decades. While the world watches the headlines, Ryan forces us to confront the grim reality that without a shift in momentum, the human cost will only deepen, and the window for a diplomatic resolution is rapidly closing.

The Southern Offensive and the Evolution of Tactics

Ryan begins by dismantling the notion that the war has stagnated, pointing to hard data from the southern front where Ukrainian forces liberated 745 square kilometers and 26 settlements between January and August 2026. He writes, "The southern offensive appears to have had a dual focus of liberating territory and causing as much destruction to Russian ground forces as possible." This is not a simple push for land; it is a calculated campaign to bleed the enemy dry. Ryan argues that this success was not accidental but the result of a sophisticated "operational interdiction campaign" that destroyed Russian logistics, effectively strangling the front lines before the ground troops even advanced.

The author highlights how Ukraine has moved beyond defensive posturing to embrace aggressive innovation. "The Ukrainians have demonstrated throughout this war the desire and the willingness to trial evolved forms of combined arms tactics that exploit aerial and ground drone technology to dominate the tactical reconnaissance battle," Ryan observes. This shift is critical. It suggests that the battlefield is no longer dominated by massed artillery alone but by a networked system where drones, electronic warfare, and traditional infantry work in lockstep. The human cost of this evolution is staggering; Ryan notes that Russian casualties in July alone approached 43,000, a figure that underscores the ferocity of these new tactics.

The Ukrainians continue to give us insights into modern war, and the trajectory of human conflict. We need to do better at learning from them, while also supporting them at the same time.

Critics might argue that focusing on tactical innovation ignores the sheer material disparity between the two nations, suggesting that such gains are unsustainable without massive Western aid. However, Ryan's framing suggests that the very act of sustaining these operations is a strategic victory in itself, forcing the adversary to expend resources faster than they can be replaced. The context of the FP-5 Flamingo strike on the Progress Rocket and Space Centre in Samara further illustrates this point; by targeting the only serial production facility for Soyuz-2 launch vehicles, Ukraine is striking at the heart of Russia's future space and satellite ambitions, not just its current battlefield capabilities.

The Kremlin's Dilemma and the Mobilization Gamble

As the southern offensive strains Russian defenses, the Kremlin faces a desperate choice. Ryan points to recent command shake-ups in the Russian Unmanned Systems Forces and logistics as a sign of internal panic. The administration is reportedly considering a massive mobilization of up to 500,000 troops, a move that would take months to yield results but could precipitate a grim winter for Ukraine. Ryan asks the pivotal question: "What if Putin's Mobilisation Fails?" He argues that even with a surge of new troops, the Russian goal of capturing the Donbas is becoming increasingly unlikely given the effectiveness of Ukrainian defenses.

The author outlines three potential paths for the Russian leadership if this mobilization fails to turn the tide. The first is a ceasefire, which Ryan dismisses as politically impossible for a leader who has already suffered over 1.5 million casualties. The second is escalation, including the potential use of chemical or nuclear weapons. Ryan is clear on the risks here: "His nuclear sabre rattling in the past has not shifted Ukrainian determination to hold on to their territory." Yet, he warns that if intelligence confirms orders for nuclear strikes, the dynamic with NATO and even China could shift dramatically. This is a terrifying prospect that centers the human suffering of millions, not just the geopolitical maneuvering.

Putin would not want that. So, this too is an unlikely pathway for Putin if his mobilization fails to secure the military objectives he wants to achieve.

The third option, which Ryan identifies as the most dangerous, is a strategy of "hold in Ukraine, strike elsewhere." He speculates that a desperate Russian leadership might launch a short, sharp campaign against a Baltic nation to test NATO's resolve. This would be a gamble designed to fracture the alliance, asking nations like Spain or the United States to risk nuclear war for a small nation in eastern Europe. While speculative, this scenario highlights the fragility of the current security architecture. It is a stark reminder that the war in Ukraine is a proxy for the stability of the entire European order.

Strategic Momentum and the Cost of Inaction

Ryan's analysis extends beyond the immediate battlefield to the broader strategic implications for the West. He notes that Ukraine's ability to inflict such high casualties is a double-edged sword; it keeps the pressure on Moscow but requires sustained Western support, particularly in air defense. "Ukraine has the strategic momentum but still requires our support, especially for air defence," he writes, emphasizing that momentum without resources is fleeting. The author also touches on the geopolitical ripple effects, noting that the war in the Middle East has diverted missile interceptors, allowing Russia to intensify its own strikes. This interconnectedness means that a conflict in one region can directly exacerbate suffering in another.

The piece also draws a parallel to the Han Kuang exercise in Taiwan, where the nation rehearses resilience against aggression. Ryan uses this to illustrate a broader lesson: modern defense is not just about military hardware but about the will of a society to endure. "The exercise is more than just a military activity. It is a whole of government and society exercise that rehearses national resilience and demonstrates national will." This comparison adds depth to the argument, suggesting that the outcome of the war in Ukraine will serve as a blueprint for how other nations prepare for potential aggression.

Regardless of Ukraine's future intentions... there remains much that Western military organisations might learn from what has occurred in southern Ukraine in recent months.

However, Ryan is critical of the West's response. He points to a "systemic learning deficit" in Western military institutions, arguing that they have been slow to adopt the lessons Ukraine is learning in real-time. This is a damning indictment of the very allies Ukraine relies on. If the West fails to adapt its force structures and tactics to match the reality of drone warfare and electronic combat, it risks being left behind in future conflicts. The acquisition of Chinese drone technology by Ukraine, as mentioned in the source, further complicates the picture, showing how adversaries can appropriate and adapt technology from unexpected sources.

Bottom Line

Mick Ryan's argument is strongest in its refusal to view the war through the lens of static front lines, instead presenting a dynamic, evolving conflict where tactical innovation is driving strategic shifts. His willingness to confront the grim possibility of a Russian strike on the Baltics serves as a necessary wake-up call for NATO, forcing a reckoning with the limits of deterrence. The piece's greatest vulnerability lies in its reliance on speculative scenarios regarding Russian mobilization and nuclear escalation, which, while plausible, remain unproven. Ultimately, the reader must watch for the next phase of the southern offensive and the West's ability to close the learning gap before the window for decisive action closes forever.
