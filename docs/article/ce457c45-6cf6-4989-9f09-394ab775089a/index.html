Ms. Rachel says black boys deserve to grow up

Kahlil Greene · History Can't Hide ·Aug 11, 2026 ·10 min read
Commentary by Hex Index staff
Original article →

Kahlil Greene confronts a modern silence by linking a 2026 tragedy on a Mississippi barrier island to a century of American indifference toward Black childhood. The piece is notable not for breaking the news of Nolan Wells' death, but for its unflinching refusal to let the investigation's ambiguity erase the pattern of violence that precedes it. Greene argues that the outrage directed at children's educator Rachel Accurso reveals a national discomfort with the idea that Black boys are not afforded the same presumption of innocence as their white peers.

The Lane of a Children's Educator

The article centers on the backlash against Rachel Accurso, known professionally as Ms. Rachel, after she posted, "Black boys deserve to grow up," following the death of Wells. Greene notes that the fury was not merely about a celebrity commenting on news, but about the specific claim that Black children face a unique mortality risk. "Accurso spent every episode telling small children that adults will keep them safe," Greene writes, observing that she simply extended that promise to Black children, a move that made many audiences feel accused. This framing is powerful because it strips away the abstraction of "public safety" to reveal the specific failure to protect Black youth.

The author highlights the swift, vitriolic response: critics demanded she "stay in her lane," while others issued death threats. Yet, Accurso refused to apologize, asserting that "arguing for the human rights of children should not count as controversial." Greene uses this moment to pivot from the individual controversy to the systemic issue. The core of the argument is that the demand for silence is a mechanism of erasure. By attacking the messenger, the public avoids confronting the message: that the promise of safety has never been universal.

Critics might argue that a children's entertainer lacks the expertise to weigh in on complex criminal investigations, a point Greene acknowledges but ultimately dismisses as a deflection from the human cost. The argument holds that the moral imperative to speak on the safety of children transcends professional boundaries.

A Century of Unpunished Killings

To ground the 2026 event, Greene weaves in a stark historical record, noting that the NAACP documented 4,743 lynchings between 1882 and 1968, with Mississippi recording the highest total of any state. The author draws a direct line from the fabricated accusations used to justify mob violence in the early 20th century to the current skepticism surrounding Wells' death. "White mobs often justified a lynching with a fabricated accusation of sexual contact with a white woman," Greene writes, adding that "many of the people they killed faced no accusation at all and died for speaking to white people without the deference white people demanded."

The piece connects this history to the 1955 murder of Emmett Till in Mississippi, where an all-white jury acquitted the killers who then confessed to the crime in a magazine interview. Greene emphasizes that the pattern of impunity remains intact. "In 2013, James Ware told The Birmingham News that the country remembered the four girls and forgot the two boys," referring to the two Black children killed in the aftermath of the 1963 Birmingham church bombing. This historical context is vital; it suggests that the current debate over Wells is not an anomaly but a continuation of a long-standing practice of rendering Black deaths invisible or less significant.

"I'm fighting to document stories like this before they get dismissed or erased entirely."

The Science of Adultification

Moving beyond history, Greene introduces psychological research to explain why the public reacts so defensively to the idea that Black boys are in danger. The author cites 2014 research by Phillip Goff showing that adults overestimate the ages of Black boys as young as 10 and judge them more responsible for crimes than white boys. Greene paraphrases the findings of Rebecca Epstein and colleagues at Georgetown Law, who termed this phenomenon "adultification," describing it as "a form of dehumanization that strips Black children of the innocence adults grant to other children."

This scientific evidence strengthens the article's claim that the controversy is rooted in a deep-seated cognitive bias. Greene points out that while the law has evolved to protect white children, it has largely left Black children behind. "The law professor Priscilla Ocen has traced how American courts extended new protections to white children as the modern idea of childhood developed, while leaving Black children roughly where they were," the author notes. The commentary here is sharp: the legal and social definitions of "childhood" are not neutral; they are racially coded.

Danger That Has Not Ended

The piece concludes by returning to the present, citing 2024 data that identifies homicide as the leading cause of death for Black Americans aged 10 to 24. Greene contrasts the specific case of Wells with broader trends, noting that the firearm homicide rate for Black males in this age group is more than twenty times that of white males. The author connects the dots between historical lynchings and modern high-profile deaths, quoting NAACP president Derrick Johnson, who called the killing of George Floyd "a public spectacle of the kind white mobs staged a century earlier."

Greene's final assessment is that the uncertainty surrounding Wells' death does not negate the reality of the threat. "No one yet knows how Nolan Wells died, and the grand jury may never produce an answer that satisfies his parents," Greene writes. However, the author insists that the point remains valid regardless of the autopsy results: "American juries and police departments have spent the last century making that outcome less likely for Black families, and the people who unfollowed her still have not offered any evidence against the point she made."

Bottom Line

Greene's most compelling contribution is the synthesis of a specific, unresolved tragedy with the long arc of American racial violence, proving that the demand for Black boys to "grow up" is a plea against a system designed to extinguish them prematurely. The piece's only vulnerability is its reliance on the reader's willingness to accept that historical patterns of impunity directly inform current investigative biases, a leap some may resist. However, the weight of the evidence presented—from the NAACP's lynching records to modern psychological studies—makes the argument difficult to dismiss. Readers should watch for the grand jury's decision in the Wells case, not just for the verdict, but for how the legal system's handling of it confirms or contradicts the historical precedents Greene so meticulously outlines.

Ms. Rachel says black boys deserve to grow up

by Kahlil Greene · History Can't Hide · Read full article

In July 2026, Rachel Accurso, the children’s educator known to millions of toddlers and parents as Ms. Rachel, posted on Instagram: “Black boys deserve to grow up.”

She wrote them after the death of Nolan Wells, an 18-year-old from Ocean Springs, Mississippi, who disappeared on July 4, 2026, during a gathering on Horn Island, a barrier island about ten miles off the Gulf Coast. Searchers found his body two days later. Jackson County investigators said early on that they found no signs of foul play and suspected drowning. An independent autopsy the Wells family commissioned returned an undetermined cause and manner of death on July 22, the state medical examiner has not issued a final ruling, and District Attorney Angel Myers McIlrath has said the evidence stays sealed until a grand jury reviews it. Ben Crump, the civil rights attorney representing the family, argues that local officials treated the case less seriously because Wells was Black and the friends he traveled with are white. Ms. Rachel repeated that argument in a second post.

Thousands of her followers turned on her. The anger has little to do with a children’s entertainer commenting on the news. She said that Black boys face a danger white boys do not, and Americans have spent more than a century refusing to say that plainly while the killings continued.
