Reviews of "house of the dragon" and "a knight of the seven kingdoms"

Commentary by Hex Index staff

In a landscape saturated with fantasy escapism, Reason delivers a stinging critique that refuses to let viewers off the hook for enjoying the violence of Westeros. The piece argues that these shows are not merely entertainment but a "highly entertaining!" reminder of why we need systemic constraints on power, warning against the modern nostalgia for pre-modern hierarchies. This is a rare take that treats dragons and swords as metaphors for the very real dangers of hereditary rule and unchecked aristocracy.

The Cost of Unchecked Power

The article dives deep into the "Dance of the Dragons," a civil war sparked by a succession crisis where the Targaryen dynasty's monopoly on dragons fuels a brutal conflict. Reason reports that the series forces viewers to confront a grim reality: "there are virtually no major sympathetic characters in the source material." The piece notes that leaders on both sides, from the "murderous megalomaniac" Prince Daemon to the incompetent Aegon II, care little for the welfare of the people. This framing is effective because it strips away the glamour of high fantasy to reveal the raw mechanics of power struggles that ignore human suffering.

The commentary highlights how the show attempts to humanize figures like Princess Rhaenyra, yet ultimately fails to make them truly redeemable. "The sympathy for Rhaenyra is hard to maintain given the many wrongs she herself perpetrates, and her indifference to the harm the war inflicts on the 'smallfolk'." This is a crucial observation. When Rhaenyra's forces take King's Landing, the narrative exposes the hollowness of her "reign of terror" and her confiscation of food stocks—actions that only exacerbate shortages caused by her own blockade. The piece rightly points out that this dynamic underscores a systemic failure: "the real problem plaguing Westeros is a system in which aristocratic elites wield power with little or no concern for the people."

There is no such hope in House of the Dragon. That, combined with the lack of sympathetic protagonists, makes the series somewhat dreary and depressing.

Critics might argue that the show's bleakness is a deliberate artistic choice to reflect the brutality of its source material, Fire and Blood. However, the article suggests this approach risks alienating audiences who need a moral compass in their storytelling. The piece contrasts this with the original Game of Thrones, where characters like Jon Snow or Daenerys offered a glimmer of hope to "break the wheel," a narrative thread that has been severed in the prequel.

The Illusion of Meritocracy

Shifting focus to A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms, set roughly 80 to 90 years after the civil war, the article finds a more hopeful tone but identifies a different kind of systemic rot. The story follows Dunk, a commoner turned hedge knight, and his squire Egg, a secret Targaryen prince. While these characters are "obviously admirable and highly sympathetic," the piece argues they cannot fix the underlying structure of society. "Dunk, Egg, and other relatively well-intentioned knights and aristocrats can do some real good at the margin... But the deeper evil is a social and political system in which commoners are largely at the mercy of a small hereditary elite."

The commentary offers a sharp economic critique of the show's world-building, questioning the logic of knighthood. If "any knight can make a knight," the article asks, why hasn't "knight inflation" occurred? It suggests that without legal restrictions, a black market for knighthoods should have emerged, devaluing the status. This analysis adds a layer of intellectual rigor often missing from genre reviews, treating the fantasy setting with the seriousness of a political economy study.

The piece connects these fictional struggles to contemporary political debates, noting that the shows serve as a warning against "postliberals" who promote nostalgia for pre-modern society. "We like to think we have long since left behind aristocratic feudalism and hereditary monarchy of the kind depicted in these series. But it is nonetheless useful to remember the awful nature of these systems." This is the article's most potent move: using fantasy to critique modern political regression. It reminds readers that even if a benevolent leader like Egg eventually takes the throne, history shows that "Westeros's political and social system is still just as bad as before."

Even many modern people who have no desire to return to medieval times tend to forget that we need systemic constraints on government power to address our political and economic problems, and cannot rely on bringing supposedly benevolent leaders to power.

Bottom Line

The strongest part of this argument is its refusal to treat Game of Thrones as mere spectacle, instead using the franchise to dissect the inherent instability of hereditary power and the futility of relying on "good kings." Its biggest vulnerability lies in its somewhat rigid economic critique of the fantasy world's internal logic, which may feel like over-analysis to casual viewers. Ultimately, the piece succeeds in its warning: we must remain vigilant against the seduction of nostalgia for systems that have historically failed the common person.

In recent months, I have not had a chance to write many science fiction and fantasy reviews, like I used to do more often, particularly when it comes to the Game of Thrones/Song of Ice and Fire universe based on the works of George R.R. Martin. I have the excuse that I was busy with other things, such as the tariff case. But in the meantime, we have accumulated two seasons of House of the Dragon and the start of a third, plus the first season of A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms. When you play the game of reviewing Game of Thrones, you win or your connection with your audience dies! So in this post, I'm going to begin trying to make up for lost time.

Overall, I like both series, and if you like the GOT universe generally, you are likely to enjoy them, too. But House of the Dragon has a number of shortcomings that arise partly from the source material (Martin's book Fire and Blood), and partly from its own flaws. Both series also raise interesting political issues that extend the treatment of such matters in the GOT universe as a whole.

Before going on, I should note there will be some spoilers for the two series here. Most of the episodes and plotlines covered here have been out for many months, and this material is based on George R.R. Martin's books Fire and Blood and A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms, both published six or more years ago. For those who read on but still dare to complain about spoilers, I have but one word: Dracarys!

A succession crisis arises by virtue of the fact that King Viserys (a relatively weak ruler) has no male heirs. He therefore designates his daughter Princess Rhaenyra, as his successor. But many members of the Westerosi elite are unwilling to accept a female ruler. Viserys later marries Alicent Hightower (a member of one of the other powerful noble houses), and has sons by her.
