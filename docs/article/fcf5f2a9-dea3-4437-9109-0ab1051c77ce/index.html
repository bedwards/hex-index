Abdul El-Sayed can win moderate voters

Matt Yglesias · Slow Boring ·Aug 12, 2026 ·14 min read
Commentary by Hex Index staff
Deep dives· Original article →

Matt Yglesias makes a counterintuitive claim that could reshape how Democrats view the 2026 Senate map: the very candidate the establishment fears most—a left-wing, Muslim-American progressive—might be the only one capable of winning a purple state like Michigan. In a political climate where the administration's approval ratings are low and the opposition is running a familiar, losing candidate, Yglesias argues that the path to victory isn't a return to the center, but a disciplined pivot to a specific kind of populist nationalism. This isn't just about a Senate race; it's a stress test for whether the Democratic Party can reconcile its progressive base with the moderate, often traditionalist, voters it needs to govern.

The Michigan Paradox

The article begins by dismantling the assumption that a "generic Democrat" would easily defeat the Republican challenger, Mike Rogers, in a state that has swung red under the current executive branch. Yglesias notes that while the political climate should favor Democrats, the primary battle between the establishment-backed Haley Stevens and the insurgent Abdul El-Sayed created a unique dynamic. He writes, "The reason the primary focused so heavily on Israel is that the policy differences between the left and center on 97 percent of issues are either small or unclear." This observation cuts through the noise of the primary, suggesting that the real divide wasn't policy on healthcare or the economy, but a single, highly charged foreign policy issue that obscured broader electability questions.

Yglesias acknowledges the establishment's anxiety, noting that El-Sayed is viewed by some analysts as a "very weak candidate" who turns a safe seat into a coin toss. However, he pushes back against the idea that a moderate like Stevens would have been a safer bet, citing her "literally the worst wins-above-replacement (WAR) rating among the six House Democrats from Michigan." The argument here is that electability is not a static trait of being "moderate," but a function of charisma and connection. As Yglesias puts it, "My view from 50,000 feet is that our generic expectation should be that Stevens would have slightly underperformed a generic Democrat... while El-Sayed will probably deliver an electoral performance similar to what you see from Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez." This framing is compelling because it treats the primary not as a failure of the left, but as a strategic miscalculation by the center that ignored the specific demographics of the state.

The Michigan primary essentially pitted a generic Democrat with slightly subpar communications skills against a charismatic Democrat with a leftist political identity.

Critics might argue that relying on a candidate's charisma to overcome a "slightly red" state is a dangerous gamble, especially when the national environment is hostile. Yet, Yglesias suggests that the alternative—sticking with a candidate with a poor track record of winning—was equally risky.

The Promise of Normie Muslim Politics

The piece's most distinctive contribution is its exploration of what Yglesias calls "the promise of normie Muslim politics." He challenges the stereotype that religiously observant Muslims are automatically far-left on all issues, drawing on a personal anecdote about a conservative, small-business-owner friend who held right-of-center views on regulation but left-wing views on Israel. Yglesias argues that this demographic is crucial for building a winning coalition. "Religiously observant Muslim small-business owners have a lot of right-of-center political attitudes while also holding views that align with the American left on specific questions about Israel and racial profiling of Muslims," he writes. This is a nuanced take that moves beyond the binary of "progressive vs. moderate" and instead looks at the intersection of identity and economic self-interest.

He suggests that El-Sayed is uniquely positioned to bridge this gap, particularly on issues like crime and immigration. Yglesias notes that "second-generation immigrants like El-Sayed are perfectly situated to channel the normie moderate take that openness to immigration is good for the United States and that it should be orderly and lawful." He points to the failure of the Biden-era border management as a "genuine failure" that hurt lawful immigrants, arguing that a pragmatic approach to border security is not a concession to the right but a necessity for the party. This is a bold move for a progressive commentator, as it validates concerns about disorder that are often dismissed by the left. "We need to roll back the insanity and excess of the Trump era without re-opening the doors to unselective onrushes of asylum claims," Yglesias asserts, framing border policy as a matter of protecting the system rather than restricting people.

Most women who wear a headscarf (and most of their husbands) almost certainly have views on transgender issues that are closer to those of the median voter than to the views you often hear from Democratic politicians.

This section is the most provocative, as it suggests that the left's current rhetoric on gender may be alienating a key voting bloc. Yglesias uses the example of tennis star Coco Gauff to illustrate a "humane and common sense way to discuss the sports issue," implying that El-Sayed could model a similar approach. The argument is that a broad coalition cannot "slap away people with traditionalist views about gender," especially when those views are held by a majority of the public. This is a significant departure from the typical progressive playbook, which often prioritizes ideological purity over electoral pragmatism.

Michigan First, Not Palestine First

On the issue of foreign policy, Yglesias identifies a critical tension in El-Sayed's campaign messaging. He praises the candidate's "America First" argument regarding military aid, where El-Sayed argued that "when you keep our money here rather than sending it to some other country's war machine, that's America First." Yglesias calls this a "good message that's correct on the merits," noting that the standard pro-Israel view of unconditional aid "does not make sense." This reframing of foreign aid as a domestic economic issue is a strategic masterstroke that could appeal to working-class voters in Michigan who are struggling with poverty.

However, Yglesias is critical of the candidate's official platform, which calls for an "immediate arms embargo on Israel." He argues that this position is a liability in a general election where the electorate is less sympathetic to the Palestinian cause than in the primary. "The problem I have with El-Sayed on Israel is that this position he outlined to Axios is not actually what his position on the issue is," Yglesias writes. He suggests that El-Sayed should lean harder into the argument that foreign aid is a waste of resources that could be spent on Michigan's neighborhoods. "His best argument will, once again, be the America First / solve problems at home message," he concludes. This is a pragmatic, if somewhat cynical, assessment: the candidate's most effective rhetoric is the one that downplays the ideological purity of his platform in favor of a focus on domestic needs.

El-Sayed is running to solve Michigan's problems, not Palestine's. That's a viable basis for a détente between Jewish and Muslim Democrats that will let us focus on our shared interest in pluralism at home.

Critics might argue that this approach risks alienating the progressive base that sees the arms embargo as a moral imperative. Yglesias acknowledges this tension but suggests that the political reality of a general election requires a different calculus. He posits that if El-Sayed repeats the domestic-focused message enough, he might "talk himself into it," effectively shifting his policy stance to match his electoral strategy.

A Permission Structure on Health Care

Finally, Yglesias addresses the internal Democratic war over healthcare, specifically the "Medicare for All" debate. He argues that El-Sayed has a unique opportunity to end the "destructive" infighting that has paralyzed the party for 15 years. Yglesias highlights El-Sayed's willingness to accept a "pathway" to universal coverage rather than demanding an immediate, "big-bang" transition. "I think the mistake is sometimes to say, 'Okay, we've made these changes and therefore we're good to go. We're not good to go until we have Medicare for All,'" Yglesias quotes El-Sayed as saying. This is a crucial distinction that allows for incremental progress without sacrificing the ultimate goal.

Yglesias argues that the left has been too rigid, insisting that any disagreement with the specific details of the Medicare for All Act is "motivated by corruption." He suggests that this framing has made it impossible to pass legislation that could actually improve healthcare. "This has made it extremely difficult for Democrats to come together around specific pieces of legislation that frontliners want to support," he writes. By positioning El-Sayed as a pragmatist who is willing to work on "steps along the way," Yglesias offers a way out of the stalemate. He notes that the political process has "limited bandwidth for competing priorities," and that the left must be willing to prioritize healthcare over other issues like climate to make progress.

The single most important thing that El-Sayed can do to win moderate support is to leverage his credibility with the left into ending the destructive Medicare for All wars that have paralyzed the Democratic Party for the past 15 years.

This argument is a call for strategic flexibility. Yglesias suggests that the left's obsession with a specific legislative vehicle is holding back the broader goal of universal healthcare. By embracing a more incremental approach, El-Sayed could build a coalition that includes moderates who are wary of radical change but supportive of expansion.

Bottom Line

Matt Yglesias's analysis is a masterclass in political pragmatism, arguing that the path to victory in a polarized era lies not in ideological purity but in the ability to synthesize seemingly contradictory values. His strongest point is the identification of a "normie Muslim" political identity that can bridge the gap between traditionalist voters and progressive goals. However, his argument is vulnerable to the charge that it asks too much of a candidate to constantly reframe their core beliefs for electoral gain. The biggest takeaway is that the Democratic Party's future may depend on its ability to embrace a version of populism that prioritizes domestic needs over foreign policy dogma, and to find a way to make healthcare reform work for everyone without tearing the party apart.

Since September of 2025, my number one anxiety about the midterm Senate map has been Michigan.

Gary Peters, the incumbent, is 67, which is like a teenager in Senator Years. He would have coasted to re-election in a favorable national political climate for Democrats. Republicans are opting to re-run Mike Rogers, who lost this seat two years ago when Donald Trump was popular and carried Michigan. Beating him when Trump has a 38 percent approval rating isn’t brain surgery.

And yet, as I wrote last fall:

The state I’m sweating is Michigan. I’d expect a generic Democrat to beat a generic Republican in a Trump midterm climate in this purple state. But the left is making a strong play to win this nomination with Abdul El-Sayed, who’s a very nice, very smart person, but I just see no reason to think that voters in this slightly red state are particularly excited about an unusually left-wing Democrat.

To make matters worse, the party establishment decided to line up behind Haley Stevens as the electable alternative. Stevens is an unremarkable mainstream House Democrat on ideology, but she has literally the worst wins-above-replacement (WAR) rating among the six House Democrats from Michigan.

I said back then that Mallory McMorrow would be the best option, but that obviously didn’t work out, and we ended up with the Stevens vs. El-Sayed showdown. It’s worth noting that while McMorrow was the best of the three candidates, there were easily a half-dozen potential nominees1 who at least on paper would have been better.

Dan Rosenheck from The Economist published a piece after the primary wrapped up concluding that El-Sayed is a very weak candidate, such that he thinks Michigan is now a coin toss despite the generic ballot’s distinct blue lean. Elliott Morris offered a counter-take that there’s no reason to believe Stevens would have done better than El-Sayed.

I think both of those takes are interesting but probably overstated by relying too heavily on polling done right at the apex of a very heated primary campaign.

Naturally, El-Sayed and Stevens slagging each other so relentlessly drove up negative sentiment toward both candidates and made them look weak. But within days of the primary wrapping up, Stevens was endorsing him, he was praising her, and the whole statewide ticket was doing a joint photo op.
