The only kill switch we've got

Eric Blanc · Labor Politics ·Aug 26, 2026 ·17 min read
Commentary by Hex Index staff
Deep dives· Original article →

This piece cuts through the noise of viral memes and political posturing to expose a fracture line running through the American labor movement that few are willing to name: the clash between the immediate need for construction jobs and the long-term threat of AI displacement. Eric Blanc doesn't just report on the divide; he maps the human and economic logic behind why union leaders are backing a technology that their own members increasingly fear. In an era where public opinion has turned sharply against data centers, Blanc reveals that the labor movement is not a monolith, but a battleground where the promise of a paycheck today wars with the anxiety of obsolescence tomorrow.

The Political Squeeze

The article opens by highlighting how quickly the political landscape has shifted, noting that opposition to local data centers has jumped from 49% to 75% in just over a year. This rapid change has forced politicians across the spectrum to act, with governors in New York, Texas, and Pennsylvania pausing or regulating new projects. Blanc argues that while the public and political classes are reacting to the environmental and social costs, the labor movement is caught in a precarious position. The core tension is that the construction boom is real and immediate, but the jobs it creates are temporary, while the technology they are building threatens to erase other jobs permanently.

"The benefits are so concentrated and the costs are so massive," Thomas Meyer told me. "People don't like big tech billionaires trying to bully or bribe to get what they want."

Blanc effectively uses Meyer's perspective to ground the abstract fear of AI in a concrete grievance: the concentration of power. The author notes that for many workers, fighting data centers is the only tangible way to exert agency against a system that feels rigged. This framing is powerful because it reframes the debate from one of environmentalism alone to one of democratic control. However, critics might note that this view risks oversimplifying the technological inevitability of AI, potentially ignoring that the infrastructure is necessary for the very digital economy that employs millions of knowledge workers.

The Builder's Dilemma

The coverage then pivots to the Building Trades, where the argument for data centers is starkly different. Blanc details how unions like the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers (IBEW) are actively lobbying against bans, citing the sheer volume of high-paying work available. The author points out that data center construction is now three times more unionized than the construction industry as a whole, a statistic that explains the leadership's enthusiasm. Blanc writes, "Data centers now account for 2.3% of all US construction spending, and spending has more than quadrupled since 2020." This economic reality creates a powerful incentive for union leaders to welcome the projects, even as they acknowledge the long-term risks.

"Strong rules do not block good projects. They help us identify which projects are actually good."

Dan McConnell's quote encapsulates the pragmatic middle ground many union leaders are trying to occupy. They are not blind cheerleaders; they are seeking guardrails. Blanc highlights that leaders like McConnell and Rob Bair are simultaneously negotiating limits on AI in call centers and hospitals, showing a nuanced understanding that the technology can be a tool or a threat depending on the context. This is a crucial distinction often missed in broader debates. The author also draws a parallel to the coal industry, noting that the promise of stable, high-wage union jobs in data centers may be as fleeting as the mining boom, a warning that history suggests is well-founded.

"Any legislation that phases out one form of technology in favor of another is going to draw opposition from the union supporting the technology being eliminated while the union representing workers manufacturing the new technology will be supportive of it."

Todd Vachon's observation, cited by Blanc, provides the historical lens needed to understand the current friction. The author uses this to argue that the current split is not unique but is intensified by the speed of AI adoption. The piece subtly references the North America's Building Trades Unions (NABTU) hitting record membership in 2025 and their new partnerships with tech giants like OpenAI, illustrating how deeply entrenched these unions have become in the AI supply chain. This context strengthens the argument that the labor movement is not simply resisting progress but is trying to navigate a transition where their traditional role is being redefined.

The Illusion of the Kill Switch

Blanc challenges the notion that stopping data center construction is a viable solution to the AI problem. He argues that the backlash is often misdirected, fueled by misconceptions about water usage and the nature of the facilities. The author notes that while water consumption is a legitimate concern, the figures are often exaggerated in public discourse, and many facilities use closed-loop systems that do not drain local aquifers. The piece suggests that the public's fear is less about the physical infrastructure and more about the intangible power of AI itself.

"Stopping construction by itself does not create an AI policy or protect workers from automation."

This is perhaps the article's most piercing insight. Blanc posits that the data center is merely the physical manifestation of a digital shift that cannot be halted by local zoning laws. The author points out that the real issue is the lack of a broader social contract or a "New Deal" style investment in infrastructure that would provide alternative employment for the trades. Without such a plan, workers are left with no choice but to build the very machines that may eventually replace them. The piece effectively argues that the "kill switch" the public is looking for doesn't exist in the form of a construction moratorium.

Bottom Line

Eric Blanc's analysis succeeds in humanizing a complex economic transition, revealing that the labor movement's split over data centers is a rational response to conflicting pressures rather than ideological confusion. The strongest part of the argument is its refusal to villainize either side, instead showing how both construction unions and service workers are trapped by a system that offers short-term survival at the cost of long-term security. The biggest vulnerability lies in the lack of a clear alternative; while the author correctly identifies that moratoria are insufficient, the piece offers no concrete roadmap for how to replace the construction boom with sustainable, AI-resilient employment. Readers should watch how the tension between the Building Trades and service unions evolves as the initial construction wave inevitably slows, potentially turning this uneasy truce into open conflict.

The only kill switch we've got

by Eric Blanc · Labor Politics · Read full article

Several weeks ago, Jason Kelce (football player, podcaster, Taylor Swift’s brother-in-law) starred in an ad asking Americans to pee in jars and send them to their nearest AI data center. The spot – a collaboration between Liquid Death and Kelce’s own Garage Beer – was nominally about water consumption. Mostly, though, it was evidence that hating on data centers has gone fully mainstream.

The polling backs this up. The University of Pennsylvania’s Annenberg Public Policy Center found that opposition to local data centers jumped twelve points in four months this year – from 49% in March to 61% in July. And an August Heatmap Pro poll pushed the number to 75%, with more than six in ten Americans strongly opposed – the most negative result since the outlet began tracking the question a year ago.

Americans are wary of AI data centers, and politicians have taken note (much faster than they usually do). In July, New York Governor Kathy Hochul imposed the country’s first statewide moratorium. Weeks later, Republican Texas Governor Greg Abbott – a longtime data center recruiter – paused new grid connections pending an audit. And just this month, Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro – who celebrated Amazon’s data center investment as recently as last year – signed an executive order marketed as the nation’s strictest guardrails on the industry.

But while opposition to data centers grows, the labor movement remains divided, for obvious reasons. The data centers are creating jobs (at least, right now), while AI threatens to eliminate jobs in the future. And those jobs largely belong to different sets of people.

The Building Trades.

So far, the building trades have been supportive. In May, IBEW urged its members to lobby Congress against any ban on data center construction. Several weeks ago, Don Slaiman of IBEW Local 26 took to the New York Times to argue that data centers have delivered tens of thousands of well-paid blue-collar careers, and that communities should be welcoming them. It’s not hard to see why. Data centers now account for 2.3% of all US construction spending, and spending has more than quadrupled since 2020.
