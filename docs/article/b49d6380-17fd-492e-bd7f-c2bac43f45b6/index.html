The social costs of China's education system

Various · Sinification ·Jul 29, 2026 ·25 min read
Commentary by Hex Index staff
Deep dives· Original article →

"The social costs of China's education system are often dismissed as a temporary byproduct of rapid modernization, but this piece from Sinification argues they represent a fundamental fracture in the nation's social fabric. It moves beyond the familiar narrative of 'involution' to diagnose a generation that is either silently withdrawn or psychologically brittle, stripped of the emotional resources needed to navigate adulthood.

The Myth of Fragility

The article opens by challenging the common adult perception that today's youth are simply 'snowflakes' who cannot handle hardship. Sinification reports that while older generations recall a childhood defined by physical deprivation or strict corporal punishment, the current crisis stems from a different source entirely: 'it is not that children [today] have experienced fewer setbacks, but that their ability to accept them has weakened.' The piece argues this fragility is not innate but manufactured by an environment that has removed the natural friction of childhood.

This analysis gains depth when viewed through the lens of 'agricultural involution'—a historical concept describing a system where increased labor input yields diminishing returns. Just as farmers once worked harder for the same yield, today's students are trapped in a high-effort, low-resilience cycle. The editors note that the current generation lacks the 'emotional and social resources needed to absorb setbacks and recover from them effectively.' Instead of building resilience, the system has created a binary of 'good kids' and 'bad kids,' where the former internalize discipline until they collapse, and the latter retreat into digital silence.

'Both groups develop similarly narrow inner lives, easily dominated by grades, adult approval, online distractions or other single sources of emotional meaning.'

Critics might argue that the state's recent 'Double Reduction' policy, which banned for-profit tutoring and capped homework, was a genuine attempt to address these pressures. However, the piece suggests these measures are superficial, failing to tackle the root cause: a society where 'parents have ample reason to justify themselves' by claiming, 'If we refuse to join the rat race, we'll be harming our children.' The policy intervenes in symptoms, not the disease of hyper-competition.

The Silent and the Pitiful

The commentary distinguishes sharply between two emerging archetypes of youth: the 'silent ones' and the 'pitiful ones.' The silent group, predominantly boys, has withdrawn entirely from adult supervision, creating a 'transparent wall' between themselves and the world. A headteacher quoted in the piece describes the situation with palpable helplessness: 'What makes us teachers feel most powerless nowadays is not that they [the students] are naughty and difficult to discipline. Naughty children are actually easier to discipline. It is the silence [that is the hardest to deal with].'

In contrast, the 'pitiful ones' are often girls who have internalized the system's demands to a heartbreaking degree. They are described as 'so obedient that it hurts to see,' having surrendered their autonomy to a schedule managed down to the minute. Sinification highlights the gendered nature of this crisis, noting that while girls suppress their exhaustion to succeed, boys disengage. This dynamic creates a future where 'withdrawal and over-compliance are the contrasting responses to this same narrowness,' leading to a society where mutual understanding between classes and genders is eroding.

The piece draws a sobering parallel to the phenomenon of 'left-behind children' in rural China, where migrant parents have left their offspring in the care of grandparents or alone. The editors quote sociologist Zhou Juan, who observes that these caregivers often 'have no concept of what a childhood ought to look like.' This historical disconnect is now spreading to urban centers, where the 'incomplete socialisation' of children is producing a cohort that is 'selfish' not because they have too much self, but because their self is 'underdeveloped, deprived of the play, freedom and social interaction needed to cultivate judgement and empathy.'

'These problems are likely to persist into adulthood, contributing to mental-health problems, hypersensitivity and intolerance among university students and in wider society.'

The administration's reaction to this malaise has been telling. Rather than addressing the structural pressures of the Gaokao or the economic anxiety driving the competition, the state has framed the resulting 'lying flat' movement as a security threat. As the piece notes, officials in 2026 claimed that 'hostile foreign forces systematically orchestrate the "lying flat narrative" to "brainwash" young people.' This deflection ignores the reality that the 'lying flat' movement is a rational response to a system where effort no longer guarantees mobility.

Bottom Line

The most compelling aspect of this argument is its rejection of the 'resilience training' narrative, correctly identifying that you cannot teach resilience to children who have never been allowed to fail or play. The piece's greatest vulnerability lies in its limited discussion of economic alternatives; without a broader shift in how success is defined in China, the pressure will likely remain. Readers should watch for whether the next generation of policy moves beyond censorship and tutoring bans to address the fundamental disconnect between childhood and the natural world.

Orna Naftali is Abraham Miller Chair in Chinese Studies and Associate Professor in the Department of Asian Studies at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. An anthropologist specializing in childhood, youth, education, and the family, as well as citizenship, legal consciousness, and national identity, she has authored three books: Children, Rights, and Modernity in China (2014), Children in China (2016), and Mobilising China's One-Child Generation: Education, Nationalism and Youth Militarisation in the PRC (2024). Her current research explores gender education, youth bullying, and juvenile delinquency in contemporary China. — Many thanks to her for contributing to this newsletter.

Debates over education and parenting often mirror deeper public anxieties about society’s current path and future direction. In the following essay on Chinese childhood, sociologist Zhou Juan delivers a sobering assessment, arguing that the crisis facing today’s children points to a fundamental fracture within Chinese society.

Zhou’s analysis reflects a wider academic and media discourse regarding youth issues—particularly isolation driven by digital addiction, soaring psychological distress and rising suicidal ideation. As she and other observers contend, this youth malaise is fuelled by ruthless academic competition and the strict regimentation of children’s time. Zhou argues that parents’ relentless drive for achievement strips children of emotional resilience, free play and a space of their own. She further notes that this pressurised landscape reveals distinct gender dynamics: girls frequently internalise strict discipline to succeed academically, whereas struggling boys are more likely to disengage or rebel. Meanwhile, China’s rural “left-behind children”, raised away from their migrant parents, face profound emotional detachment from caregivers who in Zhou’s words, “have no concept of what a childhood ought to look like.” Contrasting these contemporary conditions with her own 1980s-and-90s rural childhood, Zhou maintains that youth apathy today stems fundamentally from a growing disconnect with the natural environment and from “incomplete socialisation”. She warns that such deficient early experiences give rise to acute psychological distress that persists well into higher education and young adulthood.

Zhou’s insightful analysis helps explain the recent rise of the ‘lying flat’ (躺平) movement in China. Overwhelmed by 内卷 (“involutionary”) competition in school and the workplace, a growing number of Gen-Z and millennial youth are consciously lowering their expectations, choosing to forego home ownership, marriage and hyper-competitive career tracks.
