Reasonable accommodation

11 min read

Based on Wikipedia: Reasonable accommodation

In 1978, a young accountant named Karen Thompson walked into her office in New Jersey, carrying a small, folded piece of paper that would eventually reshape the legal landscape of American workplaces. She was a Jehovah's Witness, and her faith prohibited her from working on Saturdays, the Sabbath. Her employer, a large corporation, had a strict policy: work or leave. They refused to adjust her schedule, even though her colleagues could easily cover her shifts. Thompson was fired. Her lawsuit, which wound its way through the courts, did not just change her life; it forced a national reckoning with the question of how much an institution must bend before it breaks. This is the story of reasonable accommodation, a legal doctrine that sits at the fragile intersection of individual conscience and organizational rigidity, a concept that asks society to decide whether diversity is a value to be accommodated or a burden to be endured.

To understand the weight of this concept, one must first strip away the sterile language of legal briefs and look at the human reality it governs. Reasonable accommodation is not merely a bureaucratic checkbox for HR departments. It is the mechanism by which a blind employee might navigate a building designed for sight, by which a Muslim nurse might pray during a shift, or by which a parent of a child with autism might adjust their hours to manage school drop-offs. At its core, it is a recognition that the world was not built for everyone in its current state, and that equality requires more than just the absence of discrimination; it requires the active, often inconvenient, modification of environments to allow full participation.

The legal architecture of this doctrine in the United States was not built overnight. It began with the Civil Rights Act of 1964, specifically Title VII, which prohibited employment discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin. However, the initial text was silent on the practicalities of religious practice. It took a specific judicial interpretation and subsequent legislative amendments to flesh out the requirement. The Supreme Court case Trans World Airlines, Inc. v. Hardison in 1977 became a pivotal, and controversial, turning point. In this case, the court ruled that an employer did not have to accommodate an employee's religious needs if doing so would impose more than a "de minimis" cost on the business. This phrase—more than a de minimis cost—became the legal threshold, a low bar that made it remarkably easy for employers to deny accommodations. If the cost was trivial, or if the inconvenience was minor, the accommodation was not required. This standard effectively placed the burden of religious practice almost entirely on the individual, suggesting that the smooth functioning of the corporate machine trumped the spiritual necessities of the worker.

The implications of Hardison were profound and immediate. For decades, employees seeking to align their work lives with their religious observances faced a steep uphill battle. The standard meant that if accommodating a Sabbath-observant employee required shifting a few hours of work, or if it meant paying a supervisor overtime to cover a shift, the employer could legally say no. The cost of inclusion was deemed too high. This created a paradox where discrimination was illegal, yet the refusal to make small, manageable adjustments that would allow a person to keep their job was perfectly legal. The law, in its attempt to protect businesses from disruption, had inadvertently sanctioned a form of indirect exclusion.

The landscape began to shift again with the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) of 1990. Here, the concept of reasonable accommodation was codified with much greater specificity, moving beyond the vague "de minimis" standard of religious cases to a more robust analysis of "undue hardship." The ADA defined undue hardship as an action requiring significant difficulty or expense. This was a much higher bar for employers to clear. Suddenly, a company could not simply claim that installing a ramp was inconvenient; they had to prove it was financially devastating or structurally impossible. The law now recognized that a wheelchair user could not simply "try harder" to navigate stairs; the stairs themselves had to change. This was a philosophical pivot from asking the individual to adapt to the environment, to asking the environment to adapt to the individual.

Consider the story of a construction worker in Chicago who lost his leg in an accident. Before the ADA's full implementation, his career might have ended at the hospital bed. The employer, citing the difficulty of modifying the job site, might have terminated him. Under the framework of reasonable accommodation, the employer is now required to engage in an interactive process. They must sit down with the worker, assess the essential functions of the job, and brainstorm solutions. Perhaps the worker can no longer climb the scaffolding, but can manage the logistics from a modified office space on the ground floor. Perhaps a specialized lift can be installed. The law demands that the employer try, to dig deep for solutions before declaring the job impossible. This interactive process is the heartbeat of the accommodation doctrine. It is a dialogue, a negotiation of reality where the goal is not to lower standards, but to find a different path to the same outcome.

Yet, the doctrine is not without its critics and its complexities. There is a legitimate tension between the rights of the individual and the operational needs of the organization. When does an accommodation become a fundamental alteration of the job? If a teacher is denied the accommodation of not grading papers on weekends because the school district argues that parents expect timely feedback, the court must weigh the teacher's need against the educational mission. The line is often blurry. In some cases, the accommodation sought by one employee can infringe upon the rights or safety of another. For instance, a religious objection to wearing a specific uniform might conflict with safety regulations designed to protect all workers. In these conflicts, the courts often have to make difficult, granular decisions about what is truly "essential" to a job and what is merely a preference of the employer.

The financial argument is frequently the loudest objection raised by businesses. They argue that the cumulative cost of accommodating every employee with a disability or religious need is unsustainable, particularly for small businesses with thin margins. There is truth to this concern. The cost of modifying a workspace, purchasing adaptive technology, or restructuring shifts can be significant. However, proponents of the doctrine argue that these costs are often overstated and that the benefits of retaining experienced, diverse talent far outweigh the expenses. A study by the Job Accommodation Network (JAN) has repeatedly shown that the majority of workplace accommodations cost nothing. They involve simple changes like flexible scheduling, altered workspaces, or the use of existing technology in new ways. When costs are involved, they are often one-time investments that yield long-term dividends in employee retention and morale.

The concept has also expanded beyond the walls of the office. In the realm of education, the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) mandates that schools provide reasonable accommodations to students with disabilities, ensuring they have access to the same educational opportunities as their peers. This might mean providing a student with a hearing impairment with a sign language interpreter, or allowing a student with dyslexia extra time on exams. These are not special privileges; they are the leveling of a playing field that was historically tilted against them. Without these accommodations, the promise of public education for all children remains a hollow slogan. The refusal to accommodate in schools is a refusal to educate, a denial of the child's potential that echoes through their entire adult life.

The history of this doctrine is also a history of resistance. It has not been a smooth march toward inclusivity. There have been countless cases where employers have fought tooth and nail against accommodation, often hiding behind vague claims of hardship or using the "de minimis" standard as a shield. The legal battles have been long and exhausting for the individuals involved. They are often forced to quit their jobs, lose their livelihoods, and face public scrutiny before a court finally rules in their favor. The emotional toll on these individuals is immense. They are told that their faith is too burdensome, that their disability is too disruptive, that their needs are not worth the effort. This message of exclusion is a form of violence in itself, a slow erosion of dignity.

Today, the conversation around reasonable accommodation has evolved to include new frontiers. The rise of remote work during the global pandemic of 2020 and 2021 forced a rapid, unplanned experiment in accommodation. Suddenly, the rigid structures of the office were dissolved, and millions of employees worked from home. For many people with disabilities, this was a revelation. The commute, the inaccessible bathroom, the fluorescent lights, the noise—all of these barriers vanished or became manageable in a home environment. The pandemic proved that many accommodations were not just possible, but practical and efficient. The question now is whether the post-pandemic world will revert to the old norms of physical presence, or whether it will embrace the flexibility that the pandemic inadvertently mandated. Employers are being asked to reconsider what "undue hardship" means in a world where the traditional office is no longer the only option.

There is also a growing recognition that accommodation is not a static concept. It changes as technology advances. What was impossible twenty years ago is often trivial today. Screen readers, speech-to-text software, ergonomic keyboards, and virtual meeting platforms have transformed the workplace. The law must keep pace with these innovations. A definition of "undue hardship" that made sense in 1990 may not hold up in 2026. The digital age has lowered the cost of many accommodations, making the argument against them weaker. Yet, new challenges have emerged. The digital divide, the accessibility of new software, and the ethics of AI-driven hiring processes are the new frontiers where the doctrine of reasonable accommodation will be tested.

The human cost of failure in this domain is high. When an employer refuses to accommodate, the result is often not just a lost job, but a lost identity. Work is a primary source of meaning and community for most adults. To be told that one cannot work because of a disability or a religious belief is to be told that one does not belong in society. It is a message of marginalization that can lead to isolation, poverty, and despair. Conversely, when accommodation is granted, the results are transformative. People are able to contribute their talents, support their families, and find purpose in their labor. The difference between a workplace that accommodates and one that does not is the difference between a community that thrives and one that fractures.

The doctrine of reasonable accommodation is a testament to the idea that our institutions are not immutable. They are human creations, and like all human creations, they can be changed. The law requires us to bend, to adjust, to look for the path of least resistance that still leads to the goal of equality. It is a call to humility, an admission that our default settings are not perfect for everyone. It asks us to imagine a world where the barrier is not the person, but the environment, and where the solution is not to fix the person, but to fix the environment.

In the end, the story of reasonable accommodation is a story about the measure of a society. It is easy to claim we value diversity; it is much harder to pay the price for it. It is easy to say we care about inclusion; it is much harder to change our schedules, our buildings, and our habits to make it real. The legal framework provides the tools, but the will to use them comes from us. Every time an employer agrees to a flexible schedule, every time a school provides an interpreter, every time a landlord installs a ramp, they are affirming the dignity of the individual. They are saying, "You belong here, and we will make the space work for you."

The journey from Hardison to the present day has been one of gradual expansion, a slow widening of the circle of inclusion. But the work is never done. As society changes, as new disabilities are understood, as new religious movements emerge, and as technology reshapes our reality, the question of what is "reasonable" will continue to evolve. The law provides a baseline, a floor beneath which we cannot fall, but the ceiling is as high as our imagination and our empathy allow. The true test of reasonable accommodation is not in the courtrooms, but in the everyday choices of employers, educators, and citizens. It is in the willingness to see the person behind the barrier and to take the small, often inconvenient step to remove it. That is the heart of the matter. That is the work that remains.
