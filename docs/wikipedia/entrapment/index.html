Entrapment

15 min read

Based on Wikipedia: Entrapment

In the summer of 1932, a man named Jacob was arrested in Los Angeles not for committing a crime he had planned, but for one he had never considered until a federal agent, posing as a desperate buyer, pressed him into it. Jacob had no prior record, no history of drug dealing, and no intent to sell heroin. He was a quiet man with a family and a steady job. Yet, through a series of calculated manipulations by law enforcement, he was coaxed into a transaction that landed him in federal prison. His case would eventually reach the Supreme Court, where the justices would be forced to confront a disturbing question: at what point does the government stop catching criminals and start creating them? This is the story of entrapment, a legal doctrine that serves as the only shield an ordinary citizen has against the state's most aggressive, overzealous, and sometimes corrupt tactics. It is a story of the fine line between catching a predator and manufacturing a victim, a line that has been blurred, crossed, and redrawn in courtrooms from the nation's capital to the smallest county towns for nearly a century.

To understand entrapment, one must first discard the popular image of the sting operation as a heroic pursuit of justice. While the television drama depicts a cunning detective outwitting a hardened criminal, the reality of entrapment is often far more insidious. It is a scenario where a person, who might otherwise live a law-abiding life, is targeted by law enforcement for their vulnerability, their poverty, or their naivety. The government agent does not merely provide an opportunity for a crime to occur; they actively implant the criminal intent in the mind of the defendant. They become the architect of the offense, building the case brick by brick until the defendant has no choice but to comply. This is not policing; it is a form of psychological engineering where the state acts as the provocateur.

The legal definition of entrapment is narrow but potent. In the United States, the defense of entrapment generally requires the defendant to prove two things: first, that the government induced them to commit the crime, and second, that they were not predisposed to commit it before the government approached them. This is known as the subjective test, which focuses on the state of mind of the defendant. It asks: Was this person already a criminal waiting to happen, or did the government create a criminal out of thin air? There is also an objective test, used in some jurisdictions, which focuses on the conduct of the police. It asks: Was the government's conduct so excessive that it would have induced a normally law-abiding person to commit the crime? Under this view, even if the defendant was predisposed, the police may have gone too far, and the government's own misconduct warrants the dismissal of the charges.

The origins of this doctrine are rooted in the early days of Prohibition, a time when the federal government's thirst for enforcement often outstripped its respect for individual liberty. In the landmark case of Sorrells v. United States in 1932, the Supreme Court grappled with a case almost identical to Jacob's. The defendant, a former soldier, was visited repeatedly by an undercover agent who posed as a tourist looking for moonshine. The agent claimed to be a friend of a mutual acquaintance and played on the defendant's patriotism and desire to help a "friend." After numerous rejections, the defendant finally relented and sold the agent a bottle of whiskey. The Court, in a 5-4 decision, ruled in favor of the defendant, establishing the subjective test. Justice Owen Roberts, writing for the majority, famously noted that the entrapment defense was necessary to prevent the government from "setting a trap" for the innocent. He argued that the courts could not condone the practice of government agents inducing citizens to commit crimes they would not have otherwise committed.

However, the decision in Sorrells was not a victory for the innocent without a cost. The dissent, led by Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes, argued that the Court was overstepping its bounds by creating a new defense that was not explicitly found in the Constitution or federal statutes. Hughes warned that the decision would allow criminals to escape justice simply because they were tricked into committing a crime. This tension between the need for effective law enforcement and the protection of individual rights has defined the doctrine of entrapment ever since. The majority view, which became the law of the land, was that the government should not be allowed to manufacture crime, but the dissent's warning that the defense could be abused by guilty defendants has never fully disappeared.

The decades following Sorrells saw the doctrine evolve, but it remained fraught with ambiguity. In 1958, the Supreme Court decided Sherman v. United States, a case that would further refine the subjective test. In Sherman, the defendant was a recovering heroin addict who was approached by an undercover agent posing as a fellow addict. The agent claimed to be in need of drugs and begged the defendant for help. Over several weeks, the agent harassed the defendant, expressing his desperation and pleading for assistance. Eventually, the defendant, overwhelmed by the agent's persistence and the agent's claims of shared suffering, agreed to sell him some drugs. The Court found that the defendant was not predisposed to commit the crime and that the government's conduct was excessive. Justice Felix Frankfurter, writing for the plurality, emphasized that the defense was based on the "public policy" that the government should not "create crimes" to increase its arrest statistics. He argued that the entrapment defense was a necessary check on the "overreaching" of law enforcement, even if it meant that some guilty individuals might go free.

But what happens when the government argues that the defendant was predisposed, regardless of the inducement? This is where the subjective test becomes a battleground of credibility. The prosecution must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was ready and willing to commit the crime before the government approached them. This often involves digging into the defendant's past, looking for any prior arrests, any statements they may have made, or any behavior that suggests a criminal intent. In Jacobson v. United States in 1992, the Supreme Court addressed a case where the defendant, a farmer, was charged with receiving child pornography through the mail. The government had not approached him initially; instead, they sent him numerous solicitations from fake organizations over a period of several years. The solicitations were designed to appeal to his specific interests and to gradually desensitize him to the illegal nature of the materials. When he finally ordered the materials, the government arrested him. The Court ruled in favor of Jacobson, finding that the government had not proven that he was predisposed to commit the crime before the government's extensive campaign of solicitation began. Justice Byron White, writing for the majority, stated that the government's conduct was "too intrusive" and that the defendant's predisposition could not be assumed simply because he eventually complied with the government's requests.

The Jacobson decision was a significant victory for the entrapment defense, but it also highlighted the difficulties in applying the doctrine in the modern era. In the age of the internet, the line between providing an opportunity and inducing a crime has become even more blurred. Undercover agents now operate in chat rooms, on social media platforms, and in virtual environments where they can pose as minors, vulnerable individuals, or desperate buyers. They can engage in long-term conversations with unsuspecting individuals, building trust and slowly coaxing them into illegal activities. The question of predisposition becomes even more complex when the government has the power to control the narrative and the environment in which the crime is committed. How can a defendant prove that they were not predisposed when the government has spent months or even years manipulating their thoughts and actions?

The human cost of entrapment is often invisible in the legal statistics. It is not just about the defendant who goes free or the one who goes to prison; it is about the families torn apart, the reputations destroyed, and the communities eroded by the perception that the government is out to get them. When a man is arrested for a crime he did not plan, his children may grow up without a father, his wife may lose her home, and his neighbors may view him with suspicion. The psychological toll of being targeted by the government, of being manipulated into a crime, can be devastating. It can lead to a loss of faith in the justice system, a sense of powerlessness, and a deep-seated distrust of authority.

Consider the case of the "FBI sting" operations that have targeted vulnerable individuals in recent years. In many of these cases, the government has targeted individuals with mental illnesses, intellectual disabilities, or a history of trauma. These individuals are often easy targets for government agents who know how to exploit their vulnerabilities. They may be promised money, protection, or a sense of belonging in exchange for their cooperation. When they eventually comply with the government's requests, they are arrested and charged with serious crimes. The government argues that they were predisposed to commit the crime, but the reality is that they were often just desperate for connection or support. The result is a tragedy: a life ruined not by a genuine criminal intent, but by the government's own manipulation.

The doctrine of entrapment is also a reflection of the broader tension between national security and individual liberty. In the wake of the September 11 attacks, the government has expanded its surveillance and sting operations in the name of preventing terrorism. Undercover agents have been deployed to identify and arrest individuals who they believe pose a threat to national security. In many of these cases, the government has been accused of entrapping individuals who had no prior history of violence or extremist views. They have been targeted because of their race, their religion, or their political views, and they have been coaxed into committing crimes that they would never have considered otherwise. The government argues that these operations are necessary to prevent future attacks, but the critics argue that they are a form of government overreach that violates the principles of due process and fair play.

The legal system has struggled to balance these competing interests. In some cases, the courts have been willing to dismiss charges where the government's conduct was egregious. In others, they have been reluctant to find entrapment, even when the evidence suggests that the defendant was not predisposed to commit the crime. The subjective test, with its focus on the defendant's state of mind, is inherently difficult to apply. It requires the jury to speculate about the defendant's thoughts and intentions before the government approached them. It also requires the jury to weigh the government's conduct against the defendant's predisposition, a task that is often subjective and fraught with bias.

The objective test, which focuses on the government's conduct, offers a different approach. It asks whether the government's actions were so excessive that they would have induced a normally law-abiding person to commit a crime. This test is more concerned with the integrity of the justice system than with the guilt or innocence of the defendant. It is a way of saying that the government should not be allowed to use its power to create crimes, even if the defendant is guilty. But the objective test is not without its critics. Some argue that it is too broad and that it could allow guilty defendants to escape justice simply because the government used aggressive tactics. Others argue that it is too narrow and that it fails to account for the unique vulnerabilities of certain individuals.

Despite these challenges, the doctrine of entrapment remains a crucial safeguard for individual liberty. It is a reminder that the government's power is not unlimited and that there are limits to what the state can do in the name of law enforcement. It is a protection for the innocent, for the vulnerable, and for those who might otherwise be targeted by the government's overzealous agents. But it is also a reminder that the justice system is not perfect and that the line between catching a criminal and creating one is often thin and easily crossed.
