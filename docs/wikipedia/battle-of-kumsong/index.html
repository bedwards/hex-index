Battle of Kumsong

14 min read

Based on Wikipedia: Battle of Kumsong

On July 13, 1953, under the cover of a moonless night, the Chinese People's Volunteer Army launched a massive artillery barrage that turned the hills around the Kumsong River into a sea of fire. It was not a skirmish or a probe; it was the largest offensive operation of the entire Korean War, involving 150,000 soldiers and nearly 2,000 guns. The target was not a strategic city or a supply hub, but a narrow, jagged strip of terrain known as the Kumsong Salient, a protrusion of North Korean and Chinese lines that had long been a thorn in the side of the United Nations Command. For the soldiers huddled in the foxholes of the Republic of Korea's 5th and 7th Divisions, the ground did not just shake; it ceased to exist. In the hours that followed, the battle would claim thousands of lives, shatter the illusion of a static war, and force a final, bloody reckoning before the armistice could finally be signed.

To understand the sheer scale of the violence that erupted that night, one must first grasp the absurdity of the geography. The Kumsong Salient was a wedge of land that pushed southward from the Chinese line, threatening the main UN supply routes and the city of Pyongyang in the north. For months, it had been a grinding stalemate, a place where artillery duels replaced infantry charges, where men died by the dozen in the mud while the world watched the diplomatic negotiations in Panmunjom with a mixture of hope and exhaustion. The United States and its allies believed the armistice was imminent. The Chinese, led by General Peng Dehuai and advised by Soviet military experts, held a different view. They understood that in war, the last words spoken on the battlefield are often the ones that define the peace. If the armistice was to be signed, the Chinese leadership decided, it would be signed from a position of strength, not desperation. They would seize the salient, flatten the UN defenses, and present the world with a fait accompli.

The preparation for the assault was meticulous and terrifying in its scope. The Chinese did not simply gather their troops; they moved them with a precision that the UN Command failed to detect. Under the cover of darkness and heavy rain, they dug tunnels, concealed artillery pieces in reverse slopes, and stockpiled ammunition in quantities that defied belief. They were not fighting with the improvisational tactics of the early war years. This was a mechanized, coordinated effort, supported by the Soviet Union with advanced radar and communication gear. The UN forces, particularly the Republic of Korea (ROK) army, were the primary targets. The Chinese command viewed the ROK troops as the weakest link in the UN chain, a perception that would be tested to its breaking point. The plan was simple in its brutality: overwhelm the ROK divisions with a concentrated artillery strike, followed by a multi-pronged infantry assault that would cut off their retreat and annihilate them.

When the barrage began at 22:00 on July 13, the sky above the Kumsong River turned a sickly orange. The sound was not a roar but a continuous, deafening crack that seemed to tear the fabric of the night. Within minutes, the ROK 5th and 7th Divisions, holding the forward lines, were being erased. The Chinese artillery, numbering in the hundreds, fired with such density that the earth itself seemed to liquefy. Soldiers who had survived the harsh winters of the previous two years, who had faced down American air power and naval gunfire, now found themselves trapped in a cage of steel and fire. There was no time to dig deeper, no time to call for help. The shells were falling so fast that men were buried alive in their own foxholes. The human cost of this opening salvo was immediate and catastrophic. By dawn, the forward positions of the ROK 5th Division had been completely overrun. The 7th Division, though slightly more prepared, was pinned down, its command structure shattered by the sheer weight of the bombardment.

The infantry assault that followed was a testament to the sheer will of the Chinese soldiers. They did not charge in a wave; they flowed over the craters and the bodies of the dead, using the smoke and dust as a shield. The fighting was close, brutal, and personal. It was not a battle of maneuver but a battle of attrition, fought with bayonets, grenades, and the cold steel of the trench. The ROK troops, many of them conscripts who had never seen such an overwhelming concentration of force, fought with a desperation born of knowing there was no escape. In the village of Kumsong, the streets became a slaughterhouse. Civilians who had taken refuge in the area were caught in the crossfire, their homes reduced to rubble. The war had been fought on the backs of the Korean people for three years, and in this final, violent chapter, they bore the brunt of the violence. The Chinese forces, driven by the belief that this offensive would secure their legacy and force the UN to accept their terms, pressed forward with a ferocity that was both inspiring and horrifying.

The United Nations Command was forced to react with speed and violence of its own. General Mark Clark, the commander of the UN forces, and General James Van Fleet, who commanded the Eighth Army, could not afford to lose the Kumsong Salient. To lose it would be to admit that the Chinese could dictate the terms of the armistice. They ordered an immediate counterattack, pouring in fresh troops and unleashing every ounce of air power they had. American and ROK artillery, which had been relatively quiet in the days leading up to the offensive, now joined the fray. The sky filled with the roar of B-29 bombers and the flash of 155mm howitzers. The air was thick with smoke and the smell of cordite. The battle became a chaotic swirl of friend and foe, where the lines were blurred by the smoke and the confusion of night fighting.

The human toll of the counterattack was just as severe as the initial assault. The ROK 2nd Infantry Division, sent to reinforce the crumbling front, arrived to find a landscape of destruction. The men who had survived the Chinese barrage were exhausted, wounded, and demoralized. The Chinese, however, were relentless. They had dug in deep, using the very craters created by the UN artillery as defensive positions. They had turned the battlefield into a fortress, making every inch of ground a deadly trap. The fighting raged for three days, from July 13 to July 16. It was a war within a war, a microcosm of the entire Korean conflict. The soldiers on both sides were tired, hungry, and terrified. They fought not for ideology or glory, but for the man next to them, for the hope of seeing another sunrise.

The Chinese offensive achieved a tactical success that would ripple through history. They captured the Kumsong Salient, pushing the UN lines back several kilometers. They inflicted heavy casualties on the ROK army, demonstrating that the South Korean forces, while brave, were not invincible. But the cost was staggering. The Chinese suffered thousands of casualties, many of them young men who had never seen the ocean or the cities of the south. The ROK divisions were decimated, their ranks filled with the dead and the wounded. The battle was a pyrrhic victory. It proved that the Chinese could still strike with devastating force, but it also proved that the UN Command was willing to pay any price to hold the line. The armistice negotiations, which had been dragging on for months, suddenly gained a new urgency. The world watched in horror as the final bloodletting of the war took place.

The battle did not end with a grand victory for either side. It ended with a grim realization. The Chinese had shown they could take the ground, but they could not hold it against the overwhelming firepower of the UN. The UN had shown they could stop the Chinese, but only at a terrible cost. The battle of Kumsong was the last major offensive of the war, a final, desperate gamble that would shape the terms of the armistice. On July 27, 1953, just six weeks after the battle began, the armistice was signed. The line was drawn, not where the Chinese wanted it, but where the UN forces had held it after the bloodiest fighting of the entire conflict. The Kumsong Salient was returned to the Chinese, but the cost of that concession was written in the blood of the soldiers who had died there.
