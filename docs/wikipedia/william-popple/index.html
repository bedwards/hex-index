Based on Wikipedia: William Popple

In 1676, a man named William Popple stood trial not for a crime of violence, but for the dangerous act of publishing a letter that challenged the divine right of kings. He was an English translator, a minor civil servant, and a man whose quiet dedication to the circulation of forbidden ideas would eventually link the intellectual ferment of the Glorious Revolution to the broader currents of European thought. His story is not one of battlefield glory or royal decree, but of the quiet, persistent machinery of the underground press, where paper and ink became the weapons that dismantled an old order. To understand Popple is to understand the precarious position of the intellectual in an age where a single misplaced word could lead to the loss of one's livelihood, or worse, one's head. He was a man who lived in the shadow of John Locke, the great philosopher of liberalism, yet his contributions were distinct, rooted in the practical application of radical thought rather than its abstract theorization. Popple's life serves as a crucial bridge between the theoretical arguments of the 1680s and the political realities of the 1690s, a time when the very definition of English liberty was being hammered out in the fires of exile and return.

Born around 1630, the son of a Puritan minister, William Popple was raised in an environment where the tension between religious dissent and state authority was not an academic exercise but a daily reality. His father, also named William Popple, was a man of principle who found himself at odds with the ecclesiastical establishment, a lineage that predisposed the younger Popple to question authority. Unlike many of his contemporaries who sought advancement through the traditional channels of the church or the royal court, Popple found his calling in the realm of translation and correspondence. He was fluent in French, a skill that would become his primary vehicle for influence. In the seventeenth century, French was the lingua franca of the European intelligentsia, the language of diplomacy, philosophy, and, increasingly, of radical political critique. By mastering this language, Popple positioned himself at the intersection of English political exiles and the broader European discourse.

The mid-1670s were a time of intense political anxiety in England. The memory of the Civil War was still fresh, and the fear of Catholic absolutism, personified by the future King James II, loomed large over the Protestant establishment. It was in this atmosphere that Popple began to circulate his most famous work: a translation of a letter by the French philosopher Jean Barbeyrac, or more accurately, a compilation of letters that argued for the right of resistance against tyranny. These were not abstract musings; they were blueprints for regime change. The document Popple translated and disseminated argued that when a ruler violates the trust of the people, the social contract is broken, and the people have the right to withdraw their obedience. This was a dangerous idea in the court of Charles II, where the doctrine of non-resistance was the official state religion. To publish such a text was to invite the wrath of the Crown.

Popple's involvement in the underground press was not a solitary endeavor. He was part of a network of exiles and sympathizers who operated out of places like Rotterdam and London, smuggling pamphlets across the Channel. These pamphlets were the social media of their day, unregulated, viral, and capable of shifting public opinion with alarming speed. Popple's role was that of a conduit. He took the sophisticated arguments of Continental thinkers, refined them for an English audience, and distributed them through channels that bypassed the official censorship apparatus. This was a high-stakes game. The authorities were vigilant, and the penalties for sedition were severe. Yet, Popple persisted, driven by a conviction that the truth could not be suppressed by force alone. His work laid the groundwork for the intellectual justification of the Revolution of 1688, which would eventually see the deposition of James II and the installation of William and Mary.

One of the most striking aspects of Popple's career was his relationship with John Locke. While Locke is often celebrated as the father of liberalism, his own writings were published anonymously for much of his life, a precaution born of necessity. Popple, however, was willing to put his name to the cause, or at least to the machinery that spread it. He was not merely a translator; he was a collaborator. Historical records suggest a close association between the two men, with Popple acting as a kind of literary agent and distributor for Locke's more radical ideas. In 1689, after the Glorious Revolution, Locke published his Two Treatises of Government, the seminal work that would define modern political theory. While Locke's name is on the title page, the ideas within had been circulating in the underground for years, often in forms that Popple had helped to shape and distribute. The connection between the two men was not just personal; it was strategic. Locke provided the theory; Popple provided the logistics of dissent.

But the life of a political translator in the seventeenth century was not without its perils. In 1676, Popple found himself in the dock, accused of seditious libel. The charge was serious. He was accused of publishing a letter that was deemed to be a direct attack on the government. The trial was a spectacle, a public demonstration of the state's power to silence its critics. Popple's defense was not to deny the publication, but to argue for the right of free speech and the necessity of public debate. He argued that the people had a right to know the truth about their rulers, and that the suppression of information was a sign of a corrupt government. This was a bold stance, one that reflected the emerging ideals of the Enlightenment. The trial did not result in a conviction, but it did serve as a warning. Popple was forced to flee England for a time, seeking refuge in the Netherlands, where the freedom of the press was more robust. His exile was a testament to the cost of dissent, a reminder that the path to liberty is often paved with personal sacrifice.

During his time in exile, Popple continued his work, translating and distributing political tracts that kept the flame of resistance alive. He was part of a vibrant community of English exiles in Rotterdam, a city that served as a hub for radical thought. Here, he met with other dissidents, including the future Prime Minister, William III, who was then a prince in waiting. The conversations in these exiled circles were not idle chatter; they were the planning sessions for a revolution. Popple's translations provided the intellectual ammunition that would be used to justify the invasion of England in 1688. He understood that the success of the revolution depended not just on military might, but on the ability to convince the English people that their new rulers were legitimate. His work helped to craft the narrative that William of Orange was not an invader, but a liberator, coming to restore the ancient liberties of the English people.

The aftermath of the Glorious Revolution brought a new era for Popple. With the accession of William and Mary, the political climate in England shifted dramatically. The censorship laws were relaxed, and the press flourished. Popple returned to England, no longer a fugitive, but a respected figure in the new political order. He was appointed to a position in the government, a testament to his loyalty and his contributions to the revolution. He served as a translator for the Board of Trade, a role that allowed him to continue his work in the service of the state. But even in his official capacity, Popple remained a champion of liberty. He used his position to advocate for policies that protected the rights of the individual and limited the power of the monarchy. His work in the Board of Trade was not just about economics; it was about the application of liberal principles to the governance of the empire. He argued for free trade, not just as an economic necessity, but as a moral imperative. He believed that the exchange of goods and ideas was essential for the progress of humanity.

Popple's later years were marked by a quiet dedication to his principles. He continued to write and translate, but his work became more focused on the practical application of his ideas. He was involved in the founding of the Bank of England, an institution that was designed to stabilize the economy and limit the power of the Crown. His involvement in this project was a testament to his belief in the importance of institutions that could check the power of the state. He understood that liberty was not just a matter of words, but of structures. The Bank of England was one such structure, a mechanism that could prevent the monarchy from spending the country into ruin. Popple's role in its creation was not that of a visionary, but of a pragmatist. He helped to design the rules that would govern the bank, ensuring that it would serve the public interest rather than the whims of the king.

Popple brought these ideas into the public sphere, where they could be debated, discussed, and ultimately, acted upon.
