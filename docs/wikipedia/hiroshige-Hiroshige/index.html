Hiroshige

11 min read

Based on Wikipedia: Hiroshige

Born Ando Tokutarō in 1797 in Edo, the modern-day Tokyo, Hiroshige was not born into the artistic elite. His father served as a fire watchman, a position that, while lowly in the grand hierarchy of the shogunate, afforded the young boy a unique vantage point. From the watchtower, he looked down upon a city of over a million souls, a metropolis that was the largest in the world at the time, a sprawling network of wooden houses, canals, and bridges that pulsed with the energy of a merchant class rising in economic power but lacking political agency. This duality defined his life and his work: the tension between the rigid, static structure of the samurai class and the dynamic, fluid reality of the townspeople. When his father died in 1811, Hiroshige inherited the post of fire watchman, a duty he would eventually abdicate to pursue art, a decision that marked him as a traitor to his station but a savior of his own spirit.

He entered the studio of Utagawa Toyohiro, one of the leading masters of the ukiyo-e tradition, around 1812, taking the name Hiroshige. His early works were typical of the genre: portraits of kabuki actors and beautiful women, the two dominant subjects of the floating world. These prints were mass-produced commodities, affordable to the merchant class who had the money but not the social standing to commission oil paintings from the aristocracy. The production process was a complex, collaborative effort involving the artist, the engraver, and the printer, a division of labor that allowed for the rapid dissemination of images. A single block could be carved, inked, and printed thousands of times, turning art into a form of popular media. Yet, despite the commercial success of these early actor prints, Hiroshige struggled to find his distinct voice in a crowded field dominated by the towering figure of Katsushika Hokusai. It was not until he turned his gaze away from the stage and toward the land itself that he found the rhythm that would define his legacy.

The turning point came in 1832, an event that would alter the course of his career and the trajectory of Japanese art. The government organized a procession to transport tribute horses from Edo to the imperial capital of Kyoto, a journey that Hiroshige was invited to join as an official attendant. This was not a mere tourist excursion; it was a state-sanctioned pilgrimage that took him along the Tōkaidō, the great coastal road connecting the two capitals. The road was a artery of the nation, teeming with travelers, merchants, pilgrims, and soldiers. For the first time, Hiroshige was exposed to the vastness of the Japanese landscape, the changing weather patterns, the diverse regional architectures, and the daily lives of the people who inhabited the spaces between the great cities. He saw the mud-slicked roads, the storm-battered inns, the fishermen battling the rough seas, and the weary travelers seeking shelter. He sketched furiously, capturing the essence of the journey rather than the grandeur of the destination.

Upon his return to Edo, he began work on The Fifty-Three Stations of the Tōkaidō, a series that would become his magnum opus and one of the most famous print series in the history of art. Published between 1833 and 1834, the series consisted of fifty-five prints, including the departure and arrival points, each depicting a specific post town along the road. Unlike his predecessors, who often relied on stylized conventions and idealized landscapes, Hiroshige approached the scenery with a reporter's eye. He did not shy away from the mundane or the difficult. He depicted the rain at Atsuka, the fog at Mishima, the snow at Hakone, and the wind at Kakegawa. He focused on the human element, showing travelers struggling against the elements, children playing in the mud, and women balancing baskets on their heads. He captured the transient nature of the journey, the sense of movement and the feeling of being a small part of a vast, indifferent world.

"The road is long, the rain is heavy, and the traveler is alone."

This sentiment, though not a direct quote from Hiroshige, encapsulates the emotional core of his work. He was a master of atmosphere, using color and composition to evoke a specific mood or feeling. He understood that the beauty of a landscape was not just in its physical form but in the way it made the viewer feel. In Sudden Shower over Shin-Ōhashi Bridge and Atake, he captures the sudden, chaotic nature of a summer downpour, the diagonal lines of the rain cutting across the composition, the umbrellas tilting, the people rushing for cover. The print is not a static image; it is a moment frozen in time, a snapshot of life as it is actually lived, with all its messiness and unpredictability. He used a technique known as bokashi, or gradation, to create subtle shifts in color, allowing the sky to fade from a deep blue to a pale grey, suggesting the approach of a storm or the setting of the sun. This technique gave his prints a sense of depth and atmosphere that was unprecedented in the medium.

The success of the Tōkaidō series was immediate and overwhelming. It spawned countless imitations and pirated editions, a testament to its popularity. But it also established a new standard for landscape art, shifting the focus from the idealized to the real, from the static to the dynamic. Hiroshige had found a way to make the landscape speak, to give it a voice that resonated with the common people of Edo. He showed them their own world, with all its beauty and its hardship, and in doing so, he validated their experience. He was not painting for the aristocracy; he was painting for the traveler, the merchant, the peasant, the person who walked the road and felt the rain.

Following the success of the Tōkaidō, Hiroshige embarked on a series of other landscape projects, each exploring different aspects of the Japanese experience. One Hundred Famous Views of Edo, published between 1856 and 1858, was his final major series and perhaps his most innovative. Unlike the linear narrative of the Tokaido, this series was a loose collection of vignettes, capturing the city of Edo in all its seasonal glory. He depicted the cherry blossoms of Ueno, the snow of Asakusa, the summer festivals of the Sumida River, and the winter quiet of the temples. He played with perspective, using unusual angles and cropping to create a sense of immediacy and intimacy. He showed the city not as a grand monument, but as a living, breathing entity, full of small, personal moments. He captured the smoke of the incense burners, the steam of the bathhouses, the laughter of the children, the silence of the snow. He showed the city in all its moods, from the festive energy of the New Year to the melancholic stillness of a winter night.

The final years of Hiroshige's life were marked by personal tragedy and the looming shadow of a changing world. His wife died in 1854, and the Great Ansei Earthquake of the same year devastated Edo, destroying many of his works and the homes of his neighbors. The city he loved was being reshaped by disaster and by the growing pressure of foreign powers. The arrival of Commodore Perry in 1853 had forced Japan to open its borders, ending centuries of isolation. The Tokugawa shogunate, already weakened, was beginning to crumble, and the country was hurtling toward a new era of modernization and Westernization. Hiroshige witnessed these changes with a sense of profound sadness. He saw the old ways disappearing, the traditional landscapes being replaced by new structures, the old rhythms of life being disrupted by the new demands of a global economy. His later works reflect this sense of loss, a longing for a world that was slipping away.

He died in 1858, just two years before the Meiji Restoration formally ended the Tokugawa shogunate. He never saw the full extent of the transformation he had predicted, but his work served as a bridge between the old and the new. He left behind a body of work that was both a celebration of the Japanese landscape and a mourning of its passing. His prints were not just beautiful images; they were historical documents, records of a time and a place that no longer exists. They captured the spirit of the Edo period, the resilience of its people, and the enduring beauty of their world.

The influence of Hiroshige on the rest of the world was immediate and profound. When his prints were first shown in Europe in the mid-19th century, they caused a sensation. They were unlike anything the Western world had ever seen. The flattened perspective, the bold use of color, the focus on everyday life, the sense of atmosphere and mood—these were revolutionary concepts that challenged the traditional Western conventions of art. Artists like Vincent van Gogh, Claude Monet, and James McNeill Whistler were deeply influenced by his work. Van Gogh even copied several of Hiroshige's prints, trying to capture the same sense of light and color. The Japanese aesthetic, known as Japonisme, became a major force in the development of modern art, shaping the Impressionist and Post-Impressionist movements.

But to view Hiroshige solely through the lens of his influence on Western art is to miss the point. His primary audience was the people of Japan, and his primary goal was to capture the essence of their world. He was a chronicler of his time, a witness to the daily lives of the common people. He did not seek to elevate the mundane to the sacred; he sought to show the sacred in the mundane. He found beauty in the rain, in the mud, in the sweat of the laborer, in the quiet moment of a traveler resting by the roadside. He showed that the world was not just a place to be conquered or studied, but a place to be experienced, to be felt, to be loved.

In the end, Hiroshige's legacy is one of empathy. He looked at the world with a kind of tender curiosity, a desire to understand the lives of others. He did not judge; he observed. He did not preach; he showed. He captured the fleeting moments of life, the impermanence of existence, and the beauty that can be found in the most ordinary of places. He reminds us that art is not just about creating something beautiful; it is about connecting with the world around us, about seeing the world as it is, with all its flaws and all its wonders. He reminds us that the past is not dead; it is alive in the images we leave behind, in the stories we tell, in the memories we keep. And as we look at his prints today, we see not just a beautiful landscape, but a glimpse into the soul of a people, a moment in time that has been preserved for us to cherish and to learn from.

"The world is a floating world, and we are all travelers on its shores."

This is the lesson of Hiroshige. He teaches us to look closely, to pay attention, to find the extraordinary in the ordinary. He teaches us that even in the face of change, even in the face of loss, there is beauty to be found. He teaches us that the world is a place of wonder, and that it is our duty to witness it, to record it, to share it with others. His prints are a testament to the human spirit, to our capacity for wonder, for joy, for sorrow, for love. They are a reminder that we are all part of a larger story, a story that continues to unfold, a story that is written in the dust of the road, in the rain of the storm, in the light of the moon. And as we walk our own paths, we can look back to Hiroshige, to the one hundred views of Edo, to the fifty-three stations of the Tokaido, and find a sense of peace, a sense of connection, a sense of hope. For in the end, we are all travelers, and the road is long, and the rain is heavy, but the journey is worth it. The journey is the point. The journey is the art."
