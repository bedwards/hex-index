Paradox of tolerance

25 min read

Based on Wikipedia: Paradox of tolerance

In 1945, as the smoke cleared over a continent ravaged by fascism, Karl Popper, an Austrian-British philosopher, sat down to write a book that would challenge the very foundation of the liberal democracies he had fought to save. He was not writing about the mechanics of bombs or the strategies of generals, but about a far more subtle and insidious vulnerability: the idea that a society which is tolerant without limit will eventually be destroyed by the intolerant. This was the birth of the Paradox of Tolerance. Popper observed that if a society extends its tolerance even to those who are intolerant, if it refuses to stand up against movements that would dismantle the tolerant framework itself, then the tolerant will be destroyed, and with them, the very capacity for tolerance. This is not a theoretical game; it is a mathematical certainty of social dynamics, a warning that the moment we prioritize the principle of unlimited openness over the survival of the open society itself, we have already lost.

The concept first appeared in Popper's seminal work, The Open Society and Its Enemies, published in 1945. To understand the weight of this argument, one must look at the world that produced it. Popper had fled the rise of Nazism in Austria, watching as a society that prided itself on enlightenment and reason slowly capitulated to a movement dedicated to its erasure. The Nazis did not rise by being banned; they rose by being permitted to speak, to organize, and to spread their poison under the guise of free speech. They utilized the very freedoms of the Weimar Republic to dismantle the Republic itself. Popper's insight was a direct response to this historical trauma. He argued that the right to free speech is not absolute when that speech is used to incite violence or to deny the rights of others to exist. The paradox lies in the necessity of intolerance toward intolerance. If we are tolerant of the intolerant, we are, in effect, acting as their accomplices.

This is where the rubber meets the road in any discussion of free expression. The standard liberal defense of free speech is that it allows for the marketplace of ideas, where the best arguments will inevitably win out and the worst will be exposed and rejected. But Popper pointed out a fatal flaw in this logic: the intolerant do not play by the rules of the marketplace. They do not seek to debate; they seek to dominate. They do not want to be heard; they want to silence. When an intolerant group gains enough traction, they do not simply argue for their viewpoint; they begin to use force, violence, and coercion to suppress opposing voices. In this scenario, the tolerant society faces a dilemma. Should it allow the intolerant to use the freedom of speech to destroy the freedom of speech for everyone else? Popper's answer was stark and unambiguous: no.

"Unlimited tolerance must lead to the disappearance of tolerance. If we extend unlimited tolerance even to those who are intolerant, if we are not prepared to defend a tolerant society against the onslaught of the intolerant, then the tolerant will be destroyed, and tolerance with them."

This quote, often cited and frequently misunderstood, is not a call for authoritarianism. It is a call for self-defense. Popper was not suggesting that we should silence every disagreeable opinion or that we should ban every group we find offensive. He was drawing a line in the sand. The line is drawn at the point where speech ceases to be an argument and becomes an act of aggression. The line is drawn where the intolerant refuse to engage in rational debate and instead resort to the suppression of others. This distinction is crucial. It separates the defense of democracy from the defense of any particular ideology. The goal is not to protect a specific set of ideas, but to protect the framework that allows those ideas to be discussed.

The application of this principle has been a source of fierce debate in the decades since Popper wrote it. In the 1960s, during the height of the civil rights movement, the question of how to handle hate groups like the Ku Klux Klan was a matter of life and death. Should the state allow the Klan to march and spread their message of racial supremacy? The answer, in a tolerant society, is that they must be allowed to speak, but they must also be stopped from acting on their ideology. They cannot be allowed to intimidate, threaten, or kill. But where does the line end? When does speech become an act of violence? This is the central tension of the paradox. It is not a clean, mathematical boundary. It is a messy, human one, drawn in the sand of every generation.

Consider the case of free speech on college campuses in the United States. In the 1990s and 2000s, universities grappled with how to handle speakers who promoted hate speech. Should a university invite a notorious racist to speak, under the guise of open inquiry? Or should they deny them a platform? The paradox of tolerance suggests that denying a platform to those who would use it to incite violence is not a violation of free speech, but a necessary defense of the community's ability to function. Yet, the fear of censorship is so deeply ingrained in the American psyche that many institutions hesitate. They fear that once they start drawing lines, the lines will move, and soon, no one will be allowed to speak. This fear is not unfounded. History is littered with examples of governments using the pretext of fighting intolerance to silence legitimate dissent. The challenge is to find a way to protect the open society without becoming the very thing we seek to defeat.

The paradox also raises questions about the role of the state. Popper argued that the state has a duty to protect the open society. This means that the state must be willing to use its power to suppress those who would destroy it. But who decides what is intolerable? Who draws the line? In a democracy, this decision must be made through a rigorous, transparent process, not by the whims of a single leader or a small group of elites. The criteria must be clear: the intolerant must be those who refuse to engage in rational debate and who resort to violence or coercion. The state's role is to enforce these criteria, to ensure that the marketplace of ideas remains open, but not to the point where it is destroyed by those who would burn it down.

In the digital age, the paradox of tolerance has taken on new dimensions. The internet has democratized speech, giving a voice to billions of people who were previously silenced. But it has also given a voice to the intolerant, allowing them to organize, spread their message, and incite violence on a global scale. Social media platforms are now the gatekeepers of public discourse, and they face the same dilemma Popper described. Should they allow hate speech on their platforms, under the guise of free expression? Or should they ban it? The answer is not simple. Banning hate speech can be seen as a necessary defense of the open society, but it can also be seen as a form of censorship that undermines the very principles of free speech. The challenge is to find a balance, to protect the community without becoming the censor.

The paradox of tolerance is not just a philosophical puzzle; it is a practical guide for action. It reminds us that tolerance is not a passive state. It is an active commitment to the values of the open society. It requires us to be vigilant, to be willing to stand up for those who are threatened, and to be willing to use force, if necessary, to protect the freedom of all. It requires us to recognize that the intolerant are not just wrong; they are a threat to the very existence of the open society. And it requires us to be willing to make the hard choice to deny them the tools they need to destroy us.

"We should therefore claim, in the name of tolerance, the right not to tolerate the intolerant."

This right is not a license for tyranny. It is a defense mechanism. It is the recognition that the open society is fragile, that it can be destroyed from within, and that we must be willing to fight for its survival. The paradox of tolerance is a reminder that the price of freedom is eternal vigilance. It is a call to action, a demand that we do not let our commitment to tolerance blind us to the dangers that threaten it. In a world where the forces of intolerance are rising, where hate is spreading faster than ever before, the paradox of tolerance is more relevant than ever. It is a guide for how to navigate the complex terrain of free speech, how to defend the open society without becoming the enemy we seek to defeat.

The human cost of ignoring this paradox is incalculable. When we fail to act against intolerance, we allow the seeds of violence to take root. We allow the intolerant to gain power, to organize, and to strike. We allow the voices of the marginalized to be silenced, to be erased. The history of the 20th century is a testament to the cost of inaction. The Holocaust, the Rwandan genocide, the ethnic cleansing in the Balkans—all of these were made possible by a society that failed to recognize the danger of intolerance. They were made possible by a society that believed that tolerance was an absolute, that it must be extended even to those who would destroy it.

Popper's warning is not a call to arms, but a call to reason. It is a reminder that the open society is not a given. It is a choice, a commitment, a struggle.
