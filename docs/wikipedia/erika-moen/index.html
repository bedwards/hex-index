Erika Moen

11 min read

Based on Wikipedia: Erika Moen

In 2017, a Texas school district in the rural town of Palestine received a complaint from a parent who claimed a graphic novel was teaching children to be "demons." The target of this fury was not a controversial politician or a radical preacher, but a small, hand-drawn book about the mechanics of sex, the nuances of consent, and the messy reality of growing up. The book was Dear Pen Pal by Erika Moen, a cartoonist whose work had quietly been building a bridge between the clinical dryness of traditional sex ed and the actual, breathing experiences of young people. The complaint didn't just spark a local debate; it ignited a national flashpoint that would see Moen's work labeled as "demonic" by state officials, her book pulled from shelves, and her name shouted in legislative hearings as a symbol of cultural decay. To understand why a cartoon about talking to your pen pal became a battleground for the soul of American education, one must first understand the person drawing the lines, the ink on her fingers, and the specific, often terrifying, void she was trying to fill.

Erika Moen was not born into the world of graphic novels. She was born in 1981 in New Jersey, a child of the pre-internet, pre-social media era where information was gatekept by libraries, teachers, and parents who often didn't know the answers themselves. Her early artistic influences were the underground comix of the 1970s and 80s, a medium that had long been the repository for stories too taboo for mainstream publishing. But Moen's career didn't begin with a grand manifesto on sexual liberation. It began with a simple, desperate need for representation. In her early twenties, while working at a comic shop and creating webcomics, she realized that the existing landscape of sex education was fractured. On one side, there was "abstinence-only" curricula, a pedagogical approach that often relied on fear, misinformation, and the omission of basic biological facts. On the other side, there were clinical textbooks, sterile and devoid of the emotional context that makes human connection possible. Between these two poles lay a vast desert where millions of young people were left to guess, Google, or learn from peers who were just as confused as they were.

Moen decided to draw the map herself. Her first major foray into this space was Oh Joy Sex Toy, a webcomic that began in 2012. The premise was deceptively simple: Moen and her husband, Matthew Nolan, would review sex toys, but they did so with the warmth, humor, and vulnerability of a couple chatting over dinner. They didn't just rate the vibration or the material; they discussed the awkwardness of the first time, the logistics of cleaning up, the anxiety of buying the product, and the joy of discovering what made them feel good. It was a radical act of normalization. By treating sex toys with the same casual, respectful attention one might give a kitchen appliance, Moen stripped away the shame that had been weaponized against them for decades. The comic went viral, not because it was explicit, but because it was honest. It was the first time many readers saw their own internal monologues about intimacy reflected back at them in ink and pixels.

The success of Oh Joy Sex Toy paved the way for Moen's most controversial and impactful work: Dumb Sex Ed and the Drawn To Be series, but it was her graphic novel Dear Pen Pal that would bring her into the crosshairs of a culture war. Dear Pen Pal, published in 2015, is a story about a teenage girl named Emily who becomes pen pals with a mysterious person who turns out to be a boy named Sam. Through their letters, they discuss everything from their favorite bands to their fears about the future, but they also talk about bodies. They talk about puberty, about the confusion of menstruation, about the pressure of relationships, and about the importance of consent. The book is gentle. It is illustrated in soft lines and pastel colors. There are no graphic depictions of sexual acts, only the emotional and physical realities of being a teenager. It is a book that asks questions rather than demanding obedience.

Yet, in the landscape of American sex education, even gentle questions can be seen as acts of rebellion. The controversy surrounding Moen's work came to a head in the summer of 2017, in the Palestine Independent School District in Texas. A parent, whose child was in a 9th-grade health class, filed a formal complaint against a book the teacher had used as a supplementary resource. The book in question was Dumb Sex Ed by Erika Moen and Matthew Nolan, a collection of comics designed to explain sexual health in a way that was accessible to young adults. The parent alleged that the book was inappropriate, that it contained "obscene" material, and that it promoted a "demonic" worldview. The complaint was not just about the content of a book; it was about the authority to define what children were allowed to know about their own bodies.

The district's response was swift and severe. The book was removed from the classroom. A committee was formed to review the material, and the decision was made to ban it from the school library. The news of the ban traveled quickly, finding its way to conservative news outlets and social media echo chambers. Suddenly, Erika Moen was not just a cartoonist; she was a target. The narrative shifted from a local dispute over curriculum to a national symbol of the "radical left" infiltrating schools. The term "demonic" was repeated in headlines, a word chosen deliberately to evoke a sense of spiritual danger and moral corruption. It was a rhetorical move that bypassed any discussion of the actual content of the book and instead framed the entire concept of comprehensive sex education as an existential threat to the family and the faith.

Moen's response to the controversy was characteristic of her work: calm, factual, and deeply human. She did not retreat. She did not issue a defensive press release. Instead, she went on the record to explain what the book actually contained. She pointed out that the "obscene" material the parent complained about was, in reality, a comic panel explaining the difference between a tampon and a menstrual cup. She highlighted that the "demonic" worldview was simply the idea that young people deserve to know how their bodies work and how to protect themselves. She noted that the book had been vetted by health educators and had been used in classrooms across the country without incident for years. The ban in Palestine was an anomaly, a reaction to fear rather than a reflection of the book's actual merit.

The impact of the ban, however, extended far beyond the borders of Palestine, Texas. It highlighted a systemic failure in American education. In 2017, the United States was one of the few developed nations without a national standard for sex education. This lack of federal oversight meant that the curriculum in a classroom was entirely dependent on the whim of local school boards, the biases of individual parents, and the political climate of the state. In Texas, the state law mandated that sex education be "medically accurate," but it also required that abstinence be emphasized and that the curriculum not promote "homosexual conduct." This created a catch-22 for teachers and cartoonists like Moen. How do you teach "medically accurate" information about contraception and LGBTQ+ health in a system that legally requires you to prioritize abstinence and stigmatize homosexuality? The answer, for many, was to use resources like Moen's, which filled the gaps left by the official curriculum. When those resources were banned, the gaps remained, and the students remained vulnerable.

The controversy also shed light on the unique power of the graphic novel as a medium for education. Unlike a textbook, which can be dry and intimidating, or a lecture, which can be passive, a graphic novel demands engagement. The reader has to follow the narrative, identify with the characters, and process the information in a visual and emotional way. For young people, especially those who have been taught to feel ashamed of their bodies, this format can be a lifeline. It allows them to see themselves in the story, to realize that their confusion is normal, and that they are not alone. When a district bans a graphic novel, they are not just removing a book; they are removing a mirror. They are telling a child that their questions are invalid, their curiosity is dangerous, and their experience is something to be hidden in the dark.

Erika Moen's work continued to evolve in the years following the Texas ban. She published The Best of Erika Moen's Dumb Sex Ed, a collection that brought together her most popular webcomics into a single volume. She launched Drawn To Be, a zine series that focused on the intersection of sex, gender, and identity, featuring contributions from a diverse array of artists and writers. She became a sought-after speaker at conferences and universities, where she advocated for the inclusion of comics in the classroom. Her message was consistent: sex education is not just about biology; it is about empathy, communication, and respect. It is about teaching young people how to navigate the complex social landscape of relationships, how to recognize coercion, and how to advocate for their own boundaries.

The "demonic" accusation, while loud and frightening at the time, ultimately failed to silence Moen. Instead, it amplified her voice. The ban in Palestine became a rallying cry for advocates of comprehensive sex education across the country. It drew attention to the fact that the fight for accurate, inclusive education was not just a battle over policy, but a battle for the future of the next generation. It forced parents, teachers, and politicians to confront the uncomfortable question: what are we trying to protect our children from? The dangers of misinformation, the risk of unintended pregnancy, the trauma of sexual assault, or the discomfort of discussing sex in a public forum?

In the years since, the landscape has shifted, but the fundamental tension remains. The United States continues to grapple with the legacy of abstinence-only education, with studies showing that it has failed to reduce rates of teen pregnancy or sexually transmitted infections. Meanwhile, the demand for resources like Moen's books has never been higher. Parents are seeking out materials that are inclusive, medically accurate, and age-appropriate. Teachers are looking for tools that can help them navigate the difficult conversations that the official curriculum ignores. And young people are finding their own ways to access information, often through the very webcomics and zines that were once banned in school libraries.

Erika Moen's journey from a small-town cartoonist to a central figure in the culture war is a testament to the power of art to challenge the status quo. She did not set out to be a provocateur. She set out to be a friend to her readers, a guide in the dark. She drew the lines of human connection with a steady hand, refusing to let shame dictate the narrative. Her work reminds us that sex education is not a luxury; it is a necessity. It is a fundamental right of every young person to know their body, to understand their health, and to enter into relationships with confidence and care. When we ban books like Dear Pen Pal, we are not protecting our children. We are leaving them in the dark, hoping they can find their way without a map. And in a world where the stakes are so high, that is a gamble we cannot afford to take.

The story of Erika Moen is not just about one cartoonist and one banned book. It is about the broader struggle for truth in an age of misinformation. It is about the courage it takes to speak honestly about the human experience when the world tells you to stay silent. It is about the resilience of communities that come together to defend the rights of their children to learn, to grow, and to be whole. As the debate over sex education continues to rage in school boards and legislative halls across the nation, the ink on Moen's pages remains a beacon of clarity. It is a reminder that the most powerful tool we have in the fight for our children's future is not a law, a policy, or a slogan. It is the simple, radical act of telling the truth.

In the end, the "demonic" book was just a collection of comics about a girl and a boy writing letters. There were no demons. There were no secrets. There was only the messy, beautiful, complicated reality of being human. And perhaps, that was the most revolutionary thing of all.
