Catholicos of All Armenians

12 min read

Based on Wikipedia: Catholicos of All Armenians

In 1903, the Russian Tsar Nicholas II issued a decree that did more than confiscate property; it attempted to sever the spiritual spine of a nation. The edict ordered the nationalization of all assets belonging to the Armenian Apostolic Church, transferring control of its vast estates, schools, and treasuries to the Russian imperial administration. The goal was explicit: to dismantle the one institution that had kept Armenian identity alive for centuries in the absence of a state. The reaction was not a whisper of dissent but a roar of unified defiance that swept from the mountains of Nagorno-Karabakh to the diaspora in Constantinople. It was a moment that crystallized the unique nature of the Catholicos of All Armenians, a title that represents not merely a bishop, but the living embodiment of a people who have survived empires, genocides, and the erasure of their homeland by anchoring their existence to a single, unbroken line of spiritual leadership.

To understand the weight of this office, one must first strip away the modern assumptions of what a religious leader does. In the Western secular imagination, a church leader is often confined to the sanctuary, a moral guide who stays out of politics. The Catholicos of All Armenians has never had that luxury. For over seventeen hundred years, the position has functioned as a de facto head of state, a diplomat, a historian, and the primary guardian of the Armenian language and culture. When the Armenian Kingdom of Cilicia fell in the 14th century and the political structures of the nation collapsed, the Church did not retreat into the background; it stepped forward to fill the vacuum. The Catholicos became the sole representative of the Armenian people before foreign powers, the administrator of their internal affairs, and the keeper of their collective memory.

The title itself, "Catholicos of All Armenians," distinguishes the Supreme Patriarch and Catholicos of All Armenians from other Catholicos titles within the Armenian Church hierarchy, most notably the Catholicos of Great House of Cilicia. This distinction is not merely administrative; it is a testament to a history of survival through fragmentation. The seat of the Catholicos of All Armenians is the Mother See of Holy Etchmiadzin, located in the city of Vagharshapat, just a few miles from the capital of modern-day Armenia. Founded in 301 AD by Saint Gregory the Illuminator, this site is not just a cathedral; it is the cradle of the first nation in the world to adopt Christianity as its state religion. The spiritual lineage claimed by the Catholicos traces directly back to the apostles Thaddeus and Bartholomew, a connection that has provided a sense of divine continuity even when the physical nation was scattered across the globe.

The structure of the office is rooted in a system of election that reflects both the democratic aspirations of the Armenian people and the ancient traditions of the church. The Catholicos is not appointed by a monarch or a pope; he is elected by the National Ecclesiastical Assembly, a body composed of clergy and lay representatives from across the Armenian world. This includes delegates from the diaspora, ensuring that the voice of Armenians in Los Angeles, Beirut, or Paris carries the same weight as those in Yerevan. The election process is rigorous, often involving weeks of debate, prayer, and political maneuvering. The candidate must possess not only theological acumen but also the diplomatic fortitude to navigate the treacherous waters of geopolitics. In an era where the Armenian homeland has been squeezed between the military might of Turkey and the strategic interests of Russia, the election of a Catholicos is always a high-stakes event with implications that ripple far beyond the cathedral walls.

The human cost of the office cannot be overstated. The Catholicos has often been the first to stand in the way of destruction and the last to leave when the dust settles. During the Armenian Genocide of 1915, while the Ottoman government systematically deported and murdered 1.5 million Armenians, the Catholicos at the time, Gevorg V, was confined to the Mother See in Etchmiadzin, which was under the control of the Russian Empire. Yet, his role was not passive. From within the relative safety of the Russian border, he became the focal point of international appeals. He worked tirelessly to document the atrocities, sending dispatches to the Allied powers and coordinating with relief organizations. The tragedy was that the physical distance between the Catholicos and the victims was vast, and the bureaucratic barriers erected by warring empires were often insurmountable. The office, therefore, became a vessel of grief, a place where the prayers of the living were offered for the dead, and where the silence of the international community was met with a relentless, spiritual scream.

The relationship between the Catholicos and the state has been a complex dance of cooperation and conflict, a dynamic that has defined Armenian history for centuries. In the Soviet era, this relationship became a tool for both survival and suppression. The Soviet government recognized the Catholicos as a symbol of Armenian identity that could be co-opted to promote Soviet ideals, or suppressed if it threatened the party line. In the 1930s, under the pressure of Stalinist purges, the church was forced to operate under severe restrictions. Monasteries were closed, clergy were executed or sent to labor camps, and the Catholicos was reduced to a figurehead with little real power. Yet, the institution survived. The line of succession was never broken, even when the office was held by men who were effectively prisoners in their own cathedral. This resilience was not just a matter of stubbornness; it was a reflection of the deep-seated belief among Armenians that the church was the only entity that could guarantee their future.

The modern era has brought new challenges and new opportunities for the Catholicos. With the restoration of Armenian independence in 1991, the role of the Catholicos expanded once again. Karekin II, who has served as the Catholicos of All Armenians since 1999, has navigated a post-Soviet landscape marked by economic hardship, political instability, and the ongoing conflict over Nagorno-Karabakh. His tenure has been defined by a delicate balancing act between supporting the Armenian state and maintaining the church's independence. He has been a vocal advocate for peace and reconciliation, yet he has also been a fierce defender of Armenian rights in the face of aggression from Azerbaijan and Turkey. The human dimension of his work is evident in his visits to the front lines, where he has tended to the wounded and prayed with the families of the fallen. These are not ceremonial visits; they are acts of solidarity that remind the soldiers and civilians that they are not forgotten.

The conflict in Nagorno-Karabakh, which has flared repeatedly since the 1990s and culminated in a devastating war in 2020 and a final exodus in 2023, has placed the Catholicos at the center of a humanitarian crisis. When the Armenian population of Nagorno-Karabakh was forced to flee their ancestral homes in September 2023, the Catholicos was one of the first to speak out. He did not limit his statements to theological platitudes; he condemned the ethnic cleansing and called for international intervention. The loss of Nagorno-Karabakh was not just a territorial defeat; it was the loss of a historical heartland that had been inhabited by Armenians for millennia. The Catholicos's role in this crisis has been to provide a voice for the displaced, to document the cultural destruction, and to ensure that the memory of Karabakh remains a central part of the Armenian narrative. The human cost of this conflict is measured in the thousands of families who lost their homes, their livelihoods, and in some cases, their lives. The Catholicos has stood among the refugees in Yerevan, listening to their stories, validating their pain, and urging the world to look at the human reality behind the geopolitical headlines.

The authority of the Catholicos extends beyond the borders of Armenia. The Armenian diaspora, which numbers in the millions, looks to the Mother See in Etchmiadzin as the spiritual anchor of their identity. In cities like Paris, London, and Buenos Aires, the Catholicos's words are read in churches and discussed in community centers. This global reach gives the office a unique diplomatic weight. The Catholicos can mobilize the diaspora to advocate for recognition of the Armenian Genocide, to support humanitarian aid, and to lobby for political change. However, this global influence also comes with the burden of representing a fragmented people. The diaspora is not a monolith; it is a diverse group with varying political views and levels of connection to the homeland. The Catholicos must navigate these differences, seeking unity without erasing dissent. This is a difficult task, but it is essential for the survival of the Armenian nation in the 21st century.

The internal structure of the Catholicate is a testament to the complexity of the church's administration. The Mother See is not just a cathedral; it is a vast institution that includes seminaries, museums, libraries, and a publishing house. It is a center of learning and culture, where the Armenian language is preserved and taught. The Catholicos oversees a vast network of dioceses around the world, each led by a bishop who reports to him. This network is the backbone of the church's global presence. It is through this network that the church provides social services, education, and spiritual guidance to Armenians everywhere. The Catholicos is the head of this network, but he is also its servant. He is expected to visit every diocese, to hear the concerns of the local clergy and laity, and to ensure that the church remains relevant to the needs of the people.

The role of the Catholicos in the modern world is also defined by his relationship with other Christian denominations. In a globalized world, ecumenical dialogue is more important than ever. The Catholicos has been a key figure in the World Council of Churches and has engaged in dialogue with the Pope, the Patriarch of Constantinople, and other Christian leaders. These dialogues are not just about theology; they are about building bridges of understanding and cooperation in a world that is often divided by religious conflict. The Catholicos has also been a vocal advocate for interfaith dialogue, emphasizing the common values that unite different religions. This work is crucial for the Armenian Church, which has a long history of coexisting with other faiths, even in the face of persecution.

Yet, the office is not without its critics. Some within the Armenian community argue that the Catholicos has become too political, too close to the state, or too distant from the grassroots concerns of the people. Others argue that the church has not done enough to address social issues such as poverty, corruption, and inequality. These criticisms are valid and necessary. They reflect the ongoing struggle of the Armenian Church to remain relevant in a changing world. The Catholicos must be willing to listen to these criticisms and to adapt to the needs of the people. He must be a leader who is both firm in his convictions and open to new ideas. He must be a shepherd who is willing to get his hands dirty, to walk among his flock, and to share in their struggles.

The history of the Catholicos of All Armenians is a history of resilience. It is a story of a people who have faced extinction and have refused to disappear. It is a story of a spiritual leader who has stood in the gap between the past and the future, between the sacred and the secular, between the individual and the nation. The Catholicos is not just a religious figure; he is a symbol of the enduring spirit of the Armenian people. As the world continues to change, as new threats emerge and old wounds reopen, the role of the Catholicos will remain as crucial as ever. He will continue to be the voice of the voiceless, the defender of the weak, and the keeper of the flame that has burned for seventeen hundred years.

The future of the office depends on its ability to adapt to the challenges of the 21st century. The diaspora is growing, and the needs of the Armenian people are changing. The Catholicos must find new ways to engage with the younger generation, to address the issues of identity and belonging in a globalized world, and to build bridges with other communities. He must also be willing to confront the painful truths of the past and to work towards reconciliation. This is not an easy task, but it is a necessary one. The survival of the Armenian nation depends on it.

In the end, the Catholicos of All Armenians is more than a title. It is a covenant between the past and the future. It is a promise that as long as there is a Catholicos, there will be Armenians. And as long as there are Armenians, there will be a Catholicos. This promise has been kept for centuries, and it will be kept for centuries to come. The road ahead is uncertain, and the challenges are great. But the spirit of the Catholicos, and the people he represents, is unbreakable. They have survived the impossible, and they will survive whatever comes next. The story of the Catholicos is the story of Armenia itself: a story of faith, of suffering, of hope, and of the unyielding will to survive.
