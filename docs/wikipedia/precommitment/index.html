Precommitment

14 min read

Based on Wikipedia: Precommitment

In the winter of 1992, a woman named Odette Sansom, a British Special Operations Executive agent in occupied France during World War II, faced a choice that would define the rest of her life. She was captured by the Gestapo and subjected to brutal interrogation. Before her capture, she had been part of a network of agents who understood the fundamental flaw in human will: under extreme duress, the promise to remain silent often evaporates. To counter this, some agents engaged in a form of psychological precommitment, binding their future selves to a course of action they knew they might otherwise abandon. While Odette's specific tactics remain a matter of historical debate, the principle she navigated is one of the most robust and counterintuitive findings in behavioral economics and game theory. We are not the rational agents of classical economic theory, capable of coolly calculating our best interests in the moment. We are creatures of the present, plagued by a specific cognitive bias known as time inconsistency, where our preferences change as the moment of decision approaches. Precommitment is the mechanism by which we, or others, bind our future hands to force our future selves to act in accordance with our long-term goals, effectively turning a promise into a law.

The concept of precommitment is not merely a metaphor for self-discipline; it is a strategic tool used in everything from international diplomacy to addiction recovery, from financial planning to the design of public policy. At its core, it addresses a paradox that has confounded philosophers and economists for centuries: how can a person commit to a future action when they know that, once that future arrives, they will have an incentive to renege? The answer lies in altering the payoff structure of the future decision, making the cost of breaking the promise so high that it becomes irrational to do so. This is not about having more willpower; it is about engineering the environment so that willpower becomes irrelevant.

The Tyranny of the Present Self

To understand why precommitment is necessary, one must first dismantle the illusion of a unified self. In the span of a single day, an individual can harbor radically different desires. In the quiet of a morning, the "planner" self looks ahead to a year of health and financial security. This self values the long-term outcome above all else. But as evening falls, or as stress mounts, a different entity emerges: the "doer" self. This self is driven by immediate gratification, comfort, and the alleviation of present anxiety. The planner wants to save money; the doer wants to buy the new shoes. The planner wants to quit smoking; the doer craves the nicotine.

This conflict is not a moral failing but a structural feature of human cognition. Behavioral economists refer to this as hyperbolic discounting. Unlike the exponential discounting of standard economic models, where we devalue the future at a constant rate, we actually discount the immediate future at a terrifyingly steep rate. A reward available today is valued infinitely more than the same reward available tomorrow. But a reward available in one year and a day is not valued significantly more than a reward available in one year. The gap between "now" and "tomorrow" is a chasm that swallows our better judgment. As the moment of temptation approaches, the value of the immediate reward skyrockets, while the value of the long-term goal plummets. The planner becomes a stranger to the doer.

This dynamic is particularly dangerous in situations involving addiction, where the stakes are biological and psychological survival. Consider the case of an alcoholic who genuinely wishes to stop drinking. On a Monday morning, they can articulate a thousand reasons to remain sober. They can recite the damage done to their family, the risks to their health, the shame of their past actions. They form a resolution. But by Friday night, the stress of the workweek has worn them down. The immediate relief offered by a drink suddenly outweighs the abstract, distant horror of liver failure. The resolution of Monday is not honored by the person of Friday. The precommitment strategy is the bridge between these two states. It is the act of making the Friday-night temptation physically or financially impossible to act upon, or at least so costly that the doer self is forced to listen to the planner.

The Ulysses Contract: Ancient Wisdom, Modern Science

The archetype of precommitment is found in the oldest stories of human conflict. In Homer's Odyssey, the hero Ulysses (or Odysseus) knows that he must pass the island of the Sirens. The Sirens sing a song so enchanting that any sailor who hears it is compelled to steer their ship toward the rocks, leading to their death. Ulysses wants to hear the song but also wants to survive. He cannot trust his future self to resist the music once he is within earshot. Therefore, he devises a precommitment strategy. He orders his crew to plug their ears with beeswax, rendering them immune to the song. Then, he has himself tied tightly to the mast of the ship. He instructs his crew that no matter how much he screams, begs, or threatens them to release him, they must not untie him until they have passed the danger zone.

When the ship passes the Sirens, the song strikes Ulysses with the force of a physical blow. The desire to steer toward the music becomes an all-consuming obsession. He struggles against his bonds, screaming for release, his will completely overridden by the immediate temptation. But the bonds hold. The crew, acting as the enforcers of his past self's rationality, ignore his pleas. Ulysses survives not because he was stronger than the Sirens, but because he had the foresight to make himself weaker than his own future desire. He voluntarily surrendered his freedom of action to preserve his life.

This narrative is not just a myth; it is a precise description of a psychological phenomenon that modern researchers call the "Ulysses Contract." In contemporary terms, this contract is any agreement where an individual restricts their future options to ensure they adhere to a long-term plan. The key insight is that the individual must anticipate their own future weakness. They must admit, "I will not be able to say no later, so I must say no for me now." This is a profound admission of fallibility. It requires a level of self-knowledge that many people lack, leading them to rely on willpower alone—a strategy that statistical evidence suggests is destined to fail.

The application of Ulysses contracts has moved far beyond mythology into the realm of institutional design. In the 19th century, the British Navy used a form of precommitment to prevent sailors from going on shore leave and deserting. By locking sailors into long-term contracts with severe penalties for abandonment, they bound the sailors' future actions. While this is a coercive form of precommitment, the voluntary version is equally powerful. In the 20th century, the rise of addiction treatment programs introduced the concept of the "commitment contract." Programs like Alcoholics Anonymous rely on the public declaration of sobriety, which acts as a social precommitment. The fear of losing face in front of the group raises the cost of relapse, binding the individual to their goal.

The Economics of Self-Control

As the field of behavioral economics matured in the late 20th century, precommitment moved from a psychological curiosity to a central pillar of economic theory. Economists like Thomas Schelling, a Nobel laureate, formalized these ideas, arguing that self-control is essentially a game played between different versions of the self. In his seminal work, Schelling described how individuals often act as their own negotiators, using threats and promises to manage their internal conflicts. He noted that the most effective way to win this internal game is to change the rules of the game itself.

One of the most striking examples of economic precommitment is the "commitment savings account." Traditional savings accounts allow for easy withdrawal, which exploits the hyperbolic discounting of the doer self. If an individual has \$1,000 in a checking account, the temptation to spend it on a weekend getaway is high. The planner self might have intended to save that money for a down payment on a house, but the doer self sees an immediate vacation. A commitment savings account, such as those offered by the Bank of Ghana's "Save-to-Save" program or the US-based "StickK" platform, removes this option. These accounts often impose a penalty for early withdrawal that is significantly higher than the interest earned, or they simply do not allow withdrawals until a specific date. By removing the option to spend, the account forces the future self to comply with the past self's plan.

The efficacy of these mechanisms has been demonstrated in numerous field experiments. In a study conducted in the Philippines, researchers offered bank accounts with withdrawal restrictions to a group of savers. Those who chose the restricted account saved significantly more over the following year than those who chose a standard account. The restriction was not a barrier to the planner self; it was a shield against the doer self. The study revealed that the mere presence of the option to restrict one's future actions increased the likelihood of long-term savings, even among those who did not immediately choose the restriction. The knowledge that such a tool existed changed their behavior.

This logic extends to the corporate and political spheres. Governments often use precommitment to signal credibility to international partners. If a country wants to convince its neighbors that it will not attack, it might sign a treaty with severe economic sanctions for violation. By binding itself to a future penalty, it makes the threat of aggression less credible. This is why nuclear disarmament treaties often include intrusive inspection regimes and automatic penalties. The signatories are not just making a promise; they are creating a mechanism that makes breaking the promise irrational. The cost of cheating becomes so high that the only rational choice is compliance. This is precommitment as a tool of statecraft.

However, the application of precommitment in the public sphere is fraught with ethical complexity. When does a beneficial Ulysses contract become a tool of oppression? The history of mental health treatment provides a harrowing example. For decades, involuntary commitment was used to bind individuals with mental illnesses to treatment, often against their will. The rationale was that the patient's "present self" was too ill to make rational decisions about their future. While the intent was to save lives, the reality was often a violation of fundamental human rights. The line between protecting a person from their own worst impulses and stripping them of their autonomy is thin and easily crossed. Ethical precommitment must be voluntary and reversible, with the individual retaining the ability to withdraw their consent when their mental state improves. The danger lies in the paternalism of assuming that the state or the doctor knows better than the individual, even when the individual is acting irrationally.

The Digital Age of Precommitment

The advent of the digital age has revolutionized the landscape of precommitment, turning it into a ubiquitous feature of modern life. In the 21st century, the Ulysses contract is no longer a myth or a manual process; it is an algorithm. Social media platforms, online retailers, and entertainment services are designed to exploit our time inconsistency, offering an endless stream of immediate gratification that undermines our long-term goals. In response, a new industry of "digital precommitment" tools has emerged.

Apps like Freedom, Cold Turkey, and SelfControl allow users to block access to distracting websites and applications for set periods. These are digital versions of Odysseus having his crew plug their ears. The user, acting as the planner, sets a timer and locks the device. Once the timer starts, the doer self cannot access the distraction, no matter how much they beg. The code enforces the contract. This is particularly effective for students, professionals, and anyone struggling with digital addiction. The barrier is not willpower; it is the software code.

But the digital age has also given rise to a more insidious form of precommitment: the algorithmic prediction of our behavior. Tech companies use vast amounts of data to anticipate our future desires and present us with the immediate gratification we are about to crave before we even realize we want it. This is precommitment in reverse. Instead of us binding our future selves to a goal, these companies are binding our future selves to their business model. They exploit our hyperbolic discounting by ensuring that the next click, the next video, and the next purchase are always one step ahead of our rational judgment. The result is a society where the planner self is constantly overwhelmed by a doer self that has been engineered by an algorithm to be insatiable.

The implications for democracy and social cohesion are profound. If precommitment is necessary for individual self-control, it may also be necessary for societal stability. A democracy relies on citizens who can delay gratification, who can vote for policies that may have short-term costs but long-term benefits, such as climate change mitigation or infrastructure investment. But if the digital environment is designed to maximize immediate engagement and consumption, it erodes the capacity for long-term thinking. The challenge for the future may not be to find better ways to commit, but to create environments where commitment is possible.

The Paradox of Freedom

There is a fundamental paradox at the heart of precommitment: to be truly free, we must sometimes limit our freedom. The ability to choose anything at any moment is not the ultimate expression of liberty; it is often the source of our enslavement to our impulses. By voluntarily restricting our options, we gain the freedom to achieve our goals. This is the lesson of Ulysses, the lesson of the savings account, and the lesson of the commitment contract. We are not free when we are at the mercy of our immediate desires. We are free when we have the power to shape our future selves.

This insight challenges the traditional libertarian view that more choice is always better. In a world of infinite options, the doer self is constantly tempted, and the planner self is constantly defeated. Precommitment is the antidote. It is the recognition that we are not omniscient, omnipotent beings, but fragile creatures who need help to navigate the treacherous waters of human desire. It is a humble admission that we cannot trust our future selves to do the right thing without a little help from our past selves.

The story of precommitment is a story of human ingenuity. It is the story of how we have learned to outsmart our own brains, to build bridges between our conflicting desires, and to create structures that support our best intentions. From the ancient myths of Odysseus to the digital algorithms of the 21st century, the struggle remains the same. The temptation to abandon our long-term goals for immediate pleasure is as strong today as it was in the age of sail. But so is our ability to bind ourselves to a better path. The question is not whether we need precommitment, but whether we have the wisdom to use it.

In the end, precommitment is not about control; it is about liberation. It is the tool that allows us to break the cycle of regret, to stop fighting ourselves, and to finally become the people we want to be. It is the realization that the most powerful force in the universe is not the will of the doer, but the foresight of the planner. And in a world that constantly pulls us toward the immediate, the planner is the only one who can see the horizon.

The history of precommitment is a testament to the resilience of the human spirit. It shows that even when we are at our weakest, even when our impulses are strongest, we have the capacity to design a better future. We can tie ourselves to the mast. We can lock the door. We can sign the contract. And in doing so, we reclaim our freedom. The battle for self-control is not won by fighting harder, but by thinking smarter. It is won by understanding that the only way to save our future selves is to bind them today.

The lesson is clear: if you want to change your life, you cannot wait for the future. You must act now to make the future inevitable. The Ulysses contract is not a restriction; it is a promise kept to the person you hope to become. And in a world of infinite distractions, that promise is the most valuable thing we have.
