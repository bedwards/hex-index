Mary Prince

14 min read

Based on Wikipedia: Mary Prince

In the winter of 1828, a thirty-six-year-old woman stood in the London office of the Anti-Slavery Society, her hands trembling not from the cold but from the sheer weight of a truth she had been forbidden to speak. Mary Prince, a woman born in Bermuda who had spent her life under the lash of plantation owners in the Caribbean, declared that she was not property. She was a person. And she was ready to tell the world exactly what it meant to be treated as chattel in the British Empire. Her story, dictated to the abolitionist Thomas Pringle and transcribed by Susanna Strickland, would become The History of Mary Prince, a West Indian Slave, the first narrative written by a Black woman in Britain. It was a document that did more than inform; it shattered the comfortable silence of a nation that claimed to value liberty while profiting from the brutal subjugation of millions. To understand the magnitude of her contribution, one must first understand the machinery of the plantation system that had tried to erase her humanity, and the legal labyrinth she had to navigate just to claim her own voice.

Mary Prince was born in 1788 on the island of Bermuda, the youngest of seven children in an enslaved family. Her father was a carpenter, and her mother worked as a domestic servant. Unlike the vast sugar plantations of Jamaica or Barbados, Bermuda's economy was driven by salt raking, timber, and maritime trade, yet the brutality of slavery remained absolute. There was no legal distinction between the field hand and the house servant in terms of ownership; both were assets to be bought, sold, and inherited. Mary's early life was marked by the constant instability of her status. She was separated from her family at various points, sold to different owners, and subjected to the whims of masters who viewed her not as a daughter or a sister, but as a commodity.

At the age of twelve, she was purchased by the Williams family. It was here that the physical and psychological toll of slavery began to etch itself permanently into her life. She was forced to work in the salt ponds, a labor so punishing that it was often described as a death sentence for the enslaved. The work required wading through brine that burned the skin raw, bending under the scorching sun, and carrying heavy loads of salt crystals until one's back gave out. The heat was relentless, and the water was corrosive. Mary later recounted that the salt would eat away at her skin, leaving open sores that festered in the humidity. There was no respite, no protection from the elements, and no mercy for the weak. If a worker fell behind, the whip was the immediate correction. The salt ponds were not merely workplaces; they were laboratories of endurance where human bodies were tested to their breaking point.

The Williams family eventually moved to Antigua, bringing Mary with them. In Antigua, the plantation economy was even more rigid, and the violence more systemic. She was subjected to the arbitrary cruelty of Mr. and Mrs. Williams. Mr. Williams was known for his temper, often striking Mary for the slightest perceived infraction. Mrs. Williams, in a display of domestic tyranny that was terrifyingly common, would beat Mary with a heavy riding crop or a stick, leaving her body bruised and bleeding. These were not isolated incidents of a "bad master" gone rogue; they were the standard operating procedure of the institution. The law provided no recourse for Mary. If she ran away, she would be hunted down by dogs and returned to be punished. If she complained, she would be beaten into silence.

It was in this environment of total powerlessness that Mary met her future husband, Daniel James. They were both enslaved, and their marriage had no legal standing. They could not sign a contract, own property, or even guarantee that they would not be separated by a sale. Yet, they found solace in each other. They had three children together, all of whom were born into slavery and belonged to their owners. The constant fear of separation hung over their union like a dark cloud. To be a slave mother in the Caribbean was to live with the perpetual terror of seeing your child sold away, perhaps to a different island, perhaps to a brutal estate where survival was unlikely. Mary's love for her children was a source of strength, but it was also a source of profound vulnerability. Every day she lived was a gamble that her family would remain intact.

In 1826, the opportunity for escape presented itself in the form of Captain John Adams, a British naval officer who traveled to Antigua. Adams purchased Mary, intending to take her to England. The law at the time was ambiguous regarding slavery in Britain itself. While the 1772 case of Somerset v Stewart had established that slavery was not supported by English common law, it did not explicitly abolish the institution. Many slave owners believed that once they stepped on British soil, they retained their rights over their human property. Adams, however, may have been motivated by a mix of personal affection and a desire to free Mary, or perhaps he simply saw her as a domestic servant. Regardless of his intent, the journey to England marked a turning point. When the ship docked in London, Mary was technically free, or at least, she was in a place where the master's whip held no legal authority.

But freedom was not immediate, nor was it simple. Captain Adams soon sold her to another owner, the Reverend John Adams, who took her back to the Caribbean. This betrayal shattered any hope Mary had of a permanent escape. She was returned to the Williams family in Antigua, where she faced even harsher treatment as punishment for her attempted flight. The Williamses, furious at the loss of their investment, subjected her to torture. They beat her, starved her, and chained her. The psychological trauma of being dragged back into the abyss after tasting the air of liberty was devastating. Yet, Mary did not break. She began to plot her next move, this time with a more concrete plan. She wrote to the Anti-Slavery Society in London, a desperate letter that would eventually change the course of history.

By 1828, Mary had managed to secure a passage back to England, this time on a ship where she was hidden or treated as a free woman. When she arrived in London, she was a fugitive. Her owners claimed she was their property and demanded her return. The legal battle that ensued was a microcosm of the larger struggle against slavery. Mary found herself in the office of the Anti-Slavery Society, surrounded by men who were eager to hear her story but hesitant to use her as a weapon. They knew that her narrative could be a powerful tool, but they also feared the backlash from the West India Interest, the powerful lobby of plantation owners who dominated British politics. They worried that her testimony might be too graphic, too raw, and too disturbing for the Victorian sensibility. They wanted a sanitized version, a story that highlighted the cruelty of slavery without indicting the entire system.

Mary refused to be silenced or edited. She dictated her life story with a ferocity that shocked her listeners. She did not shy away from the details of her abuse. She spoke of the salt ponds, the whippings, the sexual violence, and the heart-wrenching separations from her family. She described the moment she was forced to watch her husband be sold, the moment she was beaten until she was half-dead, and the moment she was told that her children were not hers to keep. Her voice was not the gentle, submissive tone the abolitionists might have preferred; it was angry, indignant, and unflinching. She demanded that her story be told in her own words, with all its horror and all its humanity.

The result was The History of Mary Prince, a West Indian Slave, published in 1831. The book was a sensation. It was the first biography of a Black woman to be published in Britain, and it was a bestseller. The public was captivated by her story, but they were also horrified. For many readers, Mary's narrative was the first time they had encountered the reality of slavery from the perspective of the enslaved. The abolitionist movement had relied on statistics, legal arguments, and moral appeals, but Mary's story was visceral. It forced readers to confront the human cost of the sugar they ate, the cotton they wore, and the coffee they drank. It was a direct challenge to the complacency of the British public.

The reaction from the slave owners and their supporters was swift and vicious. They launched a smear campaign against Mary, accusing her of lying and of being a tool of the abolitionists. They claimed that she was a prostitute, that she was mentally unstable, and that her story was a fabrication. They even published a pamphlet titled The History of Mary Prince, a West Indian Slave, Reconsidered, which sought to discredit her testimony. But the damage was done. The truth of her story had already taken root in the public consciousness. The sheer specificity of her account—the names of the people who abused her, the dates of the events, the details of the punishments—made it impossible to dismiss as fiction.

Mary's narrative played a crucial role in the passage of the Slavery Abolition Act of 1833, which finally abolished slavery throughout the British Empire. While the act included a provision for "apprenticeship" that delayed full freedom for several years, it marked a turning point in the history of the institution. Mary Prince's voice was one of many that contributed to this victory, but hers was unique in its power and its origin. She was not a white abolitionist speaking on behalf of the enslaved; she was an enslaved woman speaking for herself. She had dared to claim her own humanity in a world that had tried to deny it.

After the publication of her book, Mary's life was not easy. She remained in a state of legal limbo, her freedom still contested by her former owners. She lived in poverty, struggling to make a living in a society that was still deeply racist. She worked as a domestic servant, but she was often treated with suspicion and disdain. The fame she had achieved had not translated into financial security. Yet, she remained a symbol of resistance. She continued to speak out, to attend meetings, and to support the abolitionist cause. Her courage inspired others to find their own voices.

The legacy of Mary Prince is profound. She demonstrated that the power of the enslaved lay not in their physical strength, but in their ability to tell their stories. By breaking the silence, she exposed the moral bankruptcy of slavery and forced the world to confront its own complicity. Her narrative was not just a personal account; it was a political act. It challenged the legal and social structures that upheld the institution of slavery and paved the way for future generations of activists and writers.

Today, Mary Prince is remembered as a pioneer of Black literature and a hero of the abolitionist movement. Her story is a testament to the resilience of the human spirit and the power of truth. She stood up to a system that sought to crush her, and she refused to be silenced. In doing so, she helped to bring down the walls of slavery and open the door to freedom for millions. Her life was a struggle, but her legacy is a victory. She proved that even in the darkest of times, the human voice can be heard, and that the truth, no matter how painful, is the most powerful weapon of all.

The impact of The History of Mary Prince extends far beyond its immediate historical context. It laid the groundwork for the development of the slave narrative as a literary genre, influencing writers such as Frederick Douglass, Harriet Jacobs, and Olaudah Equiano. It also provided a model for the use of personal testimony in social justice movements, demonstrating the power of individual stories to effect change. Mary Prince's voice was a catalyst for the abolitionist movement, and her legacy continues to resonate in the fight against racism and oppression today.

Mary Prince died in 1833, just a few months after the publication of the Slavery Abolition Act. The exact circumstances of her death are unknown, but it is likely that she died in poverty, as she had lived much of her life. Yet, her death did not diminish her impact. Her story lived on, and her legacy continued to inspire future generations. She had achieved what few had before her: she had used her own voice to challenge the system that had enslaved her, and she had won.
