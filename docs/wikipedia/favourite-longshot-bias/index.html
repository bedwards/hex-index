Favourite-longshot bias

13 min read

Based on Wikipedia: Favourite-longshot bias

In the smoky, neon-lit backrooms of racetracks and the sterile, algorithmic dashboards of modern sportsbooks, a predictable mathematical error has persisted for over a century. It is a phenomenon where bettors systematically overvalue the chances of unlikely winners and undervalue the favorites, creating a structural inefficiency that bookmakers and markets exploit with ruthless consistency. This is the favourite-longshot bias, a quirk of human psychology that has defied the efficient market hypothesis and turned gambling into a study of cognitive failure.

The bias is deceptively simple to describe but complex to unravel. In a fair market, the odds offered on a horse or a team should reflect the true probability of that outcome, adjusted only for the house's profit margin, known as the overround. If a horse has a 50% chance of winning, the fair odds should be evens (2.0). If it has a 10% chance, the fair odds should be 9-to-1. However, empirical data from racetracks dating back to the 1930s shows a consistent distortion: bettors are willing to accept lower payouts on favorites than their true probability warrants, while they demand—and often accept—drastically higher payouts on longshots than the odds suggest is fair. The favorite is underbet, and the longshot is overbet.

This is not a minor statistical anomaly; it is a fundamental flaw in how humans process risk and reward. When a bettor looks at a 100-1 longshot, they do not see a 1% chance of winning. They see the dream of a life-changing payout. Conversely, when they look at a 1-5 favorite, they see a near-certainty, and the meager return feels insufficient to justify the risk, leading them to shy away or bet on the underdog instead. The result is a market where the implied probabilities derived from betting odds do not sum to 100% in a logical distribution but instead skew heavily toward the extremes.

The Historical Roots of the Distortion

The first rigorous documentation of this bias came in the mid-20th century, a time when American racetracks were the primary laboratories for gambling research. In 1952, researchers began to notice that the return on investment for betting on favorites was consistently higher than betting on longshots. But it was the seminal work of Harry Markowitz and later, more specifically, the analysis by Ali in 1977, that cemented the concept in economic literature. They found that for every dollar wagered on a favorite, the track kept a smaller percentage than for every dollar wagered on a longshot.

The data was stark. In a typical race, a bettor who consistently wagered on the top two or three favorites might break even or even turn a small profit after the track's take. However, a bettor who consistently wagered on horses with 20-1 odds or higher would lose a significant portion of their bankroll over time. The bias was not random; it was a structural feature of the market. The track, acting as the market maker, adjusted the odds to balance the books, but the human element—the crowd's irrational desire for the longshot—forced the odds to drift further from reality than pure probability would dictate.

This phenomenon was not limited to horse racing. It migrated quickly to other sports betting markets. In the 1980s and 1990s, analysts applied the same logic to NFL point spreads and tennis matches. The pattern held. Whether it was the Super Bowl or the Grand Slam, the bettors' collective psychology remained constant. They loved the underdog. They feared the favorite. And in doing so, they left money on the table, a resource that savvy bookmakers and professional bettors harvested with surgical precision.

The bias is often attributed to the "lottery effect," a psychological propensity where individuals prefer a small chance of a large gain over a high probability of a small gain, even when the expected value is lower. This is the same psychological engine that drives the massive jackpots in state lotteries. People buy lottery tickets not because the odds are good, but because the potential outcome is transformative. In the racetrack, the longshot represents that same transformative hope. The favorite, with its boring, incremental return, fails to capture the imagination, despite being the statistically safer play.

The Mechanics of the Market

To understand why the bias persists, one must look at the mechanics of the pari-mutuel system, the dominant betting model in horse racing and many other sports. In a pari-mutuel system, all bets of a particular type are placed together in a pool. The house takes a fixed percentage (the takeout), and the remainder is divided among the winners. The odds are not set in advance by the bookmaker; they are determined by the relative amount of money wagered on each outcome.

This creates a feedback loop. As more people bet on a longshot, the odds shorten, but because the initial pool for longshots is often small, even a moderate amount of money can drastically change the perceived probability. However, the bias suggests that the volume of bets on longshots is disproportionately high relative to their true win probability. This means the pool for longshots is inflated by money that is effectively "wasted" on low-probability outcomes, driving the payout down below what it should be if the market were efficient.

Conversely, the pool for favorites is often smaller than it should be. Bettors, seeking the thrill of the longshot, under-invest in the favorite. This scarcity of money on the favorite means that when the favorite does win, the payout is higher than it would be in a perfectly efficient market. The result is a positive expected value for the favorite bettor and a negative expected value for the longshot bettor.

Consider a hypothetical race with two horses: Horse A, a heavy favorite with a 75% chance of winning, and Horse B, a longshot with a 25% chance. In an efficient market, the odds might be 1.33 to 1 for Horse A and 3.0 to 1 for Horse B. If the bias kicks in, bettors might flock to Horse B, driving its odds down to 2.5 to 1, while ignoring Horse A, leaving its odds at 1.5 to 1. A sharp bettor who recognizes this can see that Horse A is now offering a value bet, while Horse B is a losing proposition in the long run.

The magnitude of the bias varies by sport, track, and even the time of day. In the United States, the bias has been found to be more pronounced in races with larger fields, where the uncertainty is higher and the allure of the longshot is stronger. In Europe, where fixed-odds betting is more common, the bias still exists but is sometimes mitigated by professional bookmakers who adjust their margins more aggressively. Yet, the core human element remains. No matter how advanced the algorithms, no matter how many data points are fed into the model, the human desire to back the underdog persists.

The Psychology of the Underdog

Why do humans fall for this trap? The answer lies deep in the cognitive architecture of the human brain. Behavioral economists have identified several mechanisms that contribute to the favourite-longshot bias. The most prominent is the availability heuristic. People tend to overestimate the probability of events that are easily recalled or vivid in their minds. A longshot victory is a dramatic, memorable event. It is the story that gets told at the bar, the headline that reads "Miracle at the Track." A favorite winning is routine, unremarkable, and easily forgotten.

Because longshot wins are more memorable, bettors incorrectly assume they happen more often than they actually do. They recall the one time they hit a 50-1 shot and forget the hundred times they bet on one and lost. This selective memory creates a distorted view of reality. The brain constructs a narrative where the underdog has a fighting chance, a narrative that overrides the cold, hard mathematics of probability.

Another factor is the utility of wealth. For many bettors, the utility of a small win is linear. Winning $10 is twice as good as winning $5. But for a longshot bettor, the utility is exponential. Winning $1,000 on a $10 bet is not just 100 times better than winning $10; it is life-altering. The potential for a massive payout provides a psychological utility that far outweighs the negative utility of losing the small stake. This is why people will buy a ticket with a 1-in-100-million chance of winning a lottery; the dream is worth the cost of the ticket, even if the math says it is a bad investment.

There is also the element of social proof and narrative bias. In a crowd, the longshot is often the favorite topic of conversation. The story of the underdog is a compelling narrative, one that resonates with human experiences of struggle and triumph. Betting on a favorite feels like a dull, corporate decision. Betting on a longshot feels like participating in a story. The bettor becomes a character in the narrative, rooting for the improbable to happen. This emotional investment clouds judgment and leads to irrational betting behavior.

Furthermore, the illusion of control plays a significant role. Bettors often believe they have insider knowledge or a special insight that allows them to identify the longshot that will actually win. They convince themselves that the market is wrong, that the odds are too high, and that their analysis is superior. This overconfidence leads them to bet on longshots with a conviction that is not supported by the data. The bias is sustained by the belief that "this time is different," a mantra that has led to countless financial disasters throughout history.

The Implications for the Modern Bettor

The existence of the favourite-longshot bias has profound implications for anyone engaging in sports betting or financial markets. It serves as a powerful reminder that markets are not always efficient and that human psychology can create persistent anomalies. For the professional gambler, the bias is a source of profit. By systematically betting on favorites and avoiding longshots, a disciplined bettor can achieve a positive expected return over time. This strategy is not glamorous; it lacks the thrill of the longshot hit. But it is mathematically sound and has been proven to work.

However, for the casual bettor, the bias is a trap. It is a mechanism that ensures the house always wins, not just through the takeout, but through the structural inefficiencies created by the crowd. The casual bettor is the one who fuels the bias, pouring money into the longshot pool and driving the odds down, thereby reducing the potential payout for everyone else. They are the liquidity providers for the professional bettors who exploit the bias.

In the context of financial markets, the favourite-longshot bias has analogues in stock trading. Investors often overvalue high-growth, speculative stocks (the longshots) and undervalue stable, blue-chip companies (the favorites). This leads to bubbles in speculative sectors and undervaluation of established firms. The bias is a universal feature of human decision-making under uncertainty, appearing wherever people are asked to assign probabilities to uncertain outcomes.

The persistence of the bias also challenges the efficient market hypothesis (EMH), which suggests that asset prices reflect all available information. If the market were truly efficient, the odds would adjust instantly to reflect the true probabilities, and the bias would not exist. The fact that the bias has persisted for decades suggests that the market is not efficient, at least not in the weak form. It is driven by the collective psychology of the participants, not just by the flow of information.

The Future of the Bias

As technology advances and data becomes more abundant, one might expect the favourite-longshot bias to diminish. Algorithms can process vast amounts of historical data and calculate probabilities with a precision that the human brain cannot match. Online betting exchanges and prediction markets allow for more efficient price discovery, potentially reducing the impact of human irrationality.

However, the bias has proven remarkably resilient. Even in the age of high-frequency trading and sophisticated modeling, the human element remains. The desire for the longshot is deeply ingrained in our psychology. It is not a bug that can be patched out; it is a feature of the human condition. As long as people are willing to dream of the improbable, the bias will persist.

Moreover, the rise of new forms of betting, such as in-play betting and micro-betting, may even exacerbate the bias. The immediacy of these bets can heighten emotional responses, leading to even more impulsive and irrational decision-making. The speed of the market may outpace the ability of bettors to think rationally, creating new opportunities for the bias to take hold.

Ultimately, the favourite-longshot bias is more than just a gambling quirk. It is a window into the human mind. It reveals our tendency to overestimate the unlikely, to seek out the dramatic, and to prioritize the potential for a massive gain over the certainty of a small return. It is a reminder that in a world driven by data and algorithms, the irrationality of the human spirit is still the most powerful force of all.

The next time you stand before a betting board, whether at a racetrack or on a screen, pause and consider the odds. Are they reflecting the true probability of the event, or are they reflecting the collective dreams and fears of the crowd? The answer may not be in the numbers, but in the psychology of the people who placed them. The bias is a constant, a shadow that follows us through every market, every game, and every gamble. It is the price we pay for our capacity to hope against the odds.

"The market is a device for transferring money from the impatient to the patient." — Warren Buffett

While Buffett was speaking of investing, his words apply equally to the racetrack. The impatient bettor, seeking the thrill of the longshot, transfers their money to the patient professional who understands the bias and exploits it. The bias is the great equalizer, a force that reminds us that in the game of chance, the house always has an edge, but the sharpest edge is often the one we carry ourselves: our own minds.

The favourite-longshot bias is a testament to the enduring power of human nature. It is a flaw in the system, a crack in the foundation of the efficient market, and a beacon for those who know how to read the signs. It is a story of hope, of disappointment, and of the eternal struggle between reason and emotion. And as long as there are gamblers, there will be a bias. It is the human cost of the dream, the price of the underdog, and the mathematical reality that no amount of logic can fully extinguish the fire of the longshot.
