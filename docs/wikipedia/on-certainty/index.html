On Certainty

13 min read

Based on Wikipedia: On Certainty

In the final months of his life, Ludwig Wittgenstein sat in a small room in Cambridge, dictating notes that would dismantle the very foundation of how we know anything at all. These were not the grand metaphysical pronouncements of his earlier work, but a series of fragmented, often contradictory observations written between November 1949 and April 1951, just weeks before his death from prostate cancer. They were published posthumously in 1969 under the title On Certainty (Über Gewißheit), a text that reads less like a systematic treatise and more like a mind grappling with the limits of language while the body fails. For a reader emerging from the recent controversy surrounding Kathleen Stock's Do Not Go Gentle, a work that dissected the fragile boundaries between biological fact and social construction, Wittgenstein’s late notes offer a startlingly different, yet deeply related, map of the terrain. Where Stock’s critics and supporters alike often clash over the definition of reality, Wittgenstein asks a more fundamental question: What makes something real enough to be questioned in the first place?

The context of these writings is often misunderstood. Wittgenstein was not writing a new philosophy of skepticism. He was, in fact, writing against skepticism, specifically the G. E. Moore variety. In 1939, the philosopher G. E. Moore had famously attempted to refute skepticism by holding up his hands and declaring, "Here is one hand, and here is another," arguing that he knew this with absolute certainty. Wittgenstein found this approach not just unconvincing, but philosophically confused. He argued that Moore was treating a basic proposition of our "world-picture" as if it were an empirical fact that could be proven or disproven. The error, Wittgenstein suggested, was in thinking that knowledge requires a foundation of proof. In On Certainty, he proposes that the opposite is true: proof is only possible because we already stand on a foundation of things we do not question.

"If you tried to doubt everything you would not get as far as doubting anything. The game of doubting itself presupposes certainty."

This insight shifts the entire framework of epistemology. Imagine a game of chess. You cannot question the rules of the game while you are playing it; if you try to ask, "Can a knight really move in an L-shape?" while the game is in progress, you are not playing chess anymore. You have left the activity. Similarly, Wittgenstein argues that our language and our understanding of the world rest on a "scaffolding" of certainties that are not themselves justified by evidence. They are the hinges upon which the door of inquiry turns. If the hinges themselves are moved, the door falls, and the activity of knowing collapses. These certainties are not empirical truths we have tested and found reliable; they are the conditions that make testing possible.

Consider the statement, "The earth has existed for a long time." In a scientific context, we might try to verify this through geology, carbon dating, or astronomy. But for Wittgenstein, this proposition is not a hypothesis to be verified. It is part of the "riverbed" of our thought. To doubt it is not to be a clever skeptic; it is to cease participating in the shared form of life that allows us to speak of earth, time, or verification at all. If a person today claimed that the earth did not exist before they were born, or that the sun rises only when they look at it, we would not engage them in a debate about evidence. We would question their sanity or their grasp of language. This is not because we have superior evidence, but because their claim strikes at the bedrock of our language game.

This distinction is crucial for understanding the modern conflicts over truth and reality, such as those that erupted around Kathleen Stock’s work. When Stock argued that biological sex is a fixed, immutable category, her critics often responded by claiming she was denying a social reality or a lived experience. When she was accused of "heresy" against the new orthodoxy of gender identity, the debate quickly devolved into a battle over who had the authority to define the "facts." Wittgenstein would likely find both sides trapped in a category error. The question is not whether sex is a social construct or a biological fact; the question is what role the concept of "sex" plays in our language games. Is it a hinge proposition, a rule of the game, or an empirical claim subject to revision?

"It is not a matter of the proposition being true, but of the proposition having a special role in our language game."

In On Certainty, Wittgenstein develops the idea that certainty is a matter of action, not belief. We do not "believe" that our hands exist or that the world is real; we act as if these things are certain. When you reach for a cup of coffee, you do not hesitate to check if your hand is there. You do not calculate the probability of the cup's existence. Your action reveals your certainty. This is what Wittgenstein means when he says that certainty is "animal." It is pre-rational, a mode of being in the world that precedes our justifications. This is a radical departure from the Cartesian tradition, which sought to ground knowledge in indubitable belief. Wittgenstein suggests that the foundation is not a rock of belief, but a flow of action.

This perspective forces us to reconsider the nature of the controversies that dominate our public discourse. When people argue over whether something is "real" or "constructed," they are often arguing over which language game they are playing. If one person treats gender as a hinge proposition (a fixed biological reality) and another treats it as an empirical claim (a fluid social category), they are not merely disagreeing about facts; they are playing different games. The tragedy of such conflicts, as seen in the Weston-super-Mare geriatric home incident and the broader cultural wars, is that the participants often fail to recognize that they are operating on different foundational layers. They try to prove their hinge propositions, which is impossible, because hinge propositions are not the kind of thing that can be proven. They are the things that make proof possible.

Wittgenstein’s notes also touch on the historical and cultural contingency of these certainties. The "riverbed" of our thought is not eternal; it shifts over time. What was once a hinge proposition in one era becomes a hypothesis in another. For centuries, it was a hinge proposition that the earth was the center of the universe. We did not "prove" it; it was simply how the world was understood. As the riverbed shifted, the proposition became subject to verification and eventually was overturned. Wittgenstein does not tell us which propositions are permanent and which are fluid; he only tells us that some must be fixed for the game to work, and that these are not fixed because they are true, but because they are useful for the form of life we lead.

This brings us to the human cost of ignoring these distinctions. When we treat hinge propositions as empirical claims, we open ourselves to a paralysis of doubt. If we demand proof for everything, including the existence of the external world or the reliability of our senses, we end up in a state of radical skepticism where no action is possible. Conversely, when we treat empirical claims as hinge propositions, we create dogma that cannot be questioned. This is the danger of the current cultural climate, where certain social truths are elevated to the status of unassailable bedrock, and any attempt to question them is met with the charge of madness or malice. Wittgenstein warns us that this is a misunderstanding of how language works. The goal is not to find absolute certainty, but to understand the role that certainty plays in our lives.

"The difficulty is to realize the groundlessness of our believing."

The groundlessness of our believing does not mean that we are lost in a void. It means that our beliefs are anchored in our actions, our practices, and our shared history. They are not grounded in a metaphysical foundation, but in the way we live. This is a humbling realization. It means that our most cherished certainties are not universal truths, but local agreements. They are the rules of the game we are currently playing. If the game changes, the rules change. This does not make our truths less real; it makes them more fragile, and thus more precious. It requires us to be vigilant about how we use our language, and how we treat those who play by different rules.

In the context of the debate over Kathleen Stock, Wittgenstein’s insights offer a way out of the impasse. Instead of arguing over whether gender is biological or social, we might ask: What are we trying to achieve with these concepts? What language games are we playing? When Stock writes about the "geriatric home in Weston-super-Mare," she is not merely making a claim about facts; she is engaging in a specific form of life, one that prioritizes biological essentialism. Her critics are engaging in a different form of life, one that prioritizes social construction and lived experience. The conflict arises not because one side is right and the other wrong, but because they are trying to force their language game onto the other. Wittgenstein teaches us that we cannot prove our way out of this conflict. We can only understand the nature of the conflict and decide whether we are willing to change the game.

The essayistic nature of On Certainty reflects the fragmented, searching quality of Wittgenstein’s mind in his final days. There are no systematic arguments, no grand conclusions. Instead, there are aphorisms, questions, and images that illuminate the dark corners of our understanding. He writes of the "hinges" of the door, the "riverbed" of thought, the "animal" nature of certainty. These are not metaphors for something else; they are attempts to describe the texture of our relationship with the world. He is not telling us what to think; he is teaching us how to think about what we think.

"I want to say: The certainty of a proposition is not a matter of its truth, but of its role in our language game."

This role is not static. It evolves as our practices evolve. The certainty that the earth is flat was once a hinge proposition. It is now a historical curiosity. The certainty that gender is binary was once a hinge proposition for many. It is now a subject of intense debate. This does not mean that the truth has changed; it means that the game has changed. The question is no longer whether we can prove the old certainties, but whether we are willing to accept new ones. This is the work of philosophy: not to find the final answer, but to keep the conversation going, to keep the riverbed shifting, to keep the door open.

The human cost of rigidifying these certainties is high. When we refuse to recognize the contingency of our beliefs, we become intolerant of those who live by different rules. We see them not as playing a different game, but as breaking the rules of the only game. This leads to the kind of vitriol and polarization that characterizes modern discourse. We see it in the attacks on Stock, in the cancellation of voices that challenge the new orthodoxy, in the refusal to engage with opposing viewpoints. It is a failure of imagination, a refusal to see the world from another perspective. Wittgenstein’s On Certainty is a call to imagine that perspective, to recognize that our certainty is not a shield against doubt, but a condition of our ability to doubt at all.

In the end, On Certainty is not a book about certainty. It is a book about the limits of doubt. It shows us that doubt is not the default state of the human mind; certainty is. We are not born skeptics; we are born into a world of taken-for-granted truths. The philosopher’s job is not to tear down these truths, but to understand their function. To see where they come from, where they go, and what happens when they shift. This is a difficult task, one that requires humility and patience. It requires us to step back from our own certainties and look at them from the outside. It requires us to see that our "truths" are not the only truths, but the truths that work for us.

For the reader of Kathleen Stock, this is a vital lesson. The debate over her work is not just about gender; it is about the nature of truth itself. It is about whether we can live with uncertainty, or whether we must impose a rigid order on a chaotic world. Wittgenstein suggests that the latter is impossible. The world is too complex, too fluid, for any single system to capture it. Our best hope is to understand the limits of our systems, to recognize the hinges that hold them up, and to be willing to move them when the time comes. This is not a call to relativism, but to responsibility. It is a call to take our language games seriously, to recognize that they shape our reality, and to be careful about the worlds we build with them.

The final pages of On Certainty are a testament to the power of this approach. Wittgenstein does not resolve the problems he raises. He leaves them open, hanging in the air like questions that cannot be answered. But in doing so, he offers a way forward. He shows us that the search for certainty is not the search for a foundation, but the search for a way of life. It is the search for a form of life that allows us to live together, despite our differences, despite our doubts, despite the fragility of our truths. This is the gift of On Certainty: not an answer, but a question that keeps us thinking, keeps us talking, keeps us human.

The date of Wittgenstein’s death, April 29, 1951, marks the end of a life, but not the end of his thought. The notes he left behind continue to shape our understanding of knowledge, language, and reality. They remind us that certainty is not a given; it is a achievement, a practice, a way of being. They challenge us to question our assumptions, to see the limits of our language, and to be humble in the face of the unknown. In a world that is increasingly polarized, where certainty is weaponized and doubt is demonized, On Certainty offers a path to a more nuanced, more humane understanding of the world. It is a path that requires us to let go of our grip on the absolute, to embrace the fluidity of our truths, and to find our footing in the shared riverbed of human experience.

This is the legacy of Ludwig Wittgenstein. Not a system of philosophy, but a method of thinking. Not a set of answers, but a way of asking questions. And in the end, that is all we can ask for. A way to keep the conversation going, to keep the door open, to keep the riverbed shifting. Because in the end, that is what it means to be human. To live in a world of uncertainty, and to find our certainty in the act of living itself.
