Meituan

10 min read

Based on Wikipedia: Meituan

In the summer of 2026, the average resident of Beijing does not simply order dinner; they summon a logistics army. At any given moment, over seven million delivery riders, clad in the distinctive yellow or blue jackets of rival platforms, weave through the city's arterial roads, a living circuit board connecting millions of consumers to tens of millions of merchants. At the center of this hyper-efficient, data-driven ecosystem sits Meituan, a company that has transcended its origins as a simple group-buying site to become the operating system for daily life in China. To understand Meituan is to understand the fundamental shift in how hundreds of millions of Chinese citizens work, eat, and consume, a transformation that has turned a smartphone app into the most valuable utility in the world's second-largest economy.

The story of Meituan is not merely one of technological innovation but of brutal, Darwinian survival in a market defined by ferocious competition. Founded in 2010 by Wang Xing, a visionary entrepreneur who had previously failed in social networking and hotel booking but possessed an uncanny ability to read the shifting tides of the Chinese internet, the company began as a clone of the American Groupon. In its early days, Meituan was one of hundreds of "group-buying" platforms fighting for scraps of consumer attention. The market was fragmented, chaotic, and rife with fraud. Wang Xing, however, saw something others missed. While competitors were obsessed with the short-term frenzy of daily deals, Wang focused on the long-term infrastructure of local services. He recognized that the real value lay not in selling a discounted pizza, but in building the digital rails that would eventually carry every transaction from the kitchen to the doorstep.

The Great Consolidation

The path to dominance was paved with the bones of a thousand competitors. Between 2010 and 2015, the "thousand battles of O2O" (Online-to-Offline) raged across China's major cities. Capital poured in from venture giants like Tencent and Alibaba, fueling a subsidy war that burned billions of dollars. Meituan, backed by Tencent, was forced to fight on multiple fronts, not just against Dazhong Dianping, its eventual merger partner, but against Alibaba's Ele.me and a myriad of smaller local players. The strategy was simple yet devastating: outspend the competition on user subsidies and rider incentives until the others ran out of cash. It was a war of attrition that reshaped the urban landscape, forcing small merchants to digitize or perish.

The turning point came in October 2015, when Meituan merged with Dazhong Dianping in a deal valued at $9 billion, creating a behemoth that controlled over 80% of the local services market. This was not a peaceful marriage but a strategic necessity. Dazhong Dianping held the crown in user reviews and restaurant discovery, while Meituan dominated the transaction volume and delivery infrastructure. The merger created a monopoly that was too large to fail but too unwieldy to ignore. For the average user, the result was seamless integration. For the merchants, it was the realization that there was no alternative platform to host their business. The era of choice for small businesses effectively ended, replaced by the tyranny of the algorithm.

The Human Engine: Riders in the Rain

While the headquarters in Beijing's Haidian District glowed with the sterile light of white-collar innovation, the real work happened on the streets. Meituan's business model relies on a workforce that is invisible in the balance sheets but visible in every alleyway: the delivery riders. By 2026, the number of registered riders on the Meituan platform had swelled to over 10 million. These are not employees in the traditional sense; they are independent contractors, often migrants from rural provinces, working in a gig economy that demands superhuman endurance.

The rider's life is dictated by the algorithm. The system calculates the optimal route, the time to pick up the food, the time to navigate the building, and the time to deliver, all with a margin of error measured in seconds. A delay of three minutes can trigger a cascade of penalties, reducing the rider's future order allocation and, consequently, their income. The pressure is relentless. In the sweltering heat of a July afternoon or the biting wind of a January morning, riders race against the clock. They balance bags of hot oil and cold beer on electric scooters, often ignoring traffic laws because the algorithm does not account for the reality of a red light. The system optimizes for speed, not safety.

The human cost of this efficiency is high. Accidents are frequent, and the injuries range from minor scrapes to life-altering trauma. In 2021, a report revealed that Meituan's algorithm had been adjusted to reduce delivery times by a few minutes, a change that directly correlated with an increase in traffic accidents. The company faced a public relations storm, eventually promising to give riders more time, but the structural pressure remained. For the rider, the app is a boss that never sleeps, a digital overseer that tracks every movement. They are the physical manifestation of the platform's promise: instant gratification for the consumer, paid for by the time and safety of the worker.

The Expansion of the Super-App

Meituan's ambition was never limited to food delivery. Wang Xing's concept was to build a "Super-App" that could serve every need of the local consumer. Following the merger, Meituan aggressively expanded into new verticals. It entered the hotel and travel market, challenging Ctrip. It launched Meituan Bike, a bike-sharing service that flooded Chinese streets with millions of bicycles. It moved into grocery delivery, fresh produce, and even grocery retail. By 2020, Meituan had become the third-largest company in China by market capitalization, trailing only Alibaba and Tencent.

The strategy was cross-subsidization. Losses in one sector, such as bike-sharing, were offset by the massive profits generated in food delivery and hotel bookings. The goal was to capture every minute of the user's day. If a user opened Meituan to order lunch, the app would suggest a movie ticket, a hotel for a weekend getaway, or a grocery list for dinner. The data gathered from these interactions allowed Meituan to refine its recommendations, creating a feedback loop that made the platform increasingly indispensable. By 2026, the app had over 700 million annual active users. It was no longer just a tool for ordering food; it was a window into the local economy.

The Regulatory Storm

However, the scale of Meituan's dominance attracted the attention of the state. In the early 2020s, the Chinese government launched a sweeping crackdown on the tech sector, targeting monopolistic practices and labor rights. Meituan was not spared. In 2021, the company was fined 3.44 billion yuan for anti-competitive practices, specifically for forcing merchants to sign "exclusive deals" that prevented them from listing on rival platforms like Ele.me. This "choose one of two" policy was a staple of Meituan's growth strategy, but it drew the ire of regulators who viewed it as a threat to the health of the ecosystem.

The crackdown extended beyond antitrust. The government demanded changes to the treatment of delivery riders, pushing for social security benefits and better working conditions. Meituan was forced to restructure its relationships with its workforce, establishing a new insurance fund for riders and adjusting its algorithms to prioritize safety over speed. These changes increased operational costs significantly, squeezing the company's profit margins. The era of unchecked growth was over. The government had made it clear that the social stability of millions of riders and the fairness of the market took precedence over corporate profitability.

For Meituan, this was a painful but necessary evolution. The company had to learn to operate within a new set of rules, where the state was the ultimate arbiter of business strategy. The "wild west" days of the early 2010s were gone, replaced by a regulated environment where compliance was as important as innovation. The company's leadership had to balance the demands of shareholders with the mandates of the state, a delicate dance that defined the Chinese tech landscape in the 2020s.

The Future of Local Services

As of 2026, Meituan stands at a crossroads. The low-hanging fruit of growth has been picked. The market is saturated, and the regulatory environment is stringent. The company's focus has shifted from expansion to efficiency and profitability. It is investing heavily in automation, experimenting with drone delivery and autonomous robots to reduce its reliance on human riders. The goal is to create a logistics network that is not only faster but also safer and more cost-effective. However, the technology is still in its infancy, and the human element remains irreplaceable for the foreseeable future.

Meituan's influence extends beyond China. The company has begun to explore international markets, testing its model in Southeast Asia and other regions. But the unique conditions of the Chinese market—the dense population, the high smartphone penetration, and the cultural acceptance of the gig economy—make it difficult to replicate its success elsewhere. The company's future will depend on its ability to innovate within the constraints of a regulated market and to adapt to the changing needs of its users.

The story of Meituan is a testament to the power of technology to reshape society. It has made life more convenient for hundreds of millions of people, connecting them to goods and services in ways that were once unimaginable. But it has also exposed the fragility of the gig economy and the dangers of unchecked corporate power. As the sun sets over Beijing, the yellow jackets of the Meituan riders continue to move through the streets, a silent reminder of the human effort that powers the digital dream. They are the engine of the Super-App, the physical reality behind the code, and the face of a new era in commerce.

The Paradox of Convenience

The Meituan model is built on a paradox: the greater the convenience for the consumer, the greater the pressure on the worker. This tension is not unique to Meituan, but it is perhaps most visible in its operations. The company has mastered the art of predicting demand, optimizing routes, and managing a workforce that is both flexible and exploitable. But as the company grows, the question of sustainability becomes harder to answer. Can a business model that relies on the overwork of its employees survive in a society that is increasingly concerned with social justice? Can a platform that dominates the market maintain its legitimacy when it is seen as a monopoly?

These are the questions that will define Meituan's next chapter. The company has the resources, the technology, and the market position to continue its growth. But it must also navigate the complex political and social landscape of modern China. It must find a way to balance profit with purpose, efficiency with empathy. The riders on the street are watching, and the government is watching. The future of Meituan will be determined by how well it can answer these challenges. It is no longer just a company; it is a institution, and its actions have far-reaching consequences for the millions of people who depend on it.

In the end, Meituan is more than a business. It is a reflection of the Chinese economy in the 21st century: fast-paced, competitive, and driven by technology. It is a story of ambition and survival, of innovation and regulation, of convenience and cost. As the city lights flicker on in the evening, the Meituan app remains open on millions of screens, a portal to a world where everything is just a click away. But behind every click, there is a rider, a merchant, and a story that reminds us of the human cost of progress.
