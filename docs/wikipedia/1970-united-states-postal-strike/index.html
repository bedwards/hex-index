1970 United States postal strike

14 min read

Based on Wikipedia: 1970 United States postal strike

On March 15, 1970, the United States postal system, a sprawling network that touched every hamlet and skyscraper in the nation, simply stopped moving. For the first time in the country's history, the massive infrastructure that delivered news, wages, and lifelines to millions of Americans ground to a halt not because of a natural disaster, a bureaucratic error, or a foreign invasion, but because the men and women who drove the trucks and sorted the mail walked off the job. It was a moment of profound rupture, a strike that paralyzed the economy of New York City and sent shockwaves through the White House, exposing a deep fissure between the federal government's rigid control of labor and the desperate, rising economic realities of the working class. The strike did not just delay letters; it halted the circulatory system of American life, forcing a confrontation that would fundamentally alter the relationship between the state and its employees, and inadvertently set the stage for the deindustrialization and labor fragmentation that would define the following decades.

To understand the weight of this moment, one must look at the conditions that simmered beneath the surface of the Post Office Department in the late 1960s. The federal government, in its infinite bureaucratic wisdom, classified postal workers not as employees with rights to bargain, but as public servants whose pay and conditions were dictated by Congress and the President. This was a relic of an earlier era, a time when the post office was viewed as a public utility akin to the army, where loyalty was expected to supersede market forces. But the world had changed. The inflation of the late 1960s, fueled by the costs of the Vietnam War and Great Society programs, was eroding the purchasing power of the average American worker. For a rural letter carrier in the Midwest or a city sorter in the Bronx, a raise of a few dollars a week was not a luxury; it was the difference between feeding a family and falling into debt.

The Post Office Department was a behemoth, employing over 600,000 people nationwide, yet it was run like a failing corporation that refused to acknowledge its own liabilities. Wages were stagnant, often falling behind those of private sector workers who performed similar tasks. A postal clerk in 1970 was making less than a comparable worker at a private shipping firm or a newspaper plant. The working conditions were grueling, characterized by mandatory overtime, outdated equipment, and a management structure that was notoriously resistant to change. The government's official stance was one of paternalistic benevolence, arguing that postal workers were privileged to serve the public and that their wages were set by the legislative branch, a process that was slow, politicized, and often disconnected from the economic reality of the workers themselves.

The spark that ignited the powder keg was not a singular event but a cumulative frustration that reached a breaking point in New York City. In early 1970, the Post Office Department announced that it would not grant the requested wage increases for the coming year, citing budgetary constraints and the need to maintain the integrity of the federal service. This decision was met with quiet fury in the local union halls. The National Federation of Post Office Clerks and the Brotherhood of Postal Clerks, along with other local unions, began to organize in the shadows of the official leadership. The official union hierarchy, wary of federal backlash and legal repercussions, hesitated to call for a strike. The rank-and-file, however, saw no other option.

On March 18, 1970, the decision was made. It was not a coordinated, nationwide action from the start, but a grassroots uprising that spread like wildfire. In New York City, thousands of workers ignored the official chain of command and walked out. They did not picket in the traditional sense; they simply stopped working. The mail piled up in the sorting rooms, a mountain of unread news, unpaid bills, and personal correspondence. The silence in the mailrooms was deafening, a stark contrast to the usual roar of machinery and the shuffle of feet. Within days, the strike had spread to other major cities, including Chicago, Philadelphia, and Boston. By the second week, over 200,000 postal workers across the country were on strike. The sheer scale of the action was unprecedented. It was a direct challenge to the no-strike policy that governed federal employees, a policy that had been in place since the days of the Taft-Hartley Act.

The government's response was immediate and aggressive. President Richard Nixon, who had just begun his second year in office, was faced with a crisis that threatened to paralyze the nation. The White House initially attempted to downplay the strike, characterizing it as a minor labor dispute that would be quickly resolved through negotiation. But as the days passed and the mail continued to pile up, the administration's tone shifted. Nixon ordered the National Guard and the U.S. Army to New York City to handle the mail. It was a surreal sight: soldiers in fatigues standing at sorting tables, trying to make sense of the chaotic flow of letters. The military was deployed not to fight an enemy, but to sort mail, a task they were ill-equipped and untrained to perform.

"The President has ordered the Army and the National Guard to New York to assist in the handling of the mail," a White House statement declared on March 23, 1970. "This is a temporary measure to ensure that the mail continues to move."

But the deployment of the military was a failure. The soldiers, many of whom were young draftees struggling with the Vietnam War, were not postal workers. They did not know the sorting codes, the delivery routes, or the intricacies of the postal system. The mail moved at a glacial pace, and the quality of service plummeted. The attempt to break the strike with force only served to highlight the absurdity of the situation. The government was trying to solve a labor dispute with a military solution, a tactic that was destined to fail. The soldiers were not the answer; the workers were.

The human cost of the strike was not measured in battlefield casualties, but in the daily struggles of families who relied on the mail. For many Americans, the postal service was their only connection to the outside world. Rural residents waited for their prescriptions; businesses waited for their checks; families waited for letters from loved ones stationed overseas. The strike disrupted the rhythm of daily life in a way that was deeply felt. In New York City, the streets were filled with the sight of mail trucks parked idle, their drivers standing on the corners, talking to neighbors, or simply watching the world go by. The strike was not just a labor action; it was a social event, a moment where the invisible workforce became visible to the public.

The public reaction was complex. Initially, there was sympathy for the postal workers. The average American could relate to the struggle of making ends meet in an inflationary economy. The image of the postal worker, often a figure of stability and trust, was one that resonated with the public. The strike was seen by many as a justified response to years of neglect and low wages. However, as the strike dragged on and the disruption to daily life became more pronounced, public opinion began to shift. The frustration of waiting for a bill or a letter grew, and the initial sympathy began to wane. The government, sensing this shift, began to use it to their advantage, portraying the strikers as selfish and unprofessional.

But the narrative of the strikers as selfish was a distortion. The reality was that these were dedicated workers who had been pushed to the brink. The strike was not about greed; it was about survival. The workers were demanding a wage increase that would bring them in line with the private sector, and they were demanding the right to bargain collectively. These were not radical demands; they were basic labor rights that were standard in almost every other industry. The fact that the federal government refused to acknowledge them was a testament to the rigidity of the system.

The turning point came on March 24, 1970, when the Supreme Court issued an injunction against the strike. The court ruled that the strike was illegal under federal law, and ordered the workers to return to their jobs. The injunction was a legal hammer, but it was not enough to break the strike. The workers, emboldened by their solidarity and the support of the public, refused to return. The government then threatened to fire the strikers, a threat that was met with a unified front from the unions. The strikers knew that if they returned without a deal, they would have nothing to show for their sacrifice.

The resolution came through a combination of political pressure and negotiation. President Nixon, realizing that the military solution was a failure and that the strike was becoming a political liability, agreed to meet with the union leaders. The negotiations were tense, with the administration initially refusing to concede on the issue of collective bargaining. But the workers held their ground. They knew that they had the upper hand; the postal system was paralyzed, and the government had no other option but to negotiate.

On March 29, 1970, a tentative agreement was reached. The postal workers were granted a 6 percent wage increase, with a promise of further increases in the coming years. More importantly, the agreement included a provision that allowed for collective bargaining, a right that had been denied to federal employees for decades. The strike was over, but the impact of the action was just beginning. The 1970 postal strike had shattered the myth of the immutable federal bureaucracy. It had shown that even the most entrenched government agencies could be forced to change by the power of organized labor.

The aftermath of the strike was profound. The passage of the Postal Reorganization Act of 1970 was a direct result of the strike. The act transformed the Post Office Department into the United States Postal Service (USPS), an independent agency of the executive branch. The new USPS was given the power to set its own rates, negotiate its own contracts, and manage its own workforce. The act also established the Postal Rate Commission, an independent body responsible for setting postal rates. The strike had forced the government to modernize its approach to labor relations, and the changes were significant.

But the strike also had a darker side. The victory of the postal workers was a high-water mark for federal labor unions. In the decades that followed, the power of organized labor in the United States would begin to erode. The political climate would shift, and the neoliberal policies that would dominate the 1980s and 1990s would dismantle many of the gains that had been won in 1970. The postal strike was a moment of triumph, but it was also a reminder of the fragility of labor rights in a changing political landscape.

The human stories of the strike are the true legacy of the event. There are the stories of the workers who stood on the picket lines, risking their jobs to fight for a fair wage. There are the stories of the families who waited for letters that never came, and the businesses that lost money because of the delay. These stories are often lost in the dry recitation of dates and laws, but they are the heart of the strike. The strike was not just a legal or political event; it was a human event, a moment when ordinary people stood up for their rights and changed the course of history.

"We are not just a bunch of letter carriers," one striker told a reporter in 1970. "We are the backbone of this country. And we are not going to be treated like we don't matter."

That sentiment, simple and powerful, captures the essence of the 1970 strike. It was a moment when the invisible became visible, when the voiceless found their voice, and when the American working class asserted its right to a fair deal. The strike did not solve all the problems of the postal system, nor did it end the struggle for labor rights. But it was a pivotal moment, a turning point that would shape the future of American labor and the role of the federal government in the economy.

The strike also serves as a cautionary tale for the future. The economic conditions that led to the strike—inflation, stagnant wages, and a disconnect between the government and the workforce—are still with us today. The lessons of 1970 are as relevant now as they were then. The power of organized labor, the importance of collective bargaining, and the need for a fair wage are not just historical footnotes; they are urgent issues that continue to shape the American landscape.

In the end, the 1970 postal strike was more than a labor dispute. It was a moment of national reckoning, a time when the United States was forced to confront the realities of its own economy and the needs of its own people. The strike was a victory for the workers, but it was also a reminder of the ongoing struggle for justice and equality in America. The mail moved again, but the memory of the strike remained, a testament to the power of ordinary people to change the course of history.

The strike's legacy is visible in the modern USPS, an agency that, despite its current struggles, still operates under the framework established in 1970. The workers who won that strike are long gone, but their fight continues in the daily battles of the postal workforce. They are the inheritors of a legacy of resistance, a legacy that was forged in the mailrooms of New York and the streets of America. The 1970 strike was a moment of courage, a moment of solidarity, and a moment that changed the world. It was a strike that proved that even the most powerful institutions can be brought to their knees by the power of the people.

The strike also highlighted the intersection of labor and race. The postal workforce was disproportionately Black, particularly in the urban centers where the strike was most intense. The strike was a moment of racial solidarity, as Black and white workers stood together in the face of a government that had long ignored their needs. The strike was a victory for the civil rights movement as well as the labor movement, a reminder that the struggle for economic justice is inextricably linked to the struggle for racial justice.

The rise of digital communication has changed the nature of the work, and the agency has struggled to adapt to the changing economy. But the spirit of the 1970 strike remains. The workers continue to fight for fair wages, safe working conditions, and the right to bargain. They are the descendants of the strikers of 1970, carrying on the legacy of a struggle that changed the course of American history.
