Divine hiddenness

13 min read

Based on Wikipedia: Divine hiddenness

In 1975, the philosopher J.L. Schellenberg, then a doctoral student at the University of California, Berkeley, began to grapple with a question that would eventually fracture the comfortable consensus of classical theism. He was not a skeptic by trade; he was a philosopher of religion seeking to understand the nature of a loving God. Yet, as he turned over the familiar arguments for existence—the design of the cosmos, the moral law within—he found himself staring into a silence that was too loud to ignore. If a personal, loving God exists, why is there not a single, unambiguous instance of divine interaction that compels belief in every capable human mind? This was not a question of intellectual arrogance, but of emotional desperation. Schellenberg realized that the mere possibility of non-belief was not just a logical gap, but a profound existential wound. He would later formalize this intuition into what is now known as the argument from divine hiddenness, a challenge that has reshaped the landscape of analytic philosophy and continues to trouble theologians, atheists, and agnostics alike.

The core of the argument rests on a seemingly simple premise that, when unpacked, reveals a deep tension in the concept of a benevolent deity. It begins with the definition of God as a personal being who desires a relationship with humanity. In human terms, if you love someone, you want to be known by them. You do not remain a ghost in their life, leaving them to guess at your presence while they suffer in confusion. A parent does not hide from a child to test their faith; a lover does not vanish to prove their devotion. Schellenberg argued that if such a God exists, non-resistant non-belief should be impossible. By "non-resistant," he meant those who are open to the idea of God, who are not actively rebelling against the divine, but who simply lack the evidence to believe. The existence of such people—sincere seekers who remain unconvinced—is, according to Schellenberg, incompatible with the existence of a loving God.

Consider the human experience of the seeker. This is not the hardened atheist who has rejected God out of spite, nor the devout believer who has found their answer in scripture. This is the person who has read the texts, prayed in the quiet hours of the night, and looked at the stars with a heart full of longing, yet finds only silence. They are not resisting; they are waiting. And in that waiting, they experience a profound isolation. If God is real, why is the silence so absolute? Why must the relationship be built on a foundation of ambiguity that leaves the most honest among us in the dark? Schellenberg's argument suggests that this silence is not a test of character, but evidence of absence. A loving God would ensure that anyone capable of a relationship with Him could enter into it. The fact that they cannot is a logical contradiction.

The implications of this argument are far-reaching, striking at the heart of theodicy—the attempt to justify God's goodness in the face of evil. For centuries, theologians have argued that human free will is the price of a meaningful relationship. God must allow evil to exist so that humans can choose good. But the argument from hiddenness cuts deeper. It suggests that even without the problem of moral evil, the problem of divine silence remains. If God's primary desire is a personal relationship, then the hiddenness of God is itself a form of suffering. It is a spiritual abandonment that leaves the seeker in a state of existential limbo. This is not a minor philosophical puzzle; it is a crisis of faith that has haunted countless individuals throughout history. The question is not just whether God exists, but whether a God who hides can truly be called loving.

The Evolution of the Argument

The intellectual lineage of this problem stretches back further than Schellenberg's 1993 formulation, though he gave it its modern, rigorous structure. Ancient thinkers like Augustine and Aquinas touched upon the mystery of God's withdrawal, but they generally framed it as a divine mystery to be accepted rather than a logical contradiction to be solved. The shift to a logical argument occurred in the 20th century, as philosophy of religion became more analytical and precise. Schellenberg's work was a watershed moment because he moved the discussion from the realm of mystery to the realm of evidence. He did not ask, "Why is God hidden?" but rather, "How can a hidden God exist?" This subtle shift changed the burden of proof. It was no longer up to the atheist to prove God does not exist; it was up to the theist to explain how a hidden God could be consistent with divine love.

In 1993, Schellenberg published his seminal paper, "The Problem of Divine Hiddenness," which laid out the argument with surgical precision. He posited that if a loving God exists, then there is no time at which any person capable of a personal relationship with God is in a state of non-resistant non-belief. The existence of such a person at any time, therefore, constitutes evidence against the existence of such a God. The argument was not merely a restatement of the problem of evil; it was a distinct, independent challenge. While the problem of evil asks why God allows suffering, the problem of hiddenness asks why God allows uncertainty. And for many, the uncertainty is more devastating than the suffering, for it robs the sufferer of the comfort of a divine presence.

The argument has since been refined and expanded by other philosophers. Paul Draper, for instance, has argued that the hiddenness of God is not just a logical inconsistency but a probabilistic one. He suggests that the evidence of divine hiddenness is much more likely if naturalism is true than if theism is true. In other words, the silence of the universe is exactly what we would expect if there were no God, but it is deeply unexpected if there is a loving God. This probabilistic approach has added a new layer of complexity to the debate, moving it away from absolute logical proofs and into the realm of evidential reasoning. The question is no longer whether the existence of non-believers disproves God, but whether it makes God's existence significantly less probable.

The Theological Counter-Arguments

The response from the theological community has been vigorous and varied, reflecting the depth of the challenge. The most common defense is the "soul-making" theodicy, which argues that God hides Himself to foster genuine human freedom and moral growth. If God were unmistakably present, the argument goes, belief would be coerced. People would believe not out of love or trust, but out of fear or overwhelming evidence. This would destroy the possibility of a free, authentic relationship. According to this view, God's hiddenness is a necessary condition for the kind of love that matters most.

However, this defense has faced significant criticism. Critics argue that it assumes a false dichotomy between coercion and belief. Is it really true that clear evidence would destroy free will? In human relationships, we often have clear evidence of love—a partner's unwavering support, a parent's protective care—yet we still have the freedom to accept or reject that love. Why should the divine relationship be different? Furthermore, the soul-making argument seems to punish the most vulnerable. The person who is sincere in their search but lacks the capacity to believe due to a lack of evidence is not being given a chance to grow; they are being denied the relationship altogether. If God's goal is to save everyone, then the strategy of hiding Himself seems counterproductive.

Another line of defense appeals to the limitations of human cognition. Some theologians argue that God is so vastly different from us that our concept of "love" and "relationship" may not apply in the way we think. Perhaps God's love is expressed in ways we cannot comprehend, and His hiddenness is actually a form of protection or a higher form of intimacy. This is often called the "mystery defense." While this may offer comfort to some, it fails to address the logical core of Schellenberg's argument. The argument is not based on a specific human concept of love, but on the general principle that a personal being who desires a relationship would make themselves known. To say "God's love is different" is to risk changing the definition of God so much that the argument no longer applies, but it does not solve the original problem.

The Human Cost of Silence

Beyond the abstract debates of academic philosophy, the problem of divine hiddenness has a very real, very human cost. For the individual seeker, the silence of God is not a theoretical puzzle; it is a source of profound grief. It is the feeling of shouting into a void and hearing only your own echo. It is the despair of looking at the beauty of the world and feeling that it is meaningless without a Creator. It is the isolation of being the only one in a room full of believers who cannot feel the presence they claim to feel. This is not a failure of faith; it is a failure of evidence. And in a world where faith is often the only thing holding people together, the absence of that evidence can be devastating.

Consider the case of the agnostic who has lost a child. They pray for comfort, for a sign that their loved one is safe, for a whisper of divine presence. They are not asking for a miracle; they are asking for a connection. When that connection does not come, they are left with a silence that feels like abandonment. The theological explanations—that God is testing them, that their faith is not strong enough, that God's ways are higher—can feel like cruel platitudes. They do not address the raw pain of the silence. The argument from hiddenness validates this pain. It says that the silence is not their fault; it is a reflection of a deeper reality. It gives a name to the feeling of abandonment and insists that it is a legitimate reason to question the existence of a loving God.

This human dimension is often overlooked in the sterile language of analytic philosophy. The argument is not just about logic; it is about the human need for meaning and connection. It is about the tragedy of a universe that may be silent. When we talk about divine hiddenness, we are not just talking about a philosophical problem; we are talking about the hearts of millions of people who are searching for something more, only to find nothing. And in that search, they face a choice: to accept the silence as a mystery, or to conclude that the silence is the answer.

The Broader Implications

The argument from divine hiddenness has rippled out beyond philosophy of religion, influencing theology, psychology, and even public discourse. It has forced theologians to reconsider the nature of divine action and the role of revelation. It has led to a greater emphasis on the experiential and the communal in religious practice, as believers seek to find God in the lives of others rather than in direct revelation. It has also provided a powerful tool for skeptics, offering a rational basis for their lack of belief that goes beyond the problem of evil.

In the public square, the argument has become a common touchpoint in debates about faith and reason. It is often cited by those who argue that religion is a human construct, a projection of our own desires onto a silent universe. It challenges the notion that faith is a leap into the dark, suggesting instead that the darkness is the natural state of the universe. And it forces believers to confront the reality that their faith is not self-evident, but a choice made in the face of uncertainty.

The debate continues to this day, with new arguments emerging and old ones being refined. But the core of the problem remains unchanged. The silence of God is a reality that cannot be ignored. It is a silence that speaks volumes. And for those who hear it, the question is not whether God exists, but whether a God who hides can truly be the source of the love and hope that humanity craves.

In the end, the argument from divine hiddenness is not just a logical puzzle. It is a mirror held up to the human condition. It reflects our deepest desires for connection, our greatest fears of abandonment, and our most profound questions about the nature of reality. It reminds us that the search for God is not just an intellectual exercise, but a deeply human journey. And in that journey, the silence of God may be the most powerful voice of all.

The legacy of Schellenberg's work is not just in the arguments he made, but in the questions he forced us to ask. He challenged the comfortable assumptions of the religious and the skeptical alike. He showed that the silence of the universe is not just a backdrop for our stories, but a central character in the drama of faith. And in doing so, he has ensured that the problem of divine hiddenness will remain a vital and vitalizing part of the philosophical and theological conversation for generations to come.

The debate is far from over. New voices are entering the conversation, bringing fresh perspectives and new arguments. Some argue that the hiddenness of God is a necessary condition for the existence of a meaningful universe. Others argue that it is the ultimate proof of God's absence. But regardless of where one stands, the argument from divine hiddenness demands our attention. It is a challenge that cannot be easily dismissed, a question that strikes at the heart of what it means to be human in a silent universe.

As we look to the future, the problem of divine hiddenness will continue to shape our understanding of God, of ourselves, and of the world we inhabit. It will continue to challenge our assumptions, to provoke our thinking, and to deepen our understanding of the human condition. And in that process, it will continue to remind us of the profound mystery that lies at the heart of existence. The silence is there. The question is what we will do with it.
