Oracle bone script

16 min read

Based on Wikipedia: Oracle bone script

In 1899, a sick man in Beijing named Wang Yirong, a high-ranking official in the Imperial Academy, was prescribed medicine containing "dragon bones" to treat his malaria. These were not mythical remains but fossilized animal bones and turtle plastrons purchased from a local apothecary. Upon closer inspection, Wang noticed that the bones were not natural; they were carved with intricate, angular characters that predated any known Chinese writing system. He had stumbled upon the earliest known form of Chinese writing, a script buried for three millennia, waiting in the earth of Henan province to be unearthed. This discovery did more than fill a gap in linguistic history; it shattered the myth that Chinese civilization was a vague, prehistoric fog before the Zhou dynasty, anchoring the Shang dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BCE) in the hard, undeniable reality of written record.

These bones, known as jiaguwen, are the physical receipts of a kingdom obsessed with the supernatural. They were not written for history books, nor were they intended for the education of the masses. They were the intimate, terrified, and desperate dialogues between the living kings of Shang and the dead spirits of their ancestors. The script itself is a marvel of structural evolution, yet it carries the heavy weight of a society where the boundary between the human and the divine was permeable, often sealed only by the blood of the sacrificed.

The material world of the Shang was one of bronze and bone, fire and smoke. When a king faced a decision—whether to go to war, to harvest crops, to marry a queen, or to simply survive the next storm—he did not consult a council of ministers in the modern sense. He turned to the oracle. The process was a ritual of extreme precision and terrifying stakes. Diviners, usually members of the royal court, would take a plastron from a tortoise or the scapula of a bull, ox, or deer. They would drill holes into the bone, then apply a heated bronze rod to the back. The heat would cause the bone to crack, producing a series of fissures on the front surface. The diviner would then interpret the shape of these cracks as the answer from the ancestors.

The inscriptions were then carved directly onto the bone, often right next to the crack that dictated the answer. The script is pictographic in its origins but rapidly evolved into a complex logographic system. A drawing of the sun, a circle with a dot in the middle, became the character for ri (sun). A drawing of a river, with three winding lines, became chuan (river). But unlike later, more fluid scripts, oracle bone script is rigid, sharp, and angular. This is not merely an aesthetic choice; it is a physical necessity of the medium. Carving into hard bone or turtle shell with a bronze knife requires short, straight strokes. Curves are difficult; flourishes are impossible. The result is a script that looks like a frozen moment of urgency, a jagged landscape of meaning etched in stone and bone.

The content of these inscriptions reveals the daily anxieties of the Shang elite. They were deeply concerned with the weather, the harvest, and the health of the king. But they were equally obsessed with warfare and the cost of human life. The Shang kingdom was a militaristic state that expanded through brutal conquest, and the oracle bones document this violence with chilling matter-of-factness. One inscription records a campaign against the Tufang tribe, noting that the king led the army and captured 1,308 prisoners. Another details a sacrifice of 300 prisoners to the ancestors. These numbers are not abstract statistics; they represent 1,608 human beings whose lives were extinguished or repurposed to serve the spiritual needs of the state.

This brings us to the darkest undercurrent of the script. You cannot discuss oracle bone script without discussing the human sacrifices that accompanied the rituals of divination. The bones themselves are often the byproducts of the very sacrifices they describe. In the royal cemetery at Yinxu, the capital of the late Shang dynasty, archaeologists have uncovered thousands of sacrificial pits. These were not mass graves of the poor or the forgotten; they were the graves of the sacrificed. The victims were often prisoners of war, but also included members of the Shang nobility who were deemed necessary offerings.

The human cost was staggering. Excavations at Yinxu have revealed pits containing the dismembered bodies of children, women, and men, their skulls separated from their bodies, their limbs severed. Some were buried alongside chariots and horses, meant to accompany a deceased king into the afterlife. The script records these acts with the same clinical detachment as it records a harvest yield. "To ask the ancestors: Shall we sacrifice ten humans?" reads one inscription. "The ancestors approved." The approval came in the form of a crack in a bone, a physical manifestation of divine will that justified the slaughter of ten human beings.

For the victims, the experience was one of absolute terror. Imagine a prisoner of war, perhaps a young man from the Tufang tribe, brought before the royal court. He is not given a trial. He is not given a chance to plead. His value lies solely in his potential to serve as a conduit to the ancestors. He is led to a pit, his hands bound, and he is killed, his blood spilled on the earth or on the altars of bronze. His body is then buried, sometimes in a crouched position, sometimes in a pit with his head removed. His name, his life, his family, his story—all are erased, reduced to a number on a bone that would eventually be discarded in a pit and forgotten for three thousand years.

The script also reveals the social hierarchy of the time. The inscriptions are almost exclusively in the voice of the king or his immediate diviners. There are no records of the common people's thoughts, their fears, or their joys. The world of the oracle bones is the world of the elite, where the fate of the entire kingdom rested on the interpretation of cracks in a piece of turtle shell. The common people were the laborers who built the cities, the farmers who grew the millet, and the soldiers who fought the wars. They were the fuel for the machine of the Shang state, and their suffering was the price paid for the king's connection to the divine.

The discovery of these bones in 1899 was a watershed moment for Chinese historiography. Before this, the existence of the Shang dynasty was known only through later texts, such as the Records of the Grand Historian by Sima Qian, written a thousand years after the fact. These later texts were treated with skepticism by Western scholars, who believed the Shang was a mythological construct. The oracle bones provided the smoking gun. They proved that the Shang was a real, sophisticated civilization with a complex writing system, a centralized government, and a vast military apparatus.

The script itself is the ancestor of the modern Chinese characters. While the forms have changed over the millennia, the underlying logic remains the same. The character for "horse" in oracle bone script is a stylized drawing of a horse's head and body; the character for "moon" is a crescent shape. This continuity is a testament to the resilience of Chinese culture. The script survived the fall of the Shang, the rise and fall of dynasties, the destruction of libraries, and the chaos of war. It evolved from the angular, carved bones of the Bronze Age to the smooth, flowing ink of the calligraphers, but the essence of the language remained intact.

Today, scholars have deciphered over 4,000 distinct characters from the oracle bones, though many more remain undeciphered. The study of these texts has opened a window into the mind of the ancient Shang people. We see their obsession with the sun and the moon, their fear of disease and drought, their reverence for the ancestors, and their willingness to commit unspeakable acts of violence in the name of the divine.

The oracle bones also serve as a stark reminder of the fragility of human life in the face of absolute power. The Shang kings believed they could command the spirits of their ancestors through sacrifice. They believed that by offering the lives of others, they could secure the favor of the gods and ensure the prosperity of their kingdom. But the kingdom eventually fell. The last Shang king, Di Xin, known as King Zhou, was a tyrant whose excesses and cruelty led to his downfall. He was defeated by King Wu of Zhou in 1046 BCE, and the Shang dynasty came to an end. The oracle bones were buried, their secrets lost to time, waiting for a sick man in Beijing to rediscover them.

The rediscovery of the oracle bones was not just an archaeological triumph; it was a moral reckoning. It forced the world to confront the reality of human sacrifice in ancient China. For centuries, the narrative of Chinese history had been one of philosophical refinement and cultural continuity. The oracle bones shattered that narrative, revealing a world of brutal violence and deep spiritual anxiety. They showed that the Shang were not just a civilization of bronze and jade, but a civilization of blood and bone.

The script itself is a testament to the human need to communicate, to record, to remember. Even in the midst of such violence, the Shang people sought to leave a mark. They carved their fears, their hopes, and their commands into the hardest materials they could find. They wanted their words to last, to endure beyond the brief span of a human life. And in a way, they succeeded. The words they carved three thousand years ago are still being read, studied, and debated today. They are a bridge between the ancient world and the modern, a reminder that the human condition has not changed all that much. We still fear the unknown, we still seek the favor of the divine, and we still struggle to make sense of a world that often seems indifferent to our suffering.

The oracle bones are not just artifacts; they are the voices of the dead. They speak to us across the millennia, telling us of a time when the line between the living and the dead was thin, when the fate of a kingdom rested on the crack of a bone, and when the lives of thousands were sacrificed to the whims of the ancestors. They are a testament to the power of the written word, a power that can outlast empires, survive the ravages of time, and bring the dead back to life, if only for a moment.
