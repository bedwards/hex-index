Ward Churchill

13 min read

Based on Wikipedia: Ward Churchill

On January 11, 2007, a jury in a federal district court in Denver found Ward Churchill guilty of fraud and ordered him to repay $200,000 in back pay, effectively ending a tenure that had transformed a university professor into one of the most polarizing figures in modern American academia. This verdict did not arrive in a vacuum; it was the culmination of a decade-long firestorm that began not in a courtroom, but in the immediate, suffocating aftermath of the September 11 attacks. When the Twin Towers fell, the nation turned inward, seeking explanations and assigning blame with a ferocity that left little room for nuance. Into this vacuum of shock stepped Churchill, a tenured associate professor of ethnic studies at the University of Colorado Boulder, who published a 4,000-word essay titled "Some People Push Back: On the Justice of Roosting Chickens." In it, he described the victims of the World Trade Center attacks as "little Eichmanns," arguing that their presence in the World Trade Center made them complicit in the financial and imperial machinery of the United States. The phrase was not merely an insult; it was a theological rupture in the American civil religion, a deliberate provocation that demanded a reaction.

To understand the magnitude of the reaction, one must first understand the man who wrote the essay, not as the caricature of a villain that emerged in the tabloids, but as a figure whose academic trajectory was built on a foundation of radical activism and contested identity. Ward Churchill was born in 1947 in Colorado to a family of mixed heritage, though his claimed Native American ancestry became the central axis of his public persona. He identified as a member of the Comanche, Kiowa, and Cherokee nations, a claim that would later be the subject of intense scrutiny and eventual debunking by university investigators. Before his academic career, Churchill was a prominent figure in the American Indian Movement (AIM), participating in the Wounded Knee occupation of 1973 and serving time in federal prison. His transition from activist to academic was seamless; he leveraged his background to secure positions at various institutions before landing at the University of Colorado in 1990. There, he taught courses on Native American studies, criminal justice, and political science, cultivating a reputation as a fiery, uncompromising critic of US colonialism and imperialism.

The "little Eichmanns" essay was not an isolated outburst of rage but the crystallization of a decades-long ideological framework. Churchill’s argument rested on the premise that the United States was a genocidal state, and that the World Trade Center represented the pinnacle of its global domination. By labeling the victims as "little Eichmanns," he was drawing a direct line between the bureaucratic machinery of the Holocaust and the bureaucratic machinery of American capitalism. He posited that the workers inside the towers were not innocent bystanders but active participants in the system that oppressed indigenous peoples and destabilized the Global South. This perspective, while consistent with his previous writings, was uniquely lethal in the post-9/11 climate. The nation was mourning; Churchill was indicting. The essay was widely distributed, first online and then in print, sparking immediate outrage from political leaders, religious groups, and the general public. Senators called for his firing. Protesters gathered outside the university. The atmosphere in Boulder, a town known for its liberal leanings, turned toxic overnight.

Yet, the controversy was not solely about the essay. As the initial wave of fury receded, a more dangerous investigation began, one that would dissect Churchill’s entire academic career. The university, under immense political pressure, formed an investigative committee to look into allegations of academic misconduct. The inquiry quickly expanded beyond the ethics of his 9/11 comments to the very integrity of his scholarship. The committee discovered a pattern of what they deemed fabrications and plagiarism. They found that Churchill had claimed to be a full-blooded Native American when records suggested he had no verifiable tribal affiliation. They found that he had fabricated or heavily distorted data in his research on the smallpox blankets of the 18th century, a claim he had used to argue that biological warfare was a central tenet of US policy against indigenous populations. The committee also uncovered instances where he had plagiarized the work of other scholars without attribution, lifting passages word-for-word in his books and articles.

The findings of the investigative report were devastating. It concluded that Churchill had engaged in "a pattern of misconduct" that undermined the integrity of the university. In June 2005, the University of Colorado's Board of Regents voted to terminate his employment. The decision was met with a split response. For many, it was a long-overdue correction of a fraud who had abused the protections of academic freedom to spread lies and hate. For others, particularly within the academic left and among civil liberties advocates, it was a dangerous precedent. The question was not whether Churchill was right or wrong about the 9/11 attacks, they argued, but whether a university could fire a tenured professor simply for expressing unpopular or offensive views. The firing of Churchill became a flashpoint in the broader culture wars, a test case for the limits of free speech in an era of heightened national security anxiety.

The legal battle that followed was as protracted and bitter as the initial controversy. Churchill sued the university, alleging wrongful termination and violations of his First Amendment rights. The case dragged on for years, moving through the state courts and eventually to the federal level. Throughout the litigation, Churchill maintained his innocence, framing the investigation as a politically motivated witch hunt designed to silence a critical voice. He argued that his claims about his heritage were matters of self-identification and that his scholarly work, while perhaps controversial, was protected speech. The university, conversely, stood firm on the findings of the committee, arguing that the fraud and plagiarism were independent of the 9/11 essay and that they violated the fundamental standards of academic integrity required for tenure. The human cost of this legal warfare was not measured in casualties, but in the erosion of trust, the financial drain on both parties, and the deepening of the ideological chasm in American society.

In 2007, the federal jury delivered its verdict. They found that Churchill had committed fraud in obtaining his tenure and ordered him to repay the salary he had received during his employment. The decision was a blow to his reputation and his finances, effectively ending his career as a professor. Yet, the story did not end there. Churchill continued to write and speak, his platform shifted from the university lecture hall to the fringe of the alternative media landscape. He remained a polarizing figure, revered by some as a martyr for free speech and reviled by others as a charlatan who had exploited the vulnerabilities of indigenous identity for personal gain.

The legacy of the Ward Churchill affair is complex and deeply instructive. It serves as a stark reminder of the fragility of academic freedom when it collides with the raw nerves of national trauma. It highlights the dangers of identity politics when self-identification is not subject to verification, and the risks of scholarship that prioritizes ideological purity over factual accuracy. But perhaps most importantly, it reveals the difficulty of separating the person from the polemic. Churchill was a man who built his life on a narrative of resistance, a narrative that he believed justified his methods, even when those methods included deception. The "little Eichmanns" essay was the spark, but the fire was fueled by years of accumulated grievances, both real and imagined.

The controversy also forces a reckoning with the role of the university in society. Is the university a sanctuary for the most dangerous ideas, or is it a place where truth and integrity must be the non-negotiable foundations? The Churchill case suggests that the answer is rarely binary. The university has a duty to protect free speech, but it also has a duty to maintain the integrity of its scholarship. When a professor violates that integrity, the institution must act, but the manner of that action is scrutinized by the world. The University of Colorado was accused of capitulating to political pressure, of abandoning its principles in the face of public outcry. Churchill was accused of abusing his position, of lying to his students and the public, and of using the platform of the university to spread hatred.

In the end, the story of Ward Churchill is not just about one man. It is about the era in which he rose to prominence and the era in which he fell. It is about the 9/11 attacks and the long shadow they cast over American discourse. It is about the struggle to define truth in a post-truth world, where narratives often matter more than facts. The "little Eichmanns" essay remains a chilling document, a testament to the power of language to wound and to divide. But the investigation that followed it is a testament to the power of scrutiny, of the demand for accountability, and the relentless pursuit of the truth, however uncomfortable it may be.

The Anatomy of a Controversy

The initial explosion of the "little Eichmanns" essay was fueled by a specific rhetorical strategy that Churchill employed with calculated precision. He did not merely criticize the US government; he dehumanized the victims of the attacks. This was a deliberate choice. By invoking Eichmann, the Nazi bureaucrat who organized the logistics of the Holocaust, Churchill was attempting to strip the victims of their innocence and reframe them as agents of a genocidal system. This framing was not new to him; it was consistent with his broader thesis that the United States was a criminal enterprise. However, the timing was catastrophic. In the immediate aftermath of 9/11, the American public was in a state of collective trauma. The narrative of the "pure victim" was a source of comfort and unity. Churchill’s essay tore that narrative apart, replacing it with a narrative of complicity and guilt. The result was a backlash that was both immediate and sustained.

The university's response was initially hesitant. In the days following the publication of the essay, the administration issued statements condemning the remarks but stopping short of calling for his dismissal. They cited the principles of academic freedom, arguing that even offensive speech must be protected. However, as the pressure mounted from politicians, donors, and the public, the administration's stance shifted. The formation of the investigative committee was a turning point. It signaled a move from protecting speech to investigating conduct. The committee's findings were then used to justify the firing, effectively bypassing the First Amendment issue by focusing on fraud and plagiarism. This legal maneuver was clever, but it did not satisfy everyone. Critics argued that the investigation was a pretext, a way to fire a professor for his views without violating the Constitution. They pointed to the fact that the fraud allegations were not discovered until after the 9/11 controversy had erupted, suggesting a causal link between the essay and the investigation.

The human element of the controversy cannot be overstated. For Churchill, the firing was a personal and professional catastrophe. He lost his livelihood, his reputation, and his standing in the academic community. He became an outcast, forced to rely on the support of a small circle of allies. For the university, the controversy was a crisis of confidence. It raised questions about the standards of hiring, the rigor of tenure reviews, and the ability of the administration to withstand political pressure. For the students, the experience was a lesson in the complexities of free speech and the responsibilities of scholarship. They watched as a tenured professor, a figure of authority, was dismantled by the very system he claimed to represent.

The Broader Implications

The Ward Churchill case has had lasting implications for the landscape of American higher education. It has made universities more cautious about hiring faculty with controversial backgrounds. It has led to a greater emphasis on the verification of credentials and the integrity of scholarship. It has also sparked debates about the limits of academic freedom and the role of the university in society. Some argue that the case was a necessary correction, a reminder that academic freedom is not a license to lie or to spread hate. Others argue that it was a dangerous precedent, a signal that unpopular views are no longer welcome in the academy.

The case also highlights the dangers of identity politics. Churchill's claim to Native American heritage was central to his authority as a scholar. When that claim was debunked, it undermined his entire academic project. It raised questions about the validity of his scholarship and the ethics of his self-identification. The controversy serves as a cautionary tale about the importance of authenticity in academic work and the need for rigorous verification of claims, especially those related to identity and heritage.

Ultimately, the story of Ward Churchill is a story of the collision between ideology and reality. It is a story of a man who believed in his own narrative so deeply that he was willing to distort the truth to make it fit. It is a story of a society that was willing to tolerate a great deal of dissent, but not when that dissent came in the form of a lie. And it is a story of an institution that struggled to balance its commitment to free speech with its duty to uphold the truth. The lessons of this story are as relevant today as they were in 2005. In an age of misinformation and polarization, the need for integrity, accountability, and nuance has never been greater. The "little Eichmanns" essay was a moment of darkness, but the investigation that followed it was a search for light. The outcome was not perfect, but it was a necessary step in the long and difficult work of maintaining the integrity of the university and the society it serves.

The aftermath of the Churchill case left a scar on the University of Colorado and on the academic community at large. It exposed the fragility of tenure in the face of public outrage and the difficulty of maintaining academic standards in a politically charged environment. It also demonstrated the power of the media to shape public opinion and the role of the university in navigating that landscape. The case remains a touchstone for debates about free speech, academic integrity, and the role of identity in scholarship. It is a reminder that the pursuit of truth is often messy, painful, and controversial, but that it is essential for the health of the academy and the society it serves. The story of Ward Churchill is not over; it continues to be told, debated, and analyzed, a testament to the enduring power of controversy to shape our understanding of the world.
