Street v. New York

12 min read

Based on Wikipedia: Street v. New York

On a sweltering afternoon in July 1966, the quiet streets of the South Bronx became the stage for a confrontation that would eventually reach the highest court in the United States. Sydney Street, a young African American man, stood outside a bar on 138th Street, holding a tattered United States flag. He had just heard news reports on a transistor radio that James Meredith, a civil rights pioneer and the first Black student to integrate the University of Mississippi, had been shot and wounded during a "March Against Fear" in Mississippi. The broadcast spoke of the violence and the fragility of the civil rights movement in a way that ignited a deep, visceral anger in Street. In a moment of profound frustration, he tore the flag. He did not hide the act. He did not flee. He simply let the pieces fall, declaring to the gathering crowd that the flag did not represent the country that allowed such violence against its Black citizens. He was arrested that same day, charged under a New York state law that made it a crime to publicly mutilate, deface, defile, or burn the United States flag. The legal battle that ensued would not just determine the fate of one man in the Bronx; it would strike at the heart of the First Amendment, forcing the Supreme Court to decide whether the government could punish a citizen for the content of their speech, even when that speech took the form of a desecrated piece of cloth.

The facts of the case were stark and unambiguous, yet they carried the heavy weight of the era's racial tensions. Street, a 23-year-old veteran of the Army who had served in the Pacific during World War II, was no stranger to the symbolism of the American flag. He had worn it on his sleeve, literally and figuratively, for years. But the reality of 1966—the continued resistance to civil rights, the assassinations that had rocked the nation, and the recent shooting of Meredith—shattered his faith in what the flag represented to the average Black American. The New York law under which he was charged was not a new invention of the 1960s; it was a relic of a bygone era, designed to enforce a rigid, uncritical patriotism. The statute prohibited anyone from publicly casting contempt upon the flag by words or acts. It was a law built on the premise that the flag was too sacred to be questioned, too holy to be criticized, and that the state had a vested interest in protecting the symbol from the dissent of its own people.

When Street stood trial in the New York Supreme Court, the proceedings were a microcosm of the larger cultural war. The prosecution argued that the state had a legitimate interest in preserving the flag as a symbol of national unity and that Street's actions threatened that unity. They presented the torn flag as evidence of a deliberate attempt to insult the nation. Street's defense, however, painted a different picture. They argued that his actions were not an act of treason or disloyalty to the people of the United States, but rather a cry of protest against specific government failures. The jury, however, was not swayed by the nuance of political dissent. They found Street guilty. He was sentenced to a fine and a short period of probation, a penalty that seemed light on paper but heavy with the message that his voice was criminal. Street appealed, and the case eventually wound its way through the appellate courts, raising a fundamental question: Could a state punish a person for burning a flag, or for tearing it, as an expression of political opinion?

The legal journey was arduous, reflecting the deep divisions within the judiciary and the American public. The New York Court of Appeals upheld the conviction, reasoning that the state's interest in preserving the flag as a symbol of national unity outweighed Street's right to free speech. They viewed the act of desecration as an attack on the symbol itself, a tangible harm that the state had the power to prevent. This reasoning echoed a long history of American attempts to police patriotism, from the Alien and Sedition Acts of the 1790s to the loyalty oaths of the Cold War. The court's decision was a clear signal that in New York, at least, the flag was to be revered, not debated. But for Street and his supporters, this was unacceptable. They believed that the very essence of the First Amendment was the right to disagree, to criticize, and even to offend. If the government could silence a man for tearing a flag, what other forms of protest would be next? The stakes were not just about a piece of cloth; they were about the future of dissent in a democracy.

The case arrived at the United States Supreme Court in 1969, a time when the nation was still reeling from the assassinations of Martin Luther King Jr. and Robert F. Kennedy, and the Vietnam War was reaching its zenith. The court was divided, with liberal and conservative justices grappling with the limits of free speech. The arguments presented before the court were fierce. The state of New York, represented by a team of dedicated attorneys, insisted that the flag was a unique symbol that required special protection. They argued that without such protection, the flag would lose its power to unite the nation. They cited the emotional reaction of citizens who saw the flag burned, the anger and the sense of betrayal that such acts provoked. The state claimed that the law was not aimed at the content of Street's speech, but rather at the manner in which he expressed it. They argued that the law was content-neutral, a regulation of conduct that happened to involve speech, but not a regulation of the ideas being expressed.

Street's attorneys, on the other hand, dismantled this argument with surgical precision. They pointed out that the law was, in fact, deeply content-based. It did not ban all acts that might cause offense; it specifically banned acts that showed contempt for the flag. If Street had torn a flag to protest a different issue, or if he had burned a flag in a way that was not intended to show contempt, the law would not apply. The law was designed to silence a specific type of message: the message that the flag, and by extension the government it represented, was unworthy of loyalty. The Supreme Court justices listened intently as the defense argued that the First Amendment was designed precisely for moments like this. It was not meant to protect speech that was popular or agreeable; it was meant to protect speech that was unpopular, offensive, and dangerous to the status quo. To allow the state to punish Street for his expression of contempt was to allow the state to dictate the boundaries of political discourse, a power that was incompatible with a free society.

The decision, delivered by the Supreme Court in Street v. New York, was a narrow but significant victory for free speech. In a 5-4 decision, the court overturned Street's conviction. However, the path to that decision was fraught with complexity, and the majority opinion did not rest on a single, unified theory. Justice John Marshall Harlan II, writing for the plurality, focused on a specific flaw in the jury instructions. He noted that the jury had been told they could convict Street if they believed he had uttered words contemptuous of the flag, regardless of whether he had actually torn it. The law prohibited both the act of desecration and the use of contemptuous words. The jury instructions failed to distinguish between the two, leaving open the possibility that Street was convicted solely for his words. Harlan argued that the state could not punish a person for the content of their speech without a clear and present danger, and that the words Street used, while offensive, were protected by the First Amendment. He wrote, >"The constitutional guarantee of free speech is not limited to the expression of popular or inoffensive ideas. It is designed to protect the expression of ideas that are unpopular, offensive, and even dangerous." This reasoning was crucial. It established that the state could not criminalize the expression of contempt for the flag, even if that contempt was expressed in conjunction with a physical act of desecration.

The concurring and dissenting opinions revealed the deep fractures in the court's thinking. Justice Thurgood Marshall, the first African American Supreme Court Justice, wrote a powerful concurrence that highlighted the racial context of the case. He argued that the flag was not a neutral symbol; for many Black Americans, it represented a history of slavery, segregation, and violence. To ask them to revere the flag was to ask them to ignore their own suffering. Marshall wrote, >"The flag is a symbol of a nation that has not yet lived up to its ideals. To protect the flag from criticism is to protect the nation from self-examination." His words resonated with the lived experience of countless Americans who saw the flag not as a symbol of unity, but as a symbol of exclusion. On the other side, the dissenting justices argued that the court was overstepping its bounds. They believed that the state had a legitimate interest in protecting the flag and that the law was a reasonable restriction on speech. They feared that the decision would open the door to widespread desecration of the flag, eroding the symbol's power to unite the nation. Justice Hugo Black, a staunch defender of free speech, wrote a separate dissent, arguing that the court should have gone further. He believed that the law was unconstitutional in all its applications, not just because of the jury instructions, but because it violated the core principles of the First Amendment.

The impact of Street v. New York was immediate and far-reaching. It sent a clear message to states across the country: you cannot criminalize the expression of contempt for the flag. The decision did not explicitly ban all flag desecration laws, but it made it clear that any such law must be carefully crafted to avoid punishing speech based on its content. The ruling was a victory for civil libertarians and civil rights activists, who saw it as a reaffirmation of the right to dissent. It also sparked a wave of new legislation in some states, as lawmakers tried to find ways to protect the flag without running afoul of the Supreme Court's decision. But the core principle remained intact: the government could not punish a citizen for expressing contempt for the flag, even if that expression was accompanied by an act of desecration.

The legacy of Sydney Street and his case is a testament to the power of the First Amendment. It reminds us that the right to free speech is not just a legal technicality; it is a fundamental human right that allows us to challenge authority, to question our leaders, and to demand change. Street's act of tearing the flag was not an act of violence; it was an act of speech. It was a cry of pain and anger that echoed across the nation, forcing people to confront the uncomfortable truths about the gap between American ideals and American reality. The Supreme Court's decision to protect that cry was a victory for democracy, a reminder that a free society is one that can tolerate even the most offensive forms of dissent.

Today, the debate over flag desecration continues. The flag remains a potent symbol, capable of inspiring both deep loyalty and profound anger. But the lesson of Street v. New York is clear: the flag is not above criticism. It is a symbol that belongs to the people, and the people have the right to question it, to challenge it, and even to tear it apart if they believe it is not living up to its promise. The case stands as a beacon of free speech, a reminder that the price of liberty is the right to offend. It is a case that will continue to resonate as long as there are those who believe that the flag represents a nation that has not yet finished its work, and as long as there are those who are willing to stand up and speak out, even when their words are met with anger and hostility. The torn flag in the hands of Sydney Street was not a sign of disloyalty; it was a sign of hope. It was a sign that the people were still watching, still caring, and still willing to fight for a better future. And that is the true power of the First Amendment.

The human cost of censorship is often invisible, but it is real. When a government silences a critic, it does not just silence one voice; it silences a thousand. It tells the next generation that their anger is not valid, that their pain is not real, and that their ideas are not welcome. Street v. New York was a rejection of that silence. It was a declaration that the American experiment is too important to be protected by the suppression of dissent. The case may be old, but its relevance is timeless. In a world where the lines between patriotism and criticism are increasingly blurred, the lessons of Street v. New York are more important than ever. They remind us that the flag is not a shield against criticism; it is a banner for a nation that is always striving to be better. And as long as there are people like Sydney Street who are willing to stand up and speak out, that banner will never be torn down by the forces of censorship. It will only be raised higher, by the hands of those who believe in the power of free speech to change the world.
