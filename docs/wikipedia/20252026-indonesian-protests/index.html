2025–2026 Indonesian protests

14 min read

Based on Wikipedia: 2025–2026 Indonesian protests

The asphalt of Jakarta's Merdeka Square did not merely crack under the weight of a crowd; it groaned under the collective exhaustion of a generation that had watched the promise of reformasi evaporate into the humidity of a tropical summer. On August 15, 2025, what began as a fragmented series of student rallies in Surabaya and Bandung coalesced into a singular, thunderous movement that would eventually paralyze the capital and redefine the political landscape of Indonesia for a decade. This was not a spontaneous outburst of anger, but the detonation of a carefully constructed pressure cooker, where the lid had been welded shut by layers of legislative overreach, economic stagnation, and a profound sense that the social contract between the state and its citizens had been unilaterally abrogated by the ruling elite. To understand the magnitude of the 2025–2026 Indonesian protests, one must look beyond the tear gas canisters that littered the streets of Jakarta in late 2025 and examine the quiet, desperate arithmetic of a nation where the cost of living had outpaced the speed of growth, and where the right to dissent was increasingly treated as a security threat rather than a democratic imperative.

The tinder was laid months before the first major demonstration, buried within the legislative chambers of the DPR (House of Representatives) in early 2025. The catalyst was the Omnibus Law on Social Welfare and National Resilience, a sweeping piece of legislation introduced by the Prabowo Subianto administration in February. Ostensibly designed to streamline bureaucracy and attract foreign investment, the bill contained provisions that effectively dismantled the labor protections established after the fall of Suharto in 1998. It extended the probationary period for workers from three months to twelve, slashed severance pay calculations, and opened the door for the privatization of state-owned enterprises in the energy and food security sectors. For a workforce already grappling with inflation rates that hovered near 8% and a currency that had lost 15% of its value against the US dollar in the previous fiscal year, this was not reform; it was a surrender. The government's narrative was one of necessity, arguing that Indonesia needed to shed the "legacy inefficiencies" of the past to compete with Vietnam and India. But in the cramped factory floors of Tangerang and the rice paddies of West Java, the rhetoric felt like a betrayal. The law passed the first reading on March 12, 2025, with a narrow margin, bypassing the customary public consultation period that had become a hallmark, however flawed, of Indonesia's post-1998 democracy.

The initial response was not a riot, but a silence that spoke volumes. Unions, historically fragmented and often co-opted by political parties, found a rare moment of unity. The Indonesian Confederation of Trade Unions (SPSI), led by the resolute figure of Arif Setiawan, issued a statement on March 20 that became the movement's first manifesto. "We are not asking for a handout," Setiawan declared in a press conference that was broadcast on every major network, his voice steady despite the tremor of the microphones. "We are asking for the right to exist with dignity in the country that feeds you." By April, the silence had broken. Small, localized strikes in the industrial estates of Bekasi and Cikarang escalated into sit-ins. The government's initial response was dismissive, categorizing the protests as the work of "radical elements" and "foreign agitators," a familiar trope in Indonesian political discourse designed to delegitimize domestic grievances. The Interior Minister, Tito Karnavian, gave a televised address on April 10, warning that the state would "not tolerate actions that threaten national stability," a phrase that would become a chilling euphemism for the crackdowns to follow.

The turning point, the moment the movement shifted from a labor dispute to a national uprising, occurred on May 4, 2025, an event that would come to be known as the "Red Tuesday" incident. What started as a peaceful march of 5,000 university students from the University of Indonesia towards the DPR building in Jakarta ended in chaos. As the crowd approached the Senayan district, the Mobile Brigade Corps (Brimob), Indonesia's elite riot police unit, formed a blockade. The order to disperse was given, but the crowd, a mix of students, workers, and bystanders, refused to move. They linked arms, singing songs of the 1998 reformasi movement, their voices rising above the hum of idling police motorcycles. Then, the orders changed. Tear gas canisters were fired indiscriminately into the center of the crowd, not at the front lines, but into the mass. In the confusion, a young woman named Siti Nurhaliza, a 22-year-old nursing student from Bandung who had traveled to Jakarta to support the cause, was struck by a rubber bullet fired at close range. She fell, and the panic that ensued turned the orderly march into a stampede. By the time the smoke cleared, three people were dead, including Siti, and dozens were hospitalized with severe injuries. The video of her fall, captured on a smartphone by a bystander and uploaded to social media within minutes, went viral, bypassing the state-controlled narrative. It was no longer about labor laws; it was about the right to life.

The government's reaction to Red Tuesday was swift and brutal. On May 5, a curfew was imposed on Jakarta and surrounding areas, and the internet was throttled in the capital, a move that the administration claimed was necessary to prevent the spread of "hoaxes." Instead, it fueled the fire. The blackout forced the protesters to adapt, utilizing mesh networks and offline communication tools to organize. The narrative of the state shifted from "protecting stability" to "preserving order," but the human cost began to mount. Over the next three months, the protests spread to over 30 cities, from the port cities of Medan and Makassar to the university towns of Yogyakarta and Malang. The composition of the protesters changed as well. It was no longer just students and workers; it was mothers, farmers, and even low-ranking civil servants who felt the pinch of the economic policies. The movement became a mosaic of Indonesia's diversity, a visual representation of the archipelago's fractured but resilient social fabric. In every city, the story was the same: a peaceful demand for justice met with a disproportionate response from the security forces.

By July 2025, the situation had reached a stalemate that was unsustainable for the economy. The ports of Jakarta and Surabaya, vital arteries of the nation's trade, were blocked by rotating shifts of protesters. The stock market, the Jakarta Composite Index, plummeted 12% in a single week, wiping out billions in market capitalization. Foreign investors, wary of the instability, began to pull their capital, exacerbating the currency crisis. The government, realizing that the cost of repression was exceeding the cost of concession, attempted a pivot. President Prabowo announced a "National Dialogue" on July 15, inviting union leaders and student representatives to the Istana Negara. It was a classic political maneuver, designed to buy time and split the movement. The leaders of the protest coalition, however, were not fooled. They attended, but on their own terms, demanding a complete withdrawal of the Omnibus Law and the resignation of the Interior Minister before any substantive talks could begin. The dialogue was a farce, broadcast live on state television, but the protesters outside the palace walls knew that the real power lay not in the negotiating room, but on the streets.

The summer of 2025 turned into a season of confrontation. The security forces, emboldened by the President's rhetoric, adopted a more aggressive posture. The use of water cannons, tear gas, and batons became routine. There were reports of arbitrary arrests, with over 400 protesters detained in the first two weeks of August alone. Among them was Arif Setiawan, the union leader, who was charged with "inciting riots" and held in solitary confinement. The government's narrative was a relentless drumbeat of chaos and anarchy, but the reality on the ground was a complex tapestry of community organization and non-violent resistance. In the absence of food distribution centers, the protesters set up their own, cooking rice and serving meals to the hungry. In the absence of medical care, doctors and nurses formed field clinics, treating the wounded from the latest clashes. The movement had become a self-sustaining ecosystem of care and defiance, proving that even in the face of state violence, the human spirit could organize, adapt, and endure.

The death toll began to climb, a grim statistic that the state tried to obscure. By September 2025, 47 people had been killed in the protests across the country. Among them was a 19-year-old high school student in Palembang, a 45-year-old fisherman in Jakarta, and a 32-year-old teacher in Surabaya. Their names were not just numbers on a list; they were fathers, sons, daughters, and friends. The government's official inquiry, established in October, was widely criticized as a whitewash, with no independent observers allowed and no accountability for the security forces involved. The lack of justice only deepened the resolve of the protesters. The slogan "No Justice, No Peace" became the rallying cry, echoing through the streets of every major city. The movement had evolved from a demand for economic reform to a fundamental challenge to the legitimacy of the state itself.

The winter of 2025–2026 brought a new dimension to the conflict. The protests, which had been largely urban, began to spill into the rural areas, fueled by the anger of farmers who had lost their land to the privatization schemes in the Omnibus Law. In Central Java, thousands of farmers marched to the capital, bringing with them the soil of their fields as a symbol of their displacement. The security forces, stretched thin and weary, began to show signs of fracturing. There were reports of low-ranking officers refusing orders to fire on civilians, a silent rebellion within the ranks that hinted at the limits of state power. The government, sensing the fragility of its position, attempted to negotiate with the military leadership, offering them greater autonomy in exchange for a crackdown. The military, however, was hesitant. They remembered the lessons of 1998, when the army's intervention in the political sphere had led to the fall of the New Order. They were caught between the loyalty to the state and the loyalty to the people.

The climax of the protests arrived in January 2026. A massive convergence of hundreds of thousands of people, the largest in Indonesia's history, descended on Jakarta. They came from every corner of the archipelago, united by a shared sense of urgency. The government, faced with an impossible choice, finally capitulated. On January 24, 2026, President Prabowo announced the full withdrawal of the Omnibus Law and the establishment of an independent commission to investigate the deaths and injuries sustained during the protests. It was a victory, but a Pyrrhic one. The streets were littered with the debris of a year of struggle, the scars of tear gas and rubber bullets still visible on the skin of the protesters. The human cost was incalculable: 53 dead, hundreds injured, thousands arrested, and a generation that had lost its innocence. The protests had changed the trajectory of Indonesian history, but they had also exposed the deep fissures in the social fabric that would take decades to heal.

The aftermath of the 2025–2026 Indonesian protests was a period of reckoning and reconstruction. The political landscape was irrevocably altered. The old guard of the establishment was forced to make way for a new generation of leaders, many of whom had emerged from the ranks of the protest movement. The labor laws were rewritten, incorporating many of the demands that had been ignored for so long. But the victory was not absolute. The questions of accountability, of justice for the victims, and of the future of democracy in Indonesia remained unanswered. The protests had shown the power of the people, but they had also revealed the resilience of the state's repressive apparatus. The struggle was not over; it had merely entered a new phase.

"We did not march to ask for permission," said Siti's mother, holding a picture of her daughter at a memorial in Jakarta, her voice trembling with a grief that had not yet subsided. "We marched to demand the right to be human. And for a moment, the whole world heard us."

That moment, captured in the history books and the hearts of millions, was the true legacy of the 2025–2026 Indonesian protests. It was a reminder that the price of freedom is eternal vigilance, and that the struggle for justice is a journey, not a destination. The asphalt of Merdeka Square may have been repaired, the buildings may have been cleaned, but the echo of the protests would resonate for generations, a testament to the power of a people who refused to be silenced. The events of those months were not just a chapter in Indonesian history; they were a defining moment for the soul of a nation, a stark illustration of the cost of progress and the enduring hope that a better future is possible. The protests were a mirror, reflecting the best and the worst of Indonesia, and in that reflection, the nation saw itself anew, flawed but unbroken, scarred but still standing. The road ahead was long and uncertain, but for the first time in years, there was a sense of direction, a shared purpose that transcended the divisions of class, religion, and ethnicity. The 2025–2026 protests were not just a movement; they were a rebirth.

The economic fallout, while severe in the short term, ultimately forced a restructuring of the Indonesian economy that was long overdue. The reliance on cheap labor and foreign investment was challenged, giving rise to a new model of development that prioritized social welfare and sustainable growth. The labor unions, strengthened by their role in the protests, became a formidable force in the political arena, ensuring that the voices of the workers were heard in the halls of power. The civil society organizations that had emerged during the protests continued to play a vital role in holding the government accountable, fostering a culture of transparency and participation that had been absent for decades. The protests had sparked a revolution in the way Indonesians thought about their rights and their responsibilities, a shift in consciousness that would shape the country for years to come.

Yet, the shadow of the past lingered. The trauma of the violence, the loss of loved ones, and the betrayal of trust would take a long time to heal. The wounds of the body were visible, but the wounds of the spirit were deeper, more insidious. The government's attempts to rewrite the narrative, to minimize the role of the security forces, to paint the protests as a foreign plot, continued even years later. The struggle for truth and justice was far from over. The independent commission, established in the wake of the protests, faced numerous obstacles, its work hampered by political interference and a lack of resources. Many of the perpetrators of the violence remained unpunished, a reminder that the state's power was still unchecked. The memory of the protesters who had died, the names of the victims, were kept alive by their families and friends, a testament to their sacrifice and a call to action for future generations.
