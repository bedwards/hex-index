On May 13, 2003, in the sweltering heat of Guangzhou, a 26-year-old man named Sun Zhigang was beaten to death by police officers in a detention center. He was not a criminal. He was not a vagrant. He was a college graduate working as a designer, carrying his household registration documents but failing to produce the temporary residence permit required by local law. His death, broadcast to a nation already simmering with frustration over the arbitrary power of the state, did more to dismantle a three-decade-old system than any legislative debate ever could. Within weeks, the Custody and Repatriation (C&R) system, a mechanism that had detained millions of China's poorest and most vulnerable, was abolished. But the silence of the system prior to that day was deafening, a bureaucratic machine designed not to rehabilitate or protect, but to manage the chaos of urbanization by simply sweeping people off the streets and shipping them back to their rural origins.

To understand the sheer scale of the C&R system, one must first grasp the rigid architecture of the Chinese household registration system, or hukou. Born in the 1950s to control population movement and allocate scarce resources, the hukou tied every citizen's access to education, healthcare, and housing to their place of birth. For a farmer born in a village in Henan or Anhui, this was not merely a bureaucratic ID; it was the boundary of their legal existence. If they stepped into a major city like Beijing or Shanghai without official permission, they were technically present there as an illegal resident, regardless of how hard they worked or how long they stayed.

As China's economy surged in the 1980s and 1990s, factories in the coastal megacities demanded millions of laborers. Peasants flooded into cities, leaving their fields for assembly lines. Yet, the hukou system did not evolve to accommodate this migration. These migrant workers, essential to the economic miracle, remained legal outsiders in the places they built. They lived in fear of the police, not because they committed crimes, but because their very presence was a technical violation of administrative order.

Enter the Custody and Repatriation system. Established formally in 1982, it was ostensibly created to help vagrants and beggars, offering them food and shelter before sending them home. But in practice, it became a tool of social control, a way for local governments to cleanse their cities of the "undesirable." The law gave police broad, sweeping powers. They could detain anyone suspected of being a vagrant, a beggar, or a person without proper identification or residence permits. There was no need for a warrant. There was no trial. There was no defense lawyer. The decision to detain rested entirely on the discretion of the local police officer.

The process was brutal in its efficiency. Police vans would sweep through markets, construction sites, and subway stations. Anyone who could not immediately produce the specific stack of documents demanded—residence permit, employment contract, household registration card—was dragged away. For many, the detention was a brief, terrifying nightmare. For others, it was a prolonged ordeal of humiliation and physical abuse.

Once in the custody center, the reality of the system shattered any remaining illusion of "care." These facilities were often overcrowded, unsanitary, and run with a paramilitary discipline that prioritized extraction over welfare. Detainees were held until their families could be contacted and money could be wired to cover the cost of their repatriation. The state charged fees for the detention itself, for the food provided, and for the bus or train ticket to send the person home. For a migrant worker living day-to-day, earning perhaps a few dollars a day, these fees were insurmountable. Many were held for weeks, sometimes months, unable to pay their way out. They became de facto prisoners, forced to perform unpaid labor within the detention centers to offset their costs, or simply left to rot in cells while their families in the villages scrambled to raise funds.

The human cost of this system was staggering, though the numbers were often obscured by official secrecy. Throughout the 1990s and early 2000s, millions of people passed through C&R centers annually. In a single year, it is estimated that over 1.2 million people were detained. The demographics were specific: the vast majority were young men from rural provinces, the very workforce driving the nation's construction boom. They were the bricklayers, the welders, the cooks, the sanitation workers. They were the invisible engine of the city, rendered invisible by the law once they stepped out of line.

Violence was not an anomaly; it was a feature of the system. Reports from former detainees and human rights observers documented a culture of impunity. Guards faced no oversight. Beatings were common, used to enforce discipline or simply to vent frustration. Sexual abuse of female detainees was a persistent, though rarely documented, horror. The lack of legal recourse meant that complaints vanished into the black box of the detention center. If a person died in custody, it was often ruled as a suicide or an illness, a convenient fiction that shielded the officers from liability.

The system also created a perverse economic incentive. Local police departments and the detention centers themselves operated on a budget that often relied on the fees collected from detainees. The more people they caught, the more revenue they generated. This financial motivation turned the C&R system into a racket, a way to extract money from the poorest citizens. Police would sometimes target specific groups, such as street peddlers or homeless individuals, not because they posed a threat to public order, but because they were easy to detain and unlikely to have the resources to challenge the detention.

The social fallout extended far beyond the detention walls. The threat of C&R created a climate of fear that permeated the lives of millions of migrant workers. It forced them into a precarious existence, where they could not seek police protection, could not rent legal housing, and could not access public services. They lived in the shadows, avoiding main roads, hiding from uniformed officers, and relying on informal networks for safety. This systemic vulnerability made them easy targets for exploitation by employers who knew they could not complain to the authorities. If a factory withheld wages or imposed dangerous working conditions, the workers had no choice but to endure it, terrified that a complaint might lead to a police raid and a trip to a C&R center.

The system's flaws were not lost on the Chinese public, but for decades, they were dismissed as necessary evils in the name of social stability. The government argued that the C&R system was essential to prevent urban chaos, to curb crime, and to manage the flow of migration. They claimed it was a humanitarian gesture, providing a safety net for the destitute. But the gap between the rhetoric and the reality was a chasm. The system did not help the destitute; it punished them. It did not curb crime; it criminalized poverty.

The turning point came with the death of Sun Zhigang. In March 2003, Sun, a 27-year-old graduate from Hubei University, moved to Guangzhou to work as a computer programmer. He had a job and a place to live, but he had not yet secured the temporary residence permit required by the city. On the afternoon of March 17, he was stopped by police. When he explained that he had just lost his wallet and could not produce his documents, he was detained. He was taken to the Guangzhou Custody and Repatriation Center.

Sun was not the first to die in such a center, but his case was different. He had friends in the media. His family raised a clamor that could not be ignored. The news of his death spread like wildfire, fueled by the burgeoning power of the internet and a growing class of investigative journalists. The details of his murder were horrific. He was beaten by other detainees under the instruction of the guards, a common practice in the centers where violence was used to enforce hierarchy. He died three days later from his injuries. The autopsy report, leaked to the press, listed multiple fractures and severe internal bleeding.

The public outrage was immediate and unprecedented. Intellectuals, lawyers, and ordinary citizens demanded answers. Three prominent legal scholars, including Xu Zhiyuan, submitted a formal legal petition to the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress, arguing that the C&R system violated the Chinese Constitution and the Law on Administrative Punishment. They pointed out that the system allowed for the deprivation of personal liberty without judicial review, a direct contradiction of the rule of law. The petition was a masterstroke of legal strategy, framing the issue not as a political rebellion, but as a defense of the Constitution itself.

The government, facing a crisis of legitimacy, had no choice but to act. The silence that had protected the system for twenty years was broken. On June 18, 2003, just three months after Sun's death, the State Council announced the abolition of the Custody and Repatriation system. The regulation was repealed, and a new system, the "Custody and Education" system for specific categories of offenders, was proposed, though it too would later face scrutiny. The C&R centers were closed, and the vast machinery of detention that had moved millions of people was dismantled.

The abolition of C&R was a landmark victory for civil society in China. It demonstrated that the state could be held accountable, that the voice of the people could force change, and that the law could be a tool for justice rather than just an instrument of control. It was a moment of profound hope. For the millions of migrant workers who had lived in fear, the day of Sun Zhigang's death became a day of liberation. They could walk the streets of Beijing and Shanghai without the constant anxiety of being swept up by the police. They could seek employment without the shadow of deportation hanging over them.

But the story of C&R did not end with its abolition. The underlying problems it addressed—the hukou system, the inequality between urban and rural citizens, the lack of social safety nets for migrants—remained largely unresolved. The abolition of the detention centers did not grant migrant workers full citizenship rights. They still struggled to access education for their children, to buy homes, or to receive medical care. The structural discrimination that had fueled the C&R system persisted, even if the specific mechanism of mass detention was gone.

Moreover, the vacuum left by the C&R system was not entirely empty. Local governments, desperate to maintain order and manage the flow of people, found new ways to control the migrant population. New regulations were introduced, often more subtle but no less restrictive. The police continued to target "suspicious" individuals, using other legal pretexts to detain and deport. The fear did not vanish; it just changed shape. The legacy of C&R is a reminder that the struggle for human rights is never a single battle, but a long, arduous campaign against the entrenched structures of power.

The memory of Sun Zhigang and the millions who suffered under the C&R system remains a potent symbol in China. It is a testament to the resilience of the human spirit and the power of public outcry. But it is also a warning. The system that killed Sun was built on a foundation of indifference, a belief that the poor and the marginalized were disposable. It was a system that valued order over justice, efficiency over humanity. The abolition of C&R was a victory, but the work is far from over. As long as there are people who are treated as less than human simply because of where they were born, the ghost of the C&R system will linger.

Today, the C&R system is a chapter in history books, a dark period in China's rapid modernization. But the lessons it teaches are timeless. They speak to the dangers of unchecked administrative power, the importance of due process, and the moral imperative to treat every individual with dignity. The story of Sun Zhigang is not just about one man's death; it is about the thousands who survived, the millions who lived in fear, and the courage it took to say "enough." It is a story of a system that tried to erase people, and a people who refused to be erased.

The abolition of the C&R system marked the end of an era, but it also marked the beginning of a new struggle. The fight for the rights of migrant workers continues, in the courts, in the media, and in the streets. The memory of the C&R system serves as a constant reminder of what happens when the law is weaponized against the weak. It is a reminder that justice is not a gift from the state, but a right that must be claimed, defended, and protected by the people themselves.

In the end, the story of Custody and Repatriation is a story of the collision between two Chinas: the China of the official narrative, of stability and progress, and the China of the streets, of the struggling and the vulnerable. It is a story of how the state tried to bridge that gap with force, and how the people bridged it with truth. The death of Sun Zhigang was a tragedy, but his legacy is a triumph. It proved that even in the face of overwhelming power, the truth can prevail. It proved that the law can be a shield, not a sword. And it proved that no one, no matter how poor or powerless, is invisible.
