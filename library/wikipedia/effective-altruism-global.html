In September 2014, a group of roughly 250 people gathered in Berkeley, California, not to celebrate a holiday or to mourn a loss, but to rigorously debate the moral obligations of the living. They came from thirty countries, representing a spectrum of careers from quantitative finance to software engineering, yet they were united by a single, unsettling question: if you could save a life, how would you know you were doing it effectively? This gathering was the inaugural Effective Altruism Global (EAG) conference, an event that would grow to become the intellectual and social engine room for a movement dedicated to using evidence and reason to do the most good possible. The room was not filled with the usual platitudes of charity; instead, it buzzed with the friction of high-stakes utilitarianism, where the value of a human life was measured in disability-adjusted life years and the cost-effectiveness of a mosquito net was weighed against the potential of eradicating a pandemic. The founders of this movement, deeply influenced by the philosophical work of Peter Singer and the organizational vision of William MacAskill, had realized that the traditional non-profit sector was plagued by inefficiency, sentimentality, and a lack of rigorous data. They sought to build something new, a community where the primary metric of success was not the emotional resonance of a fundraising campaign, but the cold, hard calculus of impact. The Berkeley conference was the first concrete manifestation of this ambition, a proof of concept that a global network of rationalists could organize, deliberate, and act with a level of coordination previously unseen in the philanthropic world.

The origins of EAG are inextricably linked to the rise of the Effective Altruism movement itself, which began to coalesce in the early 2010s. Before the first conference, the community was fragmented, existing primarily in online forums and small, scattered meetups. The need for a physical gathering was driven by the realization that the ideas being discussed—long-termism, x-risk (existential risk), and effective giving—were too complex to be fully fleshed out in text-based interactions. The organizers needed a space where the most pressing questions could be debated in real-time, where the skeptics could challenge the optimists, and where the data could be scrutinized by peers. The decision to host the event in Berkeley was strategic; it placed the gathering at the intersection of Silicon Valley's technological optimism and the Bay Area's long history of radical social activism. The initial funding came from a handful of early donors who believed that the movement was worth investing in, seeing the conference not as a party, but as a critical infrastructure project for a new kind of moral philosophy in action.

As the years passed, the scope of Effective Altruism Global expanded dramatically, mirroring the growth of the movement itself. By 2016, the conference had split into regional events, with major gatherings taking place in San Francisco, London, and Sydney, reflecting the global nature of the community. The number of attendees swelled from the initial 250 to over a thousand in some years. These were not casual conferences where speakers pontificated from a stage while the audience passively absorbed information. EAG was designed to be participatory, a massive workshop where the attendees were the primary resource. The agenda was dense, covering topics ranging from the ethics of artificial general intelligence to the logistics of deworming programs in sub-Saharan Africa. The sessions were often intense, characterized by a distinctive culture of steelmanning—where participants would argue the strongest possible version of an opposing view—rather than the usual adversarial debate. This culture was a deliberate attempt to foster intellectual honesty and to ensure that the movement's conclusions were robust enough to withstand the most rigorous scrutiny.

The structure of the conferences evolved to accommodate this growing complexity. A typical EAG event would feature a mix of plenary talks, breakout sessions, and networking opportunities, but the most valuable parts were often the informal conversations that happened in the hallways and over coffee. These interactions allowed for the cross-pollination of ideas between different sub-communities. A software engineer working on AI safety might sit next to a doctor specializing in tropical medicine, and their conversation could lead to a new perspective on how to allocate resources or a new idea for a research project. The conferences also served as a recruitment ground for the movement's most ambitious projects. Many of the organizations that would go on to become pillars of the effective altruism ecosystem, such as the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, the Future of Humanity Institute, and various effective giving charities, found their early talent and funding at these gatherings. The conferences were a marketplace of ideas, but also a marketplace of careers, where young graduates were encouraged to dedicate their lives to high-impact problems rather than to high-paying jobs in finance or tech.

One of the most significant aspects of EAG was its focus on the long term. While many charitable organizations focus on immediate, visible suffering, the effective altruism community was deeply concerned with risks that could threaten the future of humanity itself. The concept of existential risk became a central theme of the conferences, particularly after the rise of concerns about unaligned artificial intelligence. Speakers like Nick Bostrom and Toby Ord brought these abstract concepts down to earth, arguing that the survival of the human species was the most important moral priority of our time. This focus on the long term often put the community at odds with traditional philanthropists, who were more concerned with the needs of the present generation. The EAG conferences provided a safe space for these controversial ideas to be explored and developed, allowing the community to refine its arguments and build a coalition around the idea that preventing human extinction was the most effective use of resources.

The human element of these conferences was often overlooked by outsiders, who saw the movement as coldly rational and devoid of emotion. However, those who attended knew that the drive behind EAG was deeply emotional. The attendees were motivated by a profound sense of moral urgency, a feeling that the world was on the brink of catastrophe and that they had a duty to do something about it. This urgency was not born out of fear, but out of a deep care for the future. The conversations at EAG were filled with a sense of shared purpose, a collective determination to make the world a better place in the most effective way possible. The conferences were a place where people found their community, where they found the support they needed to pursue difficult and often lonely paths, and where they found the courage to challenge the status quo. The emotional intensity of these gatherings was a testament to the power of the ideas being discussed, and to the dedication of the people who believed in them.

The impact of EAG extended far beyond the walls of the conference centers. The ideas that were debated and refined at these events went on to shape the policies of major foundations, the careers of thousands of individuals, and the direction of entire industries. The effective altruism movement, fueled by the momentum of these conferences, has directed hundreds of millions of dollars toward high-impact causes, from malaria prevention to global poverty reduction to AI safety research. The conferences have also played a crucial role in shaping the public discourse around these issues, bringing concepts like long-termism and x-risk into the mainstream. The growth of EAG from a small gathering in Berkeley to a global phenomenon is a testament to the power of ideas to change the world, and to the power of community to turn those ideas into action.

Yet, the movement has not been without its challenges. As EAG grew, it faced internal tensions and external criticisms. Some within the community worried that the focus on rationality and data was leading to a neglect of the emotional and psychological aspects of moral action. Others worried that the movement was becoming too insular, too focused on its own internal debates and too disconnected from the broader world. There were also criticisms from outside the movement, with some accusing the effective altruists of being overly utilitarian, of ignoring the nuances of human suffering, and of being too willing to sacrifice the needs of the present for the sake of a hypothetical future. These criticisms were not always wrong, and the EAG community had to grapple with them honestly. The conferences became a place for these internal and external critiques to be addressed, for the movement to self-correct and to evolve. The willingness to engage with these criticisms was a sign of the movement's maturity and its commitment to truth over comfort.

The logistics of organizing such a massive event were a feat in themselves. The team behind EAG had to manage everything from venue selection and catering to speaker coordination and visa applications for international attendees. The conferences were expensive, requiring significant funding to support the travel and accommodation of attendees from around the world. This funding came from a variety of sources, including individual donors, foundations, and corporate sponsors. The financial sustainability of the conferences was a constant concern, but the community was willing to make the investment, seeing the conferences as a critical tool for the movement's growth. The organizers worked tirelessly to ensure that the conferences were accessible to everyone, regardless of their financial situation, and to create an inclusive environment where people from all backgrounds could participate. The diversity of the attendees was a source of strength for the movement, bringing together people with different perspectives and experiences to tackle the world's most pressing problems.

Looking back at the history of EAG, it is clear that the conferences have played a pivotal role in the development of the effective altruism movement. They have provided a space for the community to grow, to learn, and to act. They have been a catalyst for change, inspiring individuals to dedicate their lives to the most important causes of our time. The conferences have been a place where ideas are born, where friendships are formed, and where the future of humanity is debated. They have been a testament to the power of reason, of evidence, and of compassion. The story of EAG is the story of a group of people who decided that they could not stand by and watch the world suffer when they had the power to do something about it. They chose to use their skills, their resources, and their time to make a difference, and they did it together. The conferences were the heart of this effort, the place where the movement came alive and where the work of saving the world truly began.

The legacy of EAG is still being written. As the world faces new and unprecedented challenges, from climate change to the rise of artificial intelligence, the need for a movement dedicated to effective action is greater than ever. The conferences will continue to evolve, adapting to the changing needs of the community and the world. They will continue to be a place where the most important questions are asked, where the most difficult problems are solved, and where the most effective solutions are found. The story of EAG is a reminder that even in the face of overwhelming odds, a group of people who are willing to think clearly and act effectively can change the world. It is a story of hope, of courage, and of the enduring power of human kindness. The next chapter of this story is being written right now, in the minds and hearts of the people who are preparing for the next EAG conference, ready to take on the challenges of the future with the same determination and passion that has driven the movement since its inception in a small room in Berkeley. The work is far from over, but the path forward is clear, and the community is ready to walk it together.
