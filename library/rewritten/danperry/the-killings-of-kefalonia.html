Dan Perry delivers a haunting revelation: the most tragic rupture between Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy did not happen in Berlin or Rome, but on the green, Italian-influenced hills of a Greek island where the scent of blood still lingers in local memory. This piece is notable not for recounting a battle, but for exposing how the collapse of an alliance in 1943 instantly stripped away a fragile layer of protection for Greek Jews, linking two histories—the massacre of Italian soldiers and the Holocaust—that are rarely taught together.

The Unlikely Shield of an Ally

Perry begins by dismantling the monolithic view of the Axis powers, noting that "after Mussolini's regime collapsed in 1943, Germany turned viciously on its former ally." He argues that the specific geography of the Ionian Sea created a unique historical anomaly. Unlike the arid, sun-baked Aegean islands most tourists know, Kefalonia feels like a bridge to the Adriatic, shaped by centuries of Venetian rule. This cultural proximity meant that for years, Italian occupation authorities in Greece had "generally resisted German efforts to deport Jews from the territories they controlled."

This framing is crucial because it reframes the Holocaust in Greece not as a singular German operation, but as a process interrupted and then accelerated by geopolitical shifts. Perry writes, "This produced the strange situation in which at various junctures Jews could find relative safety by moving from German-occupied Greece into Italian-held territory." The argument lands with force because it highlights the contingency of survival; safety was not a moral choice by the occupiers, but a bureaucratic friction between two fascist regimes.

"The German takeover of Italian Greece thus links two histories that are usually remembered separately: the slaughter of the Acqui Division and the destruction of the remaining Jews of Greece."

Critics might note that emphasizing Italian "protection" risks softening the narrative of Fascist occupation, yet Perry is careful to specify that this was a "strange situation" born of conflict between allies, not benevolence. When the armistice was signed on September 8, 1943, that friction vanished. The roughly 12,000 men of the Italian Acqui Division who resisted German disarmament were executed in one of the largest massacres of prisoners of war in history. But as Perry points out, the human cost extended far beyond the battlefield: "Jewish communities that had survived the first years of the war were suddenly exposed to the machinery of the Holocaust."

A Geography of Erasure

The piece excels in connecting the local tragedy of Kefalonia to the broader destruction of Greek Jewry. Perry reminds readers that "Greece lost at least 80 percent of its prewar Jewish population, one of the highest proportions in occupied Europe." He contrasts the fate of different islands to illustrate the randomness of survival. On Corfu, "nearly all of its roughly 1,800 Jews" were deported to Auschwitz, while on Zakynthos, the local mayor and bishop refused to provide a list of names, and "all 275 Jews survived."

This juxtaposition is effective because it refuses to treat the Holocaust as a monolith of inevitability. Instead, Perry shows how local agency and administrative control determined life and death. He notes that Thessaloniki, once a great Sephardic center, saw "the overwhelming majority" of its 50,000 Jews deported to Auschwitz-Birkenau. The destruction was so total that "it is easy today to visit Greece repeatedly without quite appreciating what disappeared."

"Jews had lived in the Greek world for more than 2,000 years. The Romaniote communities were among the oldest Jewish communities in Europe, predating the arrival of the Sephardim by many centuries. In a few years, most of that world was destroyed."

The historical depth here is significant. By mentioning the Romaniote communities, Perry underscores that this was not just the loss of a population, but the erasure of a distinct cultural lineage that predated the Spanish expulsion of 1492. The loss of this community meant the end of a 2,000-year-old presence, a fact that often gets lost in the broader statistics of the war.

The Hinge of History

Perry then expands the lens further, arguing that the intervention in Greece was not a sideshow but a strategic pivot that altered the course of the war. He suggests that the German distraction in the Balkans "may have helped push the launch of Operation Barbarossa... into late June 1941." This delay, he argues, had catastrophic ripple effects. Had the invasion of the Soviet Union begun earlier, the German forces might have reached the Caucasus and converged with Rommel's Afrika Korps in Egypt.

This is a bold historical claim, and while Perry admits "historians still debate how decisive that delay proved," the logic is compelling. The stakes were existential for the Jewish population of Palestine. "The Nazis had already assembled an SS Einsatzkommando to follow a German conquest of Egypt and Palestine and extend the machinery of the Holocaust there." The fact that this never happened was not due to a lack of intent, but because the German advance was halted at El Alamein and in the Soviet Union.

"Greece thus occupied an extraordinary place in this larger geographical puzzle of the war — a battlefield at the hinge between Hitler's campaigns in Europe, Russia and the Mediterranean, and a Jewish community in Palestine that came perilously close to the expanding reach of Nazi Germany."

This section elevates the piece from a local history of Kefalonia to a global strategic analysis. It forces the reader to reconsider the Balkans not as a peripheral theater, but as a critical choke point that, if lost, could have extended the Holocaust to the Middle East.

Echoes in the Present

The narrative takes a sharp turn in the final section, where Perry connects the historical trauma to the present-day tensions on the island. He describes the "golden beaches" and "lovely tavernas" of Fiskardo, but notes that "current events are never far behind." He recounts a conversation with a bartender who, upon learning he is from Tel Aviv, stated, "I support the other side" because of "genocide." Perry challenges her, asking, "Do you really think that, of all the wars in the world today, this is the only one that is a 'genocide' — like the Armenian genocide, like the Holocaust, like Rwanda?"

This confrontation is uncomfortable but necessary. It illustrates how the memory of the Holocaust is weaponized in modern conflicts, often stripping the historical event of its specific context to serve contemporary political narratives. Perry notes that the witnesses to the 1943 massacre are almost gone, leaving only oral traditions like the story of a grandmother who claimed you could "smell the blood all the way to the village."

"The older generation that lived a little closer to it talked about the massacre... There's not a lot of them left now."

The juxtaposition of the 1943 massacre with modern graffiti and political slogans creates a dissonant rhythm. It suggests that while the physical scars of war and earthquake have healed on Kefalonia, the ideological wounds remain raw. The bartender's refusal to engage with the nuance of Perry's argument highlights a broader trend: the flattening of complex history into binary moral judgments.

"There's someone standing in front of you who came from there this week. Do you want to hear what I have to say?"

This moment of attempted dialogue, ending in a shared drink but unresolved disagreement, serves as a poignant metaphor for the difficulty of transmitting historical truth in an era of polarized memory. Perry does not offer a neat resolution, which makes the ending more powerful.

Bottom Line

Perry's strongest argument is the structural link between the collapse of the Italian alliance and the acceleration of the Holocaust in Greece, a connection that transforms a local tragedy into a pivotal moment of the war. The piece's vulnerability lies in its reliance on oral history for the massacre's visceral impact, which, while evocative, lacks the documentary weight of the broader statistical analysis. Readers should watch for how this specific historical hinge is invoked in future debates about the Balkans and the memory of the Holocaust.
