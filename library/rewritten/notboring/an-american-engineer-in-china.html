Packy McCormick's latest dispatch from Not Boring bypasses the usual geopolitical noise to deliver something far more visceral: a ground-level report on the sheer, overwhelming velocity of Chinese industrial evolution. Rather than relying on sanitized press releases or Washington think-tank projections, McCormick introduces us to "Matthew," an American engineer whose journey from tourist to factory insider reveals a landscape where the rules of global competition are being rewritten in real-time. This is not a story about a rival nation; it is a story about a different operating system for technology and manufacturing that the West is only just beginning to perceive.

The Scale of the Shift

McCormick frames the narrative around a fundamental cognitive gap. He argues that Western observers are stuck in a 2010s mental model, while the reality on the ground has already sprinted toward 2036. The evidence isn't abstract; it is architectural and demographic. "The China of 2036 will be vastly different than the China of 2026," McCormick writes, noting that the sheer density of the environment forces a re-evaluation of what is possible. He cites the staggering statistic that Hong Kong alone contains 4,000 buildings over 100 meters, compared to New York City's 900, to illustrate a physical reality that defies American intuition.

This focus on physical scale is effective because it strips away the abstraction of "growth rates" and replaces it with something the reader can visualize. The author notes that "everything else just seems so…puny" when standing in the shadow of single companies that outproduce entire national industries. This visceral reaction is the piece's strongest hook. It suggests that the challenge for Western firms isn't just policy or tariffs, but a fundamental mismatch in industrial capacity and speed.

If you aren't heavily updating your mental model of China after this piece, then you will get blindsided.

The Evolution of the Supply Chain

The commentary shifts from the macro to the micro, detailing how the nature of Chinese business has transformed. McCormick highlights a critical pivot: the move from being the world's factory floor to becoming the world's brand owner. "Companies that used to manufacture luxury handbags are now becoming the brands themselves," he observes, a transition that upends the traditional division of labor where the West designs and the East assembles.

This argument is bolstered by the specific context of Shenzhen, the city that embodies this shift. McCormick's source draws a direct line to the city's origins, noting it was "Deng Xiaoping's SEZ 40 years ago," a deliberate experiment in special economic zones that has now matured into a self-sustaining ecosystem. The density of suppliers in Shenzhen is described as "unmatched," creating an environment where a hardware engineer can iterate a product in days rather than months. This connects to the broader history of the region, echoing the rapid transformation seen in the Huaqiangbei electronics markets, where the boundary between component supplier and finished product blurs.

However, the piece also acknowledges the friction this creates for outsiders. The author's friend warns that "no foreigner will ever get government subsidies," creating a ceiling on what external actors can achieve without deep local integration. Critics might argue that this reliance on state-backed playbooks and subsidies creates long-term inefficiencies or market distortions that will eventually correct themselves. Yet, the immediate reality described by McCormick is one where these mechanisms are currently accelerating innovation at a pace that ignores such theoretical corrections.

The Human Cost of Speed

Perhaps the most striking element of McCormick's reporting is the recognition of the human ecosystem that powers this machine. The narrative doesn't shy away from the social implications of rapid industrialization. He describes a scene where food delivery drivers, essential to the city's low-cost logistics, are often seen scrolling through social media while waiting for orders, a detail that hints at the precariousness of gig work. "If Meituan tried replacing them all with drone delivery, there would be so many riots in the streets," the source notes, referencing the massive food delivery platform Meituan.

This observation is crucial. It grounds the high-tech narrative in the reality of employment and social stability. The drive for automation and efficiency is tempered by the sheer number of people whose livelihoods depend on the current, labor-intensive model. McCormick's framing here is empathetic; he recognizes that the "speed" of the Chinese economy is not just a metric of output, but a function of millions of individual lives moving in unison. The piece avoids the trap of viewing these workers merely as cogs, instead presenting them as a stabilizing force that the system cannot easily discard.

The Intellectual Challenge

The core of McCormick's argument is that the West's biggest failure is intellectual, not just industrial. He posits that the process of learning is often more valuable than the data itself. "The process is often more illuminating than the output," he writes, describing how his source went from asking the wrong questions to realizing the need for a new framework entirely. This is a call for humility. The author suggests that Western companies are blinded by their own assumptions, treating China as a "cartoonish adversary" rather than a complex, heterogeneous ecosystem.

McCormick emphasizes that "China is heterogeneous," with "so many different dialects, dresses, jobs, climates, educational attainments, and personalities" that it defies simple categorization. This nuance is often lost in media coverage that focuses on monolithic state actions. By focusing on the diversity of the landscape, McCormick makes a compelling case that a one-size-fits-all strategy is doomed to fail. The argument is strengthened by the specific detail of the "Weeb Wall" and the anime culture in Shenzhen, showing a society that is simultaneously hyper-modern, deeply traditional, and culturally distinct from the West.

Understanding China as more than a cartoonish adversary is critical for anyone who wishes to compete with its companies.

Bottom Line

Packy McCormick's "An American engineer in China" succeeds by replacing geopolitical abstraction with on-the-ground texture, forcing readers to confront the reality that the center of gravity in hardware and manufacturing has shifted irrevocably. Its greatest strength is the refusal to treat China as a monolith, instead revealing a complex, rapidly evolving ecosystem where scale, speed, and social dynamics intersect. The piece's vulnerability lies in its reliance on the perspective of a single engineer, which, while insightful, may not capture the full breadth of regional disparities or the long-term sustainability of the current growth model. For the busy reader, the verdict is clear: the era of easy assumptions is over, and the only way to stay ahead is to understand the machinery of this new world, not just its output.
