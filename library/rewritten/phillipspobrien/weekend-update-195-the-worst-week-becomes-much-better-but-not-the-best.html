O'Brien delivers a rare, granular look at how a democracy under siege can correct its own course in real time, arguing that Ukraine's recent military leadership shakeup was not a crisis of command but a triumph of civil pressure. While many observers focus on the front lines, this piece illuminates the internal political mechanics that forced a change in the General Staff, revealing a system where public trust still dictates military appointments.

The Mechanics of Democratic Correction

The core of the argument rests on the idea that Ukraine's ability to adapt is its greatest strategic asset. O'Brien writes, "Sometimes we forget that democracies have certain inbuilt advantages in war... but democracies can also be better at adapting and channelling societal abilities and commitment." This framing is crucial because it shifts the narrative from a story of political instability to one of institutional resilience. The author details how protests, initially sparked by the dismissal of Defense Minister Mykhailo Fedorov, evolved to demand the removal of General Oleksandr Syrskyi, the then-commander of the armed forces.

The tension between the political leadership and the military command had reached a breaking point. Fedorov, in a press conference following his dismissal, accused Syrskyi of fracturing the military's unity. He stated that Syrskyi was "splitting the country" by favoring certain units over others based on loyalty rather than need. O'Brien highlights the severity of this claim, noting that "if you are loyal, you receive supplies; if you are not, you receive nothing." This revelation of a patronage system within the supply chain explains why the public outcry was so fierce. Critics might note that public pressure on military leadership can be double-edged, potentially undermining the chain of command in the short term, but the evidence presented here suggests the alternative—a fractured, unaccountable military—was far more dangerous.

"Only three positions in the state—in addition to soldiers on the battlefield—which determine the course of the war: president, defence minister and commander-in-chief."

This quote from Fedorov underscores the high stakes of the political standoff. O'Brien explains that while President Zelensky attempted to placate Fedorov with advisory roles, the former minister refused, understanding that real power resides only in the executive posts. The resulting compromise saw the appointment of Acting Defense Minister Evgeniy Khmara, a move that leaves the political situation in a state of suspended animation. The author notes that while Khmara is respected, the refusal of Fedorov to return means the "worst week has not transformed into the best," leaving a lingering uncertainty about the full restoration of trust between the political and military spheres.

A New Commander for a New Era

The appointment of Major General Mykhailo Drapatyi as the new overall commander is presented as the week's most significant development. O'Brien describes Drapatyi as a figure who is "universally respected and admired from practically all parts of society," a rare consensus in a polarized environment. The author emphasizes Drapatyi's generational difference from his predecessor, noting he is "17 years younger than Syrskyi and leaving him with no Soviet military experience." This distinction is vital in a conflict where the old Soviet doctrine of mass casualties is increasingly at odds with modern, precision-based warfare.

Drapatyi's reputation was cemented by a specific act of accountability. In 2025, he resigned as commander of the Ground Forces after a Russian strike killed recruits, stating, "an army in which no one is held accountable is dying from within." O'Brien argues that this willingness to take responsibility for the loss of life under his command signals a fundamental shift in military culture. Drapatyi has promised "no loud promises," a stark contrast to the performative politics that often surround such appointments. His background includes the 2014 defense of Mariupol, where he reportedly led a breakthrough that saved surrounded police, and a recent focus on the "Drone Line" project, which aims to create a 10–15 km kill zone behind the front lines.

The author connects this leadership change to broader institutional reforms, suggesting that Drapatyi is poised to continue the professionalization of the armed forces. This aligns with historical precedents where Ukraine has successfully integrated new technologies and decentralized command structures, similar to the adaptations seen in the General Staff's earlier deep dives into the Sea of Azov operations. The reception to Drapatyi's appointment has been overwhelmingly positive, with 55% of respondents approving the move. O'Brien writes, "The Ukrainian army needs a leader who will continue to head down the road of professionalization, fair treatment and no favoritism, and the integration of new technologies." This assessment suggests that the military is finally aligning its leadership with the realities of a modern, high-tech war.

The Cost of Strategic Distraction

While the leadership changes offer hope, O'Brien offers a sobering critique of Ukraine's recent offensive campaigns, specifically the strikes on Wildberries warehouses. The author details a series of attacks on Russian logistics hubs, noting that the campaign has intensified since mid-July. However, O'Brien questions the strategic efficiency of these strikes, writing, "If it distracts from the oil campaign, that is definitely not what I would say is an efficient use of Ukrainian resources." The piece points out that while the Wildberries strikes have caused disruption, there have been only five strikes on refineries since the campaign began, despite oil prices rising.

This section of the commentary serves as a necessary counterweight to the optimism surrounding the leadership change. It reminds the reader that while the internal political machinery is functioning, the external military strategy still faces difficult choices. The author notes that the Wildberries campaign, while damaging to the Russian economy, may not be the most effective use of scarce long-range assets when compared to the direct impact of targeting energy infrastructure. This tension between political signaling and military necessity is a recurring theme in the war, and O'Brien's willingness to question the utility of these strikes adds significant depth to the analysis.

"You can't even call them a neighbour. This is a nation, along with its leadership, that has no right to exist."

This quote from Drapatyi, regarding the Russian imperial mindset, highlights the ideological clarity that the new commander brings to the conflict. O'Brien contextualizes this statement, clarifying that it refers to the imperial ambitions of the leadership rather than the Russian people, a distinction that is often lost in translation. The author suggests that this hard-core stance, combined with Drapatyi's focus on accountability and technology, positions him to lead the army through the next phase of the war with a clear sense of purpose.

Bottom Line

Phillips P. O'Brien's analysis provides a compelling narrative of a democracy successfully correcting its course under fire, with the appointment of Drapatyi serving as a beacon of hope for military professionalism. However, the piece's greatest vulnerability lies in its unresolved tension between the political standoff over the Defense Ministry and the strategic ambiguity of the current offensive campaigns. Readers should watch closely to see if the new command structure can translate its moral authority into tangible gains on the battlefield, particularly as the administration balances the demands of public trust with the harsh realities of resource allocation.
