Natalie Wexler delivers a jarring correction to decades of educational dogma: the missing link in reading comprehension isn't a lack of vocabulary or a failure to "make inferences," but a fundamental inability to parse complex sentence structures. While schools have long treated reading as a set of isolated skills, Wexler argues that the real bottleneck for students hitting a wall in middle school is their inability to untangle the "syntactic signals" that hold meaning together. This is not just a technical observation; it is a call to dismantle the silos between speech therapy and classroom instruction, offering a surprisingly manageable path forward for a problem that has plagued educators for generations.

The Hidden Architecture of Meaning

The prevailing wisdom in schools has long been that reading instruction should focus on strategies like predicting or summarizing, divorced from specific content. Wexler dismantles this, noting that "for decades, reading comprehension instruction focused primarily on having students practice skills and strategies... in isolation from any particular content, contrary to what the research indicates will work." She acknowledges a shift toward content-rich instruction but argues it remains incomplete. The missing piece, she insists, is "familiarity with complex syntax, or sentence structure."

This framing is crucial because it explains the "fourth-grade slump" where a child who reads fluently suddenly cannot comprehend the text. Wexler points out that "the syntax of written language is more complex than the syntax of oral language," and this gap widens as students age. A student might decode every word perfectly but still fail to understand who is doing what to whom in a sentence like "The cat that the rat had bitten played on the ship." This disconnect is often invisible to teachers because "the problem often doesn't become apparent until students reach higher grade levels, after reading instruction is assumed to be over."

The article highlights a structural failure in the scientific community: "Syntax comprehension problems clearly do affect reading ability, but they're often categorized as a 'language impairment' rather than a reading disorder." This categorization creates a tragic gap where speech pathologists treat the issue, but classroom teachers lack the training to address it. It is a systemic oversight that leaves millions of students without the tools to navigate the increasingly complex texts of high school and beyond.

"The biggest obstacle for the majority of typically developing children in this age group is not understanding the individual words in texts, but using syntactic signals to connect the word meaning into sentence meaning."

Evidence That Turns the Tables

Wexler anchors her argument in a compelling new study involving Danish sixth graders, which managed to "disentangle" the roles of vocabulary and syntax. By using sentences with simple vocabulary but complex structures, researchers found that "syntax comprehension was 'by far the best predictor of reading comprehension,' exceeding vocabulary by a significant margin." This finding challenges the traditional hierarchy that prioritizes vocabulary acquisition above all else.

The study's methodology is particularly elegant. Students were asked to identify the actor in passive-voice sentences like "George was kicked by Sam under the table." The results showed that "some children who had strong vocabularies weren't able to parse complex sentence structure, and vice versa." This proves that syntax is a distinct cognitive skill, not just a byproduct of knowing more words. It suggests that a student can know every word in a sentence and still miss the meaning entirely if they cannot process the grammatical relationships.

Critics might argue that this study relies on a specific linguistic context (Danish) and may not fully translate to English, which has different syntactic rules. However, Wexler counters this by pointing to the evolution of English texts themselves. She notes that a study from 1982 found the average newspaper editorial sentence contained three clauses, and while sentences may have simplified slightly, "it's still not hard to find a complex sentence in a newspaper." The complexity is inherent in the medium of academic and professional writing, making this a universal challenge, not a niche one.

From Diagnosis to Instruction

The most actionable part of Wexler's commentary is her rejection of traditional grammar drills. She warns that "one thing it should not mean is just having students memorize the difference between the active and the passive voice or the names of different grammatical structures." Knowing the definition of a "subordinating conjunction" does not guarantee a student can understand a sentence that uses one. Instead, she advocates for "sentence-combining," where students actively construct complex sentences from simple ones.

This approach aligns with the concept of "syntactic bootstrapping," where children learn word meanings by analyzing the structure of the sentence they appear in. By engaging in the active construction of sentences, students internalize the rules of syntax rather than just memorizing them. Wexler suggests that "if you explicitly teach students how to use constructions like appositives and the passive voice in their own writing, they'll be in a much better position to understand those constructions when they encounter them in their reading."

The article also draws on the work of Cheryl M. Scott, a speech-and-language pathologist, to illustrate how subtle syntax errors can derail comprehension. Scott recounts a student who misread "Appalachian Mountains" as the subject of a verb that actually belonged to "land" because the noun phrases were too far apart. Wexler uses this to argue that teachers must "guide the whole class through the reading rather than to expect individual students to overcome syntax barriers on their own." This is a profound shift from the "independent reading" model that dominates many classrooms.

"Rules of syntax, on the other hand, are relatively limited in number. If teachers weave syntax comprehension into whatever texts they're teaching, they may well find that students are better able to transfer those skills to new texts—and as a result are better able to learn."

The Bottom Line

Wexler's argument is a powerful corrective that moves the needle from abstract "reading strategies" to the concrete mechanics of language. Its greatest strength is the evidence that syntax is a distinct, teachable skill that can be addressed without endless drilling, offering a relatively efficient way to boost comprehension across all subjects. However, the biggest vulnerability lies in the implementation: it requires a level of cross-curricular coordination and teacher training that the current educational infrastructure is ill-equipped to provide. For busy educators, the promise is that syntax instruction is a high-leverage, finite set of rules, but the reality demands a complete rethinking of how reading is taught from the ground up.
