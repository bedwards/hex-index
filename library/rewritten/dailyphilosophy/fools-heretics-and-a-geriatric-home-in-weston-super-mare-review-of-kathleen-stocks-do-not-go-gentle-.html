This review transforms a dry legislative debate into a profound philosophical clash, asking whether the value of human life is absolute or contingent on quality. Andreas Matthias doesn't just summarize Kathleen Stock's new book; he dissects the emotional and logical fault lines that make this upcoming UK legislation so volatile. By framing the opposition as a battle between "fools" who misread empirical risks and "heretics" who challenge the sanctity of life, the piece forces readers to confront the uncomfortable reality that some debates have no middle ground.

The Architecture of Disagreement

Matthias opens by invoking a quip from novelist Kingsley Amis to set the stakes: "No pleasure is worth giving up for the sake of two more years in a geriatric home in Weston-super-Mare." This sharp cultural reference immediately grounds the abstract policy debate in the visceral fear of a diminished existence. He argues that opponents of assisted dying often cling to the idea that life has intrinsic value even in dire biological decline, a stance that clashes directly with the lived reality of those suffering.

To explain why this debate feels so intractable, Matthias turns to Ludwig Wittgenstein's On Certainty, noting that "Where two principles really do meet which cannot be reconciled with one another, then each man declares the other a fool and a heretic." This historical framing, reminiscent of the rigid theological divides seen in the history of organizations like Dignitas, suggests that the current UK debate is not merely about policy mechanics but about incompatible worldviews. Matthias writes, "Such irreconcilable differences can also be seen when assessing some of the most determined positions in the assisted dying debates, be they for or against."

The distinction between the "fool" and the "heretic" is crucial here. Matthias suggests that calling someone a fool implies a failure to grasp consequences—a factual error—while calling them a heretic implies a rejection of a fundamental value. In this context, opponents view proponents as fools for ignoring the risk of abuse, while proponents view opponents as heretics for prioritizing biological existence over human autonomy. As Matthias puts it, "The dispute is a factual one over, roughly speaking, the weighing of likely outcomes."

Critics might note that this binary framing risks oversimplifying the nuanced positions of moderates who see room for compromise, yet Matthias insists that the most vocal actors on both sides operate in this zone of irreconcilability.

The Polemical Edge of Stock's Argument

Turning to Kathleen Stock's specific work, Matthias acknowledges her controversial reputation while focusing on her ethical arguments against formalizing assisted death. He notes that Stock's opposition is not religious but grounded in the fear that "formal structures dedicated to helping consenting people to die" will inevitably lead to coercion. Matthias writes that Stock highlights how "some supporters of assisted death are from 'gilded worlds' of celebrity, power or wealth," suggesting the legislation could become a luxury good for the elite to avoid the costs of care.

However, Matthias is critical of Stock's tone, observing that while she accuses supporters of using "sentiment laid on with a trowel," she is "ever ready with her own trowel." He points to her use of a Boston case where a woman's abuse drove her boyfriend to suicide as an example of Stock's fear that the elderly and vulnerable will be bullied into death to save families money. Matthias paraphrases the core of this fear: opponents believe that most people with terminal diagnoses are "highly susceptible to abuse" and that legalizing the practice creates a slippery slope where the vulnerable are pressured to choose death.

This is where the argument becomes most contentious. Matthias suggests that while emotional appeals can be manipulative, they also reveal genuine suffering that policy must address. He writes, "Emotional appeals can open eyes, and in life's grubby world, we have competing anguishing cases, 'on the one hand' but 'on the other'." The tension lies in whether the risk of abuse justifies denying a legal right to those who are suffering. Matthias notes that Stock's book is "one-sided and pretty polemical," a flaw that may limit its ability to persuade those who already support the legislation.

The Heresy of Mercy

The review culminates in a discussion of the fundamental values at stake: freedom versus the sanctity of life. Matthias argues that Stock views the push for autonomy as a dangerous form of individualism, quoting her description of people as "isolated monads." He finds this objection curious, noting that Stock simultaneously argues that people choose assisted death to avoid being a burden on society—a deeply relational concern, not an isolated one.

Matthias writes, "Freedom (autonomy, 'my choice') and mercy (compassion, empathy) are two sets of fundamental values favouring legalised assisted death, but they are in tension." He observes that while proponents see mercy as a virtue, Stock interprets the desire for death as a negative value judgment on the lives of the suffering. "To challenge that value is heresy — for human life is the bedrock of values," Matthias explains, capturing the absolutist stance of the opposition.

Yet, the author also points out the hypocrisy in Stock's own value judgments. He notes that Stock readily expresses negative views about those who seek to end their lives, calling them "self-interestedly... seeking to flee from life, when the going gets tough." This reciprocal accusation of heresy highlights the depth of the divide. Matthias concludes that the debate is not about logic but about which value takes precedence: the protection of life at all costs, or the relief of suffering through autonomy.

Bottom Line

Matthias's review succeeds by exposing the philosophical chasm that legislation cannot easily bridge, framing the debate as a clash of irreconcilable first principles rather than a solvable policy puzzle. The strongest part of his argument is the distinction between factual disagreement (the fool) and value-based conflict (the heretic), which clarifies why compromise is so elusive. However, the piece's vulnerability lies in its acceptance of Stock's polemical tone as a necessary tool for the argument, potentially alienating readers seeking a more balanced assessment of the risks and benefits. As the UK prepares to revisit this legislation in autumn 2026, the real question remains whether society can find a legal framework that respects both the sanctity of life and the dignity of a painful end.
