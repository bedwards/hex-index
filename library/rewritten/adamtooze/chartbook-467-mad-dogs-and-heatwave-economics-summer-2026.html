Adam Tooze shifts the climate crisis narrative from the dramatic violence of storms to the suffocating silence of extreme heat, arguing that this invisible force is already paralyzing economies in ways insurance cannot fix. The piece delivers a chilling economic forecast: heat is not just a weather event but a structural drag on global growth that threatens to trigger stagflation and deepen inequality in the Global South.

The Economics of Immobility

Tooze begins by reframing the sensory experience of the climate crisis. While previous visions focused on "huge forces unleashed in roaring winds," his recent experience in Paris revealed a different reality: "the suffocating, blanketing, smothering, suppressing force of really high temperatures." This distinction is critical. It is not a dynamic disaster but one of "immobility, silence and emptiness," where street life and small businesses vanish under the sun's pressure. The author notes that the impact was "like a heat-imposed COVID lockdown," a comparison that underscores how quickly modern urban life can grind to a halt.

The physical infrastructure of Europe is ill-equipped for this new reality. Tooze cites a grim interview regarding France's housing stock, describing late 20th-century apartments as "death traps" that are well-insulated but lack ventilation. He highlights the terrifying slang term for these units: "whistling kettles," a phrase that conjures "a vision straight out of Hieronymus Bosch's visions of hell." This imagery effectively bridges the gap between abstract temperature data and immediate human suffering. It is a stark reminder that the built environment, designed for a cooler past, is now actively endangering its inhabitants.

"The economic transmission of heat stress is non-linear, with a critical threshold around 30°C beyond which productivity losses intensify sharply."

The Non-Linear Cost of Heat

The core of Tooze's argument rests on data from a recent Allianz report, which quantifies the brutal math of heat stress. The analysis reveals that below 30°C, warming might modestly reduce heating costs, but above that threshold, the relationship reverses violently. "Output per hour declines by approximately USD1.3... for every degree across the 30-35°C range," Tooze writes, noting that wage adjustments lag behind, meaning firms absorb the initial shock before it hits household income.

This non-linearity creates a dangerous macroeconomic scenario. Tooze constructs a stress test where the five hottest years of the last decade are replayed in ascending order from 2026 to 2030. The projected cumulative GDP losses are staggering: "USD240bn for France, USD354bn for Japan, USD147bn for Italy." More concerning is the impact on long-term growth. "The decline in fixed capital formation systematically exceeds consumption losses, reaching 8% on average across affected countries," he explains. As heat compresses returns on capital, investment dries up, creating a self-reinforcing drag on future productive capacity.

Critics might argue that technological adaptation could lower these thresholds faster than the model predicts, but Tooze's reliance on current structural rigidities suggests a grim near-term trajectory. The fiscal consequences are equally dire. With tax revenues falling and emergency spending rising, "Italy and Spain risk breaching the Maastricht deficit ceiling (again) once heat-related pressures are incorporated." The single European monetary policy rate becomes a liability when economies face diverging climate exposures, leaving central banks in a "binding trade-off" between fighting inflation and supporting growth.

The Global South and the El Niño Layer

While the Allianz data focuses on wealthy European nations, Tooze pivots to the disproportionate impact on the developing world, particularly as an El Niño event layers on top of 1.4°C of human-driven warming. The disruption is not just meteorological but geopolitical, exacerbated by "the disruption of global fertilizer flows due to the US-Israel war on Iran." This combination threatens crop output in developing nations that are least equipped to handle it.

Commodity markets are already reacting. "As west African cocoa producers face an elevated threat of drought, prices of the chocolate ingredient have risen 37 per cent since the start of June," Tooze notes. Coffee, wheat, and rice face similar threats, with rainfall in India's crucial monsoon season already "more than a tenth below the average level." While some investment banks downplay the impact on global stock indices, Tooze warns that focusing on these aggregate statistics ignores the regional devastation. "The most severe impact on agriculture and incomes is likely to be felt not in Europe, or the rich countries... but in the developing world."

"The heightened financial market interest in the El Niño phenomenon probably won't be much help to those worst affected by it."

The human cost is quantified by the World Food Programme, which warns that this event could push at least 49 million more people into food insecurity. Tooze cites a 2023 study in Science by Callahan and Mankin, which found that major El Niño episodes cause income losses that persist for years. For Peru, income would have been 19% higher five years after the 1997-98 event had it not occurred. The global losses from past events are estimated in the trillions, far exceeding previous calculations that failed to account for long-term growth effects.

Migration and the Feedback Loop

The final piece of the puzzle connects climate stress to migration. Tooze points out that the World Food Programme identifies Central America as a hotspot for rising food insecurity, with a projected 83.1% increase in affected people. This region is the primary source of migration pressure on the southern borders of the USA. "Latin America and the Caribbean could see one of the sharpest rises in food insecurity with more than 16 million people affected," he writes. This creates a direct feedback loop where climate-induced poverty drives displacement, which in turn strains the political and social fabric of destination countries.

While hedge funds like Moreton Capital Partners are rushing to profit from these disruptions, betting on the failure of other investors to appreciate the market implications, Tooze remains skeptical of financial markets as a solution. The "structural mismatch" between what heat destroys and what insurance covers means that private capital will likely extract profits from the crisis rather than solve it. Public guarantees and subsidies are required to turn household wealth into resilience, but currently, the gap remains unfilled.

Bottom Line

Tooze's most powerful contribution is the shift from viewing heat as a dynamic disaster to recognizing it as a silent, immobilizing economic brake that threatens to unravel the fiscal stability of developed nations while devastating the Global South. The argument's greatest vulnerability lies in its reliance on current adaptation speeds; if policy action accelerates faster than the model assumes, the projected losses could be mitigated, though the immediate political will for such action appears absent. Readers should watch for how the Eurozone manages the diverging climate exposures of its member states, as this will be the first real test of whether a single monetary policy can survive a multi-speed climate crisis.
