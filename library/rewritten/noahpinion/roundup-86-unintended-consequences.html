Noah Smith delivers a sobering reality check on the gap between political rhetoric and economic reality, arguing that the most damaging policies are often those born of good intentions or ideological rigidity rather than malicious design. This roundup cuts through the noise of daily political theater to expose how measurement errors, migration dynamics, and urban policy failures are quietly reshaping the American landscape in ways few are discussing.

The Erosion of a Movement

Smith opens with a striking observation about the volatility of modern political movements. He notes that the surge of populist energy currently dominating the headlines is already showing signs of fatigue. "I've long predicted that MAGA is a wave that would ultimately break and recede," Smith writes, arguing that the movement lacks the institutional depth required for longevity. "It's a blast of pure populist rage, and those don't ever last very long."

The author points to declining engagement metrics for key influencers as evidence of this shift. He cites reports showing that views for prominent right-wing commentators have plummeted from their 2025 peaks. Smith suggests this is a natural "thermostatic effect" where the public's appetite for rage-based media diminishes once the party they oppose is no longer in power. He argues that the administration's decision to engage in conflict abroad has fractured a base that voted against such wars, while their failure to curb inflation has alienated voters seeking economic relief.

"The only campaign promise Trump has delivered on was reducing immigration, but it turns out that reducing immigration doesn't actually make a material difference in the lives of Americans."

This assessment is particularly sharp because it separates the emotional appeal of the movement from its tangible outcomes. Smith posits that without civic groups, religious revivals, or professional associations to anchor it, the movement is destined to fade once the initial anger subsides. A counterargument worth considering is that political movements often evolve rather than disappear; the energy may shift from online rage to institutional power, even if the audience numbers drop.

The Illusion of Falling Wages

Shifting to the economy, Smith tackles one of the most alarming trends in modern discourse: the steady decline in labor's share of national income. He challenges the prevailing narrative that this is solely due to automation or corporate greed. Instead, he highlights a compelling argument from economists Owen Zidar and Eric Zwick that much of this decline is a "statistical illusion" created by tax policy.

Smith explains how the Tax Reform Act of 1986 incentivized businesses to convert from C corporations to S corporations. In these pass-through entities, income that would traditionally be recorded as wages is reclassified as profit to avoid specific levies. "When we add these recharacterized wages back into the labor income bucket, then the corporate labor share in 2017 is about 0.9 percentage points higher," he notes. Furthermore, the growing use of stock-based compensation means billions of dollars in worker pay are not showing up as labor income in national accounts.

"This doesn't kill the idea that the falling labor share is a problem. But it's a warning that we need to figure out how big the problem really is, before we decide we need to reshape our whole economy in order to fight it."

This reframing is crucial. If the decline is partly a bookkeeping artifact, then radical economic restructuring might be an overreaction to a misdiagnosed problem. However, critics might note that even if the numbers are distorted, the underlying trend of capital concentrating wealth remains real and requires attention regardless of the accounting method.

The Paradox of Immigration Policy

Smith then turns to the contentious issue of immigration, specifically the administration's reported plan to charge foreign graduates a $100,000 fee to work in the U.S. He argues this move contradicts the economic reality that high-skilled immigrants create jobs rather than steal them. Citing a paper by Kwon, Lee, and Monras on the Optional Practical Training (OPT) program, he illustrates how expanding the pool of foreign talent actually boosts domestic employment.

The research found that increasing the supply of high-skilled immigrant workers led to a 1% increase in firm creation and a 0.5% rise in native employment. "High-skilled migration programs exist around the world in the hope that immigrants complement native workers, allow firms to grow, and boost innovation," Smith summarizes. He emphasizes that the benefits are not zero-sum; when foreign robotics engineers arrive in a city, they attract capital that hires native-born engineers.

"Basically, if you let a bunch of foreign robotics engineers work in Pittsburgh, then a bunch of robotics companies are going to start up or open branch offices or factories in Pittsburgh. And that ends up employing a bunch of high-skilled Americans of the type who would tend to work at robotics companies."

This section highlights a profound disconnect between political ideology and economic evidence. While the administration frames these restrictions as protecting American labor, the data suggests they would likely shrink the very opportunities they aim to preserve. The irony is palpable: a policy designed to help workers ends up hurting them by stifling growth.

AI and the Outsourcing Boom

In a twist that defies conventional wisdom, Smith points out that the rise of artificial intelligence has not crushed the Indian outsourcing industry as many predicted. Instead, exports from these service sectors are booming. He attributes this to the fact that AI is currently acting as a complement to human labor rather than a substitute.

"And yet so far, AI is working like a normal technology."

This observation serves as a reminder that technological disruption is rarely linear. Just as the internet created new categories of work rather than simply deleting old ones, AI appears to be expanding the demand for human coordination and specialized services. The fear that machines will render human labor obsolete may be premature, or at least, the timeline for such a shift is far longer than the hype suggests.

The Cost of Harm Reduction

Finally, Smith addresses the unintended consequences of progressive urban policy, specifically the "harm reduction" approach to drug use. He cites Michael Powell's reporting on how cities like Seattle and San Francisco, in an effort to be compassionate, have inadvertently created environments where public drug use is rampant and dangerous.

Smith notes that while these policies have succeeded in reducing immediate overdose deaths at injection sites, the broader consequence has been a spike in overall overdose rates and a degradation of public spaces. "Polluting the urban commons by flooding downtowns with drug users has got to end," he argues. He points to a shift in leadership, with mayors in San Francisco, Philadelphia, and Boston beginning to crack down on encampments and prioritize treatment over tolerance.

"These policies retain a powerful hold on public-health and political leaders in blue parts of the country."

The tragedy here is that the intent was to save lives, but the outcome has been to make cities less livable for everyone, including the vulnerable populations the policies were meant to help. The historical parallel to the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act comes to mind: just as protectionist tariffs intended to save American jobs ended up deepening the Great Depression, well-meaning drug policies have exacerbated the very crises they sought to solve. The lesson is that policy must account for human behavior and systemic incentives, not just moral intentions.

Bottom Line

Noah Smith's analysis succeeds in stripping away the ideological veneer to reveal the complex, often counterintuitive mechanics of policy and economics. His strongest argument is that good intentions and political rage are poor substitutes for rigorous evidence, whether in immigration, labor statistics, or urban planning. The biggest vulnerability in the piece is its optimism that the "MAGA wave" will simply recede on its own; history suggests that movements often mutate into something more entrenched before they fade. Readers should watch for whether the emerging shift in urban drug policy signals a durable return to pragmatism or merely a temporary reaction to public backlash.
