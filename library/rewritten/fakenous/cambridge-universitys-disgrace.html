This piece by Michael Huemer delivers a scathing, satirical takedown of a fabricated academic scandal, using an impossible biography to expose the fragility of modern institutional defenses. By weaving together verifiable facts about Jason Arday with absurd lies—claiming he is the "sole winner of all the Nobel prizes ever given"—Huemer forces the reader to confront how easily a narrative can be constructed to shield incompetence from scrutiny. The article is not a report on a real event, but a mirror held up to the mechanisms of denial that protect high-profile figures when evidence of fraud mounts.

The Architecture of a Fabrication

Huemer begins by establishing a baseline of truth before dismantling it with deliberate absurdity. He acknowledges Arday's genuine hardships, noting that the professor "did not speak till age 11, and was illiterate until age 18," and survived a brain tumor before earning his PhD. These are the facts that make the subsequent fabrication so effective. Huemer then pivots, listing impossible feats: "He is also the first astronaut to land on the moon while paralyzed from the neck down, the sole winner of all the Nobel prizes ever given, and the one true King of Narnia." He explicitly admits, "(I have interpolated a bit here.)" This technique serves a critical function: it highlights how the public discourse often elevates personal tragedy into a shield against professional accountability, blurring the line between a remarkable life story and academic fraud.

The author then introduces the catalyst for the "scandal": a blog post by Nathan Cofnas accusing Arday of plagiarism. Huemer writes, "The demonic blogger and philosopher Nathan Cofnas finally got to our hero." This sarcastic framing immediately signals the piece's critical stance toward the academic establishment's reaction. The evidence presented is stark: "There are many instances (188 sentences, according to one report), and these are about the clearest, most paradigmatic examples of plagiarism that you could ask for." Huemer argues that the copying was not accidental, pointing to specific verbatim sections from a 2009 dissertation by Paula Zwozdiak-Myers.

"The evil racists have tried to hold him back."

Huemer uses this quote to mock the immediate reflex to frame any criticism as racial hostility. He details how Arday allegedly accused journalists of harassment and threatened legal action against The Times Higher Education, causing them to kill a story. The author suggests that the institution's defense relies on silencing inquiry rather than addressing the substance of the allegations. Critics might note that the satirical exaggeration of threats (like the "severed pig's head" which the police have no record of) risks undermining the very real harm of actual harassment faced by minority scholars. However, Huemer's intent is to show how the narrative of victimhood is weaponized to avoid investigation.

The Erosion of Standards

The commentary shifts to the quality of the scholarship itself. Huemer lists a series of grammatical and syntactical errors from Arday's publications, such as "Increasing diagoneses of mental illness" and "Racism is unlikely to ever go way." These are not minor typos; they represent a fundamental lack of literacy in a professor of sociology. Huemer writes, "His department head once told him that he was 'the best in the world in terms of the research that you do.' If true, this suggests that we should shut down the entire field." This biting sarcasm underscores the disconnect between institutional praise and objective quality.

The piece argues that the problem extends beyond one individual to the ideology that enabled the hire. Huemer connects this case to broader trends in academia, referencing the "Guttenberg plagiarism scandal" to show that while plagiarism happens across the spectrum, the reaction to it is often dictated by political identity. He notes that the "Good Law Project" defended Arday, claiming he was "repeatedly subjected to baseless allegations after being fully exonerated," despite the lack of an independent investigation at the time.

"The existence of Jason Arday isn't the issue. The issue is how the rest of the academic world treated him."

This distinction is the core of Huemer's argument. He posits that the university's defense was not about truth, but about protecting a specific narrative of diversity. He suggests that administrators "implicitly assumed from the start that a black man couldn't satisfy the standards we apply to white professors," leading to a "subtle racism of low expectations." This is a provocative claim: that lowering the bar for minority candidates is itself a form of racism that harms those candidates by making them vulnerable to suspicion.

The Slippery Slope of Ideology

Huemer draws a parallel between Arday and other high-profile academic scandals, such as the case of Ward Churchill, noting that "Wokism is a dishonest ideology, so it attracts people like Claudine Gay, Jason Arday, and Ward Churchill." He argues that the acceptance of such figures is a symptom of a larger institutional rot. The author points out that while the right-wing media often highlights these cases, the academic world rarely self-corrects until forced by external pressure. "If it hadn't been for Nathan Cofnas, Jason Arday would still be teaching at Cambridge," Huemer asserts.

The piece also touches on the demographic impact of such hires. Huemer claims that "excellent students struggle to find jobs" because of DEI (Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion) policies, specifically noting a white male student with "over 20 published papers" who couldn't find permanent employment. He argues that this system harms minority professors who earned their positions legitimately, as they are wrongly suspected of being "DEI hires."

"University administrators rarely care about truth or justice. The only reason why Cambridge finally agreed to do an investigation was the negative publicity surrounding the case."

This conclusion paints a grim picture of institutional incentives. Huemer suggests that the drop in public confidence in higher education is a direct result of these dynamics. He writes, "The number of Americans expressing high confidence in higher education dropped from 57% in 2015 to 36% in 2023." While the article uses satire to make its point, the underlying data on public trust is a sobering reality that the piece leverages to argue for a return to meritocratic standards.

Critics might argue that Huemer's conflation of a satirical fabrication with real-world DEI debates risks oversimplifying complex issues of structural inequality. The piece does not engage deeply with the systemic barriers that might lead to genuine disparities in hiring, focusing instead on the individual failures of specific appointees. However, the strength of the argument lies in its insistence that lowering standards for any group ultimately devalues the achievements of that group.

Bottom Line

Michael Huemer's commentary is a masterclass in using satire to expose institutional blindness, forcing readers to question whether the defense of diversity has become an excuse for lowering academic rigor. While the piece relies on a fictionalized extreme to make its point, its critique of the reflex to dismiss criticism as racism and the failure of universities to self-correct is a potent warning. The biggest vulnerability remains the risk that such a broad brushstroke could be used to dismiss legitimate efforts to address historical inequities, but the central demand for truth and meritocracy remains undeniable.
