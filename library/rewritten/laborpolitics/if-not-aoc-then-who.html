Eric Blanc confronts a potentially catastrophic strategic vacuum on the American Left: the possibility that the 2028 presidential primary proceeds without a clear, movement-backed alternative to the establishment, leaving the field open for a candidate indistinguishable from the corporate status quo. The piece's most striking claim is not that a specific star must run, but that the Democratic Socialists of America must proactively construct a 'Plan B' centered on an unknown worker, leveraging the very lack of political experience as a radical asset. In an era where institutional trust has evaporated, Blanc argues that the movement's survival depends on breaking the cycle of waiting for a celebrity to save them, instead forcing the party to confront the demands of a furious electorate before the election cycle even begins.

The Plan B Imperative

Blanc opens by dismantling the passive hope that Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez will inevitably enter the race. He warns that the absence of a contingency plan is a luxury the Left cannot afford. "The US Left can't afford to sit out the 2028 Democratic primary," Blanc writes, noting that the 2024 abstention "self-marginalized anti-billionaire politics from the national conversation." This is a sharp, necessary critique of a movement that often confuses purity with power. By refusing to engage in the primary, the Left inadvertently ceded the center to a candidate who pivoted to the preferences of corporate donors and establishment allies. The argument gains urgency when Blanc points out that abstaining again would be "especially counterproductive" given the recent surge in democratic socialist victories in New York City and beyond. The momentum is there; the question is whether the movement has the discipline to harness it or if it will retreat to the margins during the most critical political battle of the decade.

"Even more urgently, keeping Trump's heir out of the White House may depend on whether leftists and the party's unprecedentedly disenchanted base can force the Democrats in 2028 to take meaningful steps towards a very angry electorate."

The stakes are framed not just as ideological, but as existential for the broader goal of defeating the MAGA movement. Blanc suggests that the only way to stop the next iteration of the far-right is to force the Democratic Party to address the anger of its own base, rather than catering to the "corporate-consultant blob." Critics might argue that splitting the vote in a primary could inadvertently help a right-wing candidate in the general election, a risk that has historically paralyzed left-wing strategists. However, Blanc counters this by pointing to the shifting rules of the Democratic nomination process, which now prioritize voters over party operatives, making it feasible to run a socialist candidate without guaranteeing a split that hands victory to the opposition.

The Worker Candidate Playbook

If the celebrity candidate is not available, Blanc proposes a radical shift in candidate selection: running a union worker with no political resume. He draws heavily on international precedents to validate this strategy, noting that "Lula, the current president of Brazil, began his political career as a metalworker militant who ran for governor of São Paulo in 1982." This historical anchor is crucial; it reminds readers that the most transformative leaders often emerge from the shop floor, not the think tank. Blanc expands this global context, citing Rachel Kéké, an immigrant hotel housekeeper who defeated a former minister in France, and Rudi Kennes, a veteran of the Opel Antwerp auto factory who won a seat in the European Parliament for the Belgian Workers' Party. These examples are not mere footnotes; they are the evidentiary backbone of the argument that "distance from traditional politics" can be a virtue rather than a liability.

"We don't need someone famous. We just need a charismatic, well-vetted comrade who works in a relatable working-class occupation — e.g. a nurse, or a worker in auto or construction, or at Amazon."

Blanc is careful to distinguish this approach from the performative, often problematic outsider campaigns of the past, such as the Graham Platner fiasco. He argues that the lesson from Platner is not to avoid outsiders, but to ensure they have "proper vetting and roots in democratic membership organizations." The proposal is to find a dozen potential worker leaders and convince them that their sacrifice is worth it. The goal is to signal that the movement represents workers versus bosses, rather than positioning itself as "the furthest left" force, a label that often alienates non-college-educated voters. This reframing is a sophisticated attempt to broaden the appeal of democratic socialism beyond the college-educated progressive bubble, focusing on the shared economic struggles of the working class.

Polarizing on the Essentials

The core of Blanc's strategy for the 2028 campaign is to force a polarization around two specific, high-stakes issues: domestic healthcare and foreign aid. He argues that democratic socialists must use the primary to make the election about "America's unaffordable, family-breaking private healthcare system" and the "bankrolling of the genocidal Israeli government." This is a bold move, as it deliberately chooses conflict over consensus. Blanc writes, "Polarizing litmus tests are good, actually — as long as they're fought for around popular demands in a thoughtful and strategic way." The proposed slogan, "Medicare for All — Not a Dime for Israel," is designed to pose a sharp, moral question to the American public and the Democratic establishment alike.

"Why can't working-class Americans afford their healthcare bills at the same time that our government is spending at least $3.8 billion yearly to fund an Israeli regime eager to keep dragging us and the world into more forever wars?"

The humanitarian cost of the current foreign policy is central to Blanc's argument. He does not shy away from the gravity of the situation, framing the aid as enabling "crimes against humanity" and arguing that the status quo is "life-destroying." This focus on human suffering is a necessary corrective to the often abstract nature of political strategy discussions. Blanc anticipates the objection that such a campaign is a waste of time, countering that the primary is the only venue with the attention necessary to recruit members and shift the Overton window. He draws a parallel to the "Uncommitted" movement in 2024, suggesting that with a dedicated candidate and a larger organization, the Left can exert even greater leverage. The strategy is to use the primary as a bargaining chip, pledging support in the general election only to a candidate who accepts the demands of ending aid to Israel and establishing single-payer healthcare.

"If Democratic leaders want young people, workers, and those sick of the status quo to show up in big numbers this November, they need to meet us halfway."

Blanc also addresses the potential consolidation of the anti-corporate vote. He acknowledges that if a candidate like Ro Khanna were to capture the left lane, the movement should be willing to consolidate behind them, provided they are held accountable to the core planks. This flexibility is presented as a pragmatic necessity, contrasting with the rigidity that often plagues movement politics. The argument is that the goal is not to win the nomination at all costs, but to ensure that the eventual nominee is forced to adopt a platform that reflects the needs of the working class. This approach mirrors the tactics of radical left parties in parliamentary democracies like Denmark or Sweden, where they act as junior partners to dictate terms for government formation.

Bottom Line

Eric Blanc's proposal is a compelling, high-stakes strategy that correctly identifies the danger of relying on celebrity politicians to carry the movement's banner. The strongest part of his argument is the historical and international evidence that worker candidates can succeed by leveraging their lack of establishment ties, turning a perceived weakness into a powerful narrative of authenticity. However, the plan's biggest vulnerability lies in the sheer difficulty of vetting and mobilizing a candidate who lacks a pre-existing national profile, a task that requires a level of organizational discipline the Left has yet to fully demonstrate. Readers should watch closely to see if the Democratic Socialists of America can move from debating this theory to actually identifying and backing a worker candidate before the 2028 cycle gains momentum.
