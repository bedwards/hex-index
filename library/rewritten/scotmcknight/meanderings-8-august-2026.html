Scot McKnight transforms a summer's end into a profound meditation on how communities, faith, and even personal habits are defined by what flows in and what leaks out. Rather than a simple diary entry, this piece offers a rigorous sociological lens on religious decline and a surprising neurological defense of hoarding books, all while sidestepping the noise of the White House to focus on the quiet, structural shifts reshaping American life.

The Fluidity of Faith

McKnight begins by rejecting static views of religious institutions, proposing instead that traditions are like "styrofoam cups" where the water level is never fixed. He writes, "The most unavoidable pressure is generational replacement. People will die and new people will be born every single day." This metaphor is strikingly effective because it visualizes the inevitable attrition of aging congregations against the influx of new families. The author argues that while death creates small holes, the "largest holes form in the cup: lack of retention," a point that shifts the blame from external cultural forces to internal community dynamics.

The data McKnight marshals is particularly damning for established institutions. He notes that while Jews and the non-religious retain about three-quarters of their members, "Catholics clock in at just 58%, meaning more than four in ten people raised Catholic have since left the faith." Even more startling is the finding regarding the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, where "just a bare majority of kids raised LDS are still LDS as adults (54%)." This challenges the common perception of the Latter-day Saints as a monolith of growth, suggesting instead a significant leakage of youth that mirrors the struggles of mainline denominations.

"While a young couple who has a new baby and brings them to church adds some liquid to the styrofoam vessel, what happens if that child grows into a teenager or young adult who stops attending entirely?"

McKnight extends this analysis to the United Methodist Church, tracing a "sharp" downward slope between 1970 and 1990 where the denomination lost 1.8 million members. He attributes this to a mix of "internal dynamics," the rise of evangelicals, and a broader "shifts away from religion." Critics might argue that focusing solely on retention rates ignores the quality of faith or the reasons people leave, such as theological disagreements or institutional scandals. However, the sheer volume of the exodus cannot be dismissed as mere cultural drift. The transformation of the former Trinity United Methodist Church in Nashville into affordable housing serves as a poignant, real-world example of this shift: a congregation voting to close and deeding its property to a nonprofit to "build 22 affordable homes for families." This repurposing highlights a future where the physical footprint of the church remains, but its function evolves from a place of worship to a hub of community survival.

The Psychology of Anticipation

Shifting from the macro to the micro, McKnight explores the neuroscience behind why people, including himself, cannot stop buying books they will never read. He introduces the concept of tsundoku—the act of acquiring reading materials but letting them pile up without reading them. McKnight cites researcher Spear, who explains that "the hippocampus begins to assemble that future experience," binding together memories of sunshine, coffee, and turning pages into a "coherent mental scene."

This reframing of book hoarding from a clutter problem to a psychological necessity is compelling. McKnight writes, "Unread books are future rewards," and notes that "the brain uses memories of the past to rehearse pleasures that have yet to happen." This insight connects the personal habit of his husband, who has a "book-hoarding problem" that fills closets and encroaches on the living room, to a universal human need for hope and anticipation. It suggests that the value of a book is not just in its consumption but in the promise it holds.

"Tsundoku may reflect one of the brain's most remarkable abilities: the capacity to derive genuine pleasure from experiences that have not yet happened."

This section offers a gentle counterpoint to the anxiety of the religious retention crisis. If faith is a cup leaking water, perhaps the joy of a future book is a way to fill the cup with anticipation. It is a reminder that human beings are wired to look forward, even when the present seems uncertain.

The Soundtrack of a Summer

The piece concludes with a cultural detour, examining former President Obama's summer playlist as a lens into shared human experience. McKnight notes the diversity of the list, which ranges from Glen Hansard to The Beatles, and uses it to invite readers to engage with a shared cultural moment. While the list itself is a collection of songs, McKnight's inclusion of it serves to ground the abstract sociological and neurological discussions in the tangible reality of daily life. It is a subtle nod to the fact that while institutions rise and fall and brains seek future pleasure, we all still listen to music and look for connection.

Bottom Line

McKnight's strongest move is reframing religious decline not as a failure of doctrine but as a mechanical failure of retention, supported by stark data that upends assumptions about which groups are growing. The piece's vulnerability lies in its brevity; it hints at the complex "internal dynamics" causing these leaks without fully dissecting the theological or political fissures driving them. For the busy reader, this commentary offers a vital shift in perspective: whether in a church, a neighborhood, or a living room, the health of a community is defined not by how much it keeps, but by how well it holds on to its future.
