Matt Yglesias makes a counterintuitive claim that cuts against the grain of modern political media: the very "boring" style we dismiss as a liability is actually the hallmark of effective governance and general-election success. While the news cycle chases viral moments and personality clashes, Yglesias argues that the obsession with "excitement" is a structural flaw in our primary systems that actively harms our ability to select leaders who can win and govern.

The Illusion of Engagement

Yglesias begins by reframing the low political engagement of the 1990s not as a civic failure, but as a symptom of a stable, benign political environment. He notes that during that era, "people who didn't find this inherently compelling felt that it was largely permissible to tune it all out." This perspective challenges the modern anxiety that citizens must be constantly mobilized by high-stakes drama to function as a democracy. The author points to the 1996 election as a signpost of a different time, noting that while Bill Clinton held a 54 percent approval rating, he still only secured 49 percent of the vote because the swing voter was "complacent and content," not terrified or enraged.

This historical contrast is crucial because it highlights how the definition of a "swing voter" has warped. Today, as Yglesias observes, "the key swing vote has been 'double-haters' — could Kamala Harris persuade people who thought Joe Biden did a bad job that Donald Trump was too terrifying to let back into the White House?" The shift from a contented electorate to one driven by mutual loathing suggests that the demand for "excitement" is a reaction to instability, not a prerequisite for it. Critics might argue that in an era of existential threats, complacency is a luxury we cannot afford, but Yglesias's point remains that the style of politics required to combat that threat is often the very thing that alienates the voters needed to win.

The point of representative government is to select leaders who will make reasonable policy choices and be effective stewards of citizens' rights and interests. It's not pro wrestling.

The Primary Trap

The core of Yglesias's argument targets the nomination process, where the incentives are misaligned with the general election. He writes, "when we say that 'boring' is a handicap for a politician, we're really just talking about primaries." In these crowded fields, candidates are poorly distinguished, and the ability to command attention becomes the primary currency. Yglesias illustrates this with the Wisconsin gubernatorial race, where David Crowley, a "low-key county executive," struggled to gain traction without significant help from Governor Tony Evers. Conversely, candidates who master the "attention economy" often dominate the narrative even if their general-election viability is questionable.

Yglesias contrasts the media's fascination with viral figures like Zohran Mamdani against the quiet success of Roy Cooper in North Carolina. He notes, "Because Cooper is not a master of attention politics in the way that Zohran Mamdani is, we've had about 100 times as many articles asking what lessons Democrats can learn from Mamdani as articles asking what they can learn from Cooper." Yet, as Yglesias bluntly puts it, "beating Republicans in North Carolina is obviously more relevant to the broad question of how to stop MAGA than beating Andrew Cuomo in a primary in New York City!" This disconnect reveals a media ecosystem that rewards the theatrical over the functional. The danger, he suggests, is that parties may nominate candidates who are brilliant at winning primaries but incapable of appealing to the broader, more diverse electorate.

Governing is Mundane

Even if a candidate navigates the primary gauntlet, the actual work of governing is inherently unglamorous. Yglesias argues that the public's desire for excitement clashes with the reality that policy solutions are often "tedious technical issues." He points to the Obama administration's struggles, noting that "the economic recovery was just too damn slow" not because of a lack of charisma, but due to "an incorrect analysis of the macroeconomic situation." Similarly, he suggests that the administration's missteps on immigration policy stemmed from a failure to accurately assess push and pull factors, not a lack of dramatic flair.

The author insists that "the parts of politics that people find exciting or interesting aren't really the parts that are central to governing." This is a sobering reminder for an audience that often conflates political theater with political efficacy. When bad analysis leads to bad outcomes, the public gets interested, but the policy analysis itself is rarely the source of that interest. Yglesias asks us to consider that if being boring is a "crippling handicap in the nomination process," it serves as a "really damning indictment of the process" itself.

Providing entertainment to political junkies is not the point of the process. The point is to pick a nominee who can win the general election and govern well.

The Media's Self-Reflection

Finally, Yglesias turns the lens on the political class and media consumers, arguing that our definition of "exciting" is often just a proxy for what appeals to our specific social bubbles. He writes, "'exciting' can easily become a stand-in for the idea of appealing to our specific social peers." He uses the example of Kristen McDonald Rivet, a candidate who may not be a "vertical video superstar" but is a "stellar electoral performer" in Michigan's Tri-Cities. The media's failure to recognize her potential stems from a bias toward candidates who look and sound like the coastal elite.

This bias ignores the reality that "most voters are over 50 years old, live in the suburbs of mid-sized metro areas, and don't have college degrees." While a candidate like Obama could bridge these divides, Yglesias warns that the current system rewards the very traits that alienate these crucial demographics. He concludes that if the goal is to check the power of the MAGA movement, citizens and media alike must "choose to get excited about the politicians who are demonstrably good at it," even if they lack the razzle-dazzle of the primary stage.

Bottom Line

Matt Yglesias offers a necessary corrective to the modern political obsession with personality, arguing that the demand for excitement is a structural flaw that distorts our selection of leaders. His strongest evidence lies in the stark contrast between primary dynamics and general-election realities, where boring competence consistently outperforms viral charisma. The argument's vulnerability is its reliance on a hypothetical shift in media and voter behavior that may be difficult to achieve in a hyper-partisan environment, but the verdict is clear: if we want better governance, we must stop treating politics like a reality show.
