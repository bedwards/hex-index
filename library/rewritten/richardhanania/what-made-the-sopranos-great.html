Richard Hanania argues that the enduring power of The Sopranos lies not in its mob violence, but in its ruthless excavation of how we construct our own identities through the stories we tell about the past. While many critics praise the show for its cinematic quality or its subversion of genre tropes, Hanania identifies a more unsettling core: the series captures a specific cultural moment where the gap between our idealized history and our mundane reality became impossible to ignore.

The Art of Self-Deception

Hanania posits that the show's genius is its meta-awareness, a quality that prevents the narrative from sinking into simple nostalgia. He notes that the characters are acutely aware they are "walking clichés," yet they are trapped by the very scripts they try to perform. "The self-awareness of The Sopranos nearly always avoids devolving into tedious navel-gazing," Hanania writes, pointing out that even the characters' neuroses become the subject of debate rather than just plot points. This framing is crucial because it shifts the focus from "what happens" to "why it matters." The characters aren't just living in New Jersey; they are living in a simulation of the Mafia created by movies like The Godfather.

The author illustrates this with a striking observation about the feedback loop between fiction and reality. "Gambetta writes that the influence of the film extended to the criminal undergrounds as far away as Russia and Japan, demonstrating that memetic influence crosses borders and cultural barriers," Hanania explains. He highlights how real-life mobsters, such as Sammy "The Bull" Gravano, adopted lines from cinema to justify their actions, creating a bizarre loop where "art imitates low life imitating art." This is a profound insight into how institutions, even criminal ones, rely on cultural narratives to sustain their legitimacy. Critics might argue that this overstates the influence of pop culture on organized crime, suggesting that economic desperation is a stronger driver than movie quotes, but Hanania's point about the perception of the mob remains undeniable.

We have come full circle: now art imitates low life imitating art.

The Illusion of the Golden Age

The commentary then pivots to the show's central tension: the belief that the past was superior. Hanania writes that the series captures the mood of the moment "between the end of the Cold War and 9/11, as the economy was booming, and, since peace and prosperity had been achieved, societal problems were understood in moral and psychological rather than material terms." This context is vital. The show didn't just happen; it happened when America felt safe enough to worry about the meaning of life rather than the threat of war. Tony Soprano's depression is not a personal failing but a symptom of a society that has achieved material success but lost its moral compass.

Hanania points to the scene where Tony admits, "I came in at the end. The best is over." This line, Hanania argues, is the emotional anchor of the series. It reflects a broader American anxiety about decline, a feeling that the "golden age" of the mob—and by extension, the nation—is a fabrication. The show relentlessly dismantles this myth. When Tony investigates his father's past, he finds not a noble warrior, but a "petty crook" surrounded by delusion. "The visceral disgust felt toward Fran in that moment — rather than anything about how Johnny's affairs hurt the family — being the straw that broke the camel's back is the kind of realistic touch that most shows and movies would shy away from," Hanania observes. This refusal to romanticize the past is what makes the show feel so modern. It forces the viewer to confront the fact that our heroes are often just people trying to sell a story that no longer holds up.

The Banality of Evil and Glamour

Finally, Hanania addresses the show's unique ability to balance the glamorous with the grotesque. He notes that the series "manages not to flinch in the face of either side of the human experience," showing that greatness and ugliness are often two sides of the same coin. The characters are capable of profound tenderness and sudden, senseless violence, often within the same scene. "The comedy, the action, and the investment in the characters are of course essential aspects of the show, but it is these three great themes that provide the context that sets The Sopranos apart," Hanania concludes. This balance is what keeps the audience engaged even when the characters are at their most despicable. We are not watching a morality play; we are watching a mirror held up to our own contradictions.

The author's analysis of the class dynamic is particularly sharp. While the mobsters struggle with the collapse of their code, the white-collar professionals around them, like Dr. Melfi, debate the same issues from a distance. "The sordid history of worries about anti-Italian discrimination can be found in the fact that one organization devoted to that cause was founded by mob boss Joseph Colombo," Hanania notes, adding a layer of historical irony that the show uses to its advantage. The show suggests that everyone, from the hitman to the psychiatrist, is struggling with the same fundamental question: who are we, and what story are we telling ourselves?

Bottom Line

Hanania's most compelling argument is that The Sopranos is great because it exposes the fragility of the stories we tell to make sense of our lives. The show's greatest strength is its refusal to offer easy answers, instead leaving the viewer with the uncomfortable realization that the "golden age" was likely never real. Its biggest vulnerability is that it assumes a level of cultural literacy regarding the mob genre that younger audiences might lack, but the universal themes of identity and delusion remain timeless.
