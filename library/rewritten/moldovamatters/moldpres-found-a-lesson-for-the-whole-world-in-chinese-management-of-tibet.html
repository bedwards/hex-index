This piece exposes a quiet but dangerous mechanism of modern influence: how a state news agency in an EU candidate country became an uncritical megaphone for Beijing's narrative on Tibet. David Smith's investigation reveals that what was sold as "ecological tourism" in the Ngawa region was, in reality, a curated propaganda tour funded by a Chinese public diplomacy arm, yet published without a single disclosure to Moldovan readers.

The State Agency Paradox

Smith begins by dissecting the unique and problematic position of Moldpres, Moldova's State Information Agency. He notes that while the agency claims its mission is to "provide accurate, equidistant, transparent information and to ensure pluralism of opinion," its structure blurs the line between a newsroom and a government mouthpiece. The author highlights a critical flaw in the pending media reform, Draft Law 123/2026, which explicitly exempts Moldpres from the independence requirements that apply to other public broadcasters. "Moldpres, which is explicitly named in 5(2)(c), is not" subject to the same regulatory safeguards, creating a loophole for state-controlled messaging to bypass editorial scrutiny.

This legal ambiguity sets the stage for the series of reports on Tibet. Smith argues that the agency's lack of transparency regarding foreign-funded trips is not just an oversight, but a structural feature that allows foreign powers to shape domestic discourse. The agency's own defense—that the trip was a "professional internship program" with no editorial obligations—rings hollow when the output is indistinguishable from state-sponsored promotion.

"The subjects published reflected the author's own journalistic choices..." yet the content mirrors the exact messaging of the Chinese Communist Party's "Ecological Civilization" ideology.

The "Green" Facade vs. Human Reality

The core of Smith's critique targets the specific article by Moldpres journalist Lilia Grubi, which frames the Ngawa region as a "modern model of coexistence between people, tradition, nature." Smith writes that this framing is not accidental but a direct reflection of "Xi Jinping Thought on Ecological Civilization," a concept formally adopted by the Party in 2018 to blend environmental policy with political control. The article praises the "delicate balance" of economic development and nature, quoting the journalist's observation that "tourism is allowed, but strictly controlled."

Smith dismantles this narrative by contrasting it with the brutal reality of "grid management" systems deployed across Tibet. He points out that the "strict control" mentioned in the article refers to a pervasive surveillance apparatus where religious practices, communications, and even DNA are monitored. The author notes that while Grubi celebrates the restoration of wetlands, independent reporting reveals that Ngawa has been the site of over 40 self-immolations since 2009. This omission is not merely a gap in reporting; it is a deliberate whitewashing of human rights abuses.

Critics might argue that focusing on the ecological successes of a region does not inherently deny the existence of political repression. However, Smith counters that by presenting the region solely through the lens of state-approved development, the reporting actively obscures the oppression that makes such "development" possible. As Smith puts it, "This is stunning in its failure to acknowledge that the 'strict control' for which Ngawa is famous, is for the oppression of human rights."

The Illusion of Independence

The investigation delves into the funding source, revealing that the trip was paid for by the Chinese Public Diplomacy Association, an organization with close ties to the Chinese Foreign Ministry. When pressed, Moldpres claimed that no external entity intervened in the drafting or approval of the stories. Smith finds this defense unconvincing, noting that the "carefully choreographed process" of the tour ensured that journalists only saw what the organizers wanted them to see.

He cites a report from the Foreign Correspondents' Club of China, which states that "ninety-three percent of correspondents who attempted to travel to the TAR... faced difficulty in reporting independently." The Moldpres series, therefore, fits a pattern of state-organized press tours designed to "claim increased numbers and greater access" while maintaining total control over the narrative. Smith emphasizes that the lack of a policy requiring disclosure of foreign funding means Moldovan readers are left unaware that they are consuming state propaganda disguised as travel journalism.

"Ngawa's story is not really about wetlands, yak milk or eco-tourism. It is about what happens when a system built for oppression is allowed to write its own account of itself."

Smith contrasts Moldpres's approach with that of independent outlets like Ziarul de Gardă, whose CEO Alina Radu refuses trips designed to promote a government or company. This comparison underscores the ethical void in Moldpres's current practices. The author argues that even if no contractual obligation existed to publish positive stories, the "editorial choice" to publish uncritically after a funded trip is a failure of journalistic duty.

Bottom Line

David Smith's most compelling argument is that the danger lies not just in the content of the articles, but in the institutional silence that allows them to circulate as fact. The piece's greatest strength is its exposure of how media laws intended to protect independence can be twisted to exempt state agencies from accountability. Its vulnerability is the difficulty of proving direct editorial interference when the mechanism is subtle—relying on the natural bias of a curated tour rather than explicit censorship. Until Moldpres adopts transparent funding policies, the "lesson for the whole world" from Beijing will continue to be broadcast in Moldova without the necessary context of human cost.
