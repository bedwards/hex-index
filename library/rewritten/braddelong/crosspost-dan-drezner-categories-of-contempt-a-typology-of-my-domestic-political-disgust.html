In a political landscape often dominated by outrage at the obvious, Brad DeLong highlights a more unsettling argument from Dan Drezner: that the most dangerous figures in the current administration are not the incompetent clowns, but the intelligent enablers who know better. DeLong frames Drezner's analysis as a necessary recalibration of moral outrage, suggesting that we should reserve our deepest contempt for those with the acumen to understand the damage they are causing yet choose to proceed anyway for the sake of power.

The Hierarchy of Complicity

DeLong introduces Drezner's central thesis by contrasting two types of political actors. He notes that in a "surprisingly nearby timeline," Drezner would be a traditional establishment Republican dismissing critics as "intelligent but overwrought, unbalanced, and shrill." But in reality, Drezner has pivoted to target those who betray principles they once held. As DeLong writes, Drezner argues that "contempt should be allotted in proportion to culpability, not incompetence." This distinction is crucial; it shifts the focus from the spectacle of failure to the quiet mechanics of enablement.

The author explains that Drezner views figures like Pete Hegseth as "manifestly unqualified and honorless clowns" from whom nothing could be expected. Consequently, condemning them yields diminishing returns. The real target is the "normie" Republican—someone like Marco Rubio or Elbridge Colby—who is "clearly intelligent enough to know what (and who) he is enabling and does it anyway." DeLong emphasizes that this group's behavior is more contemptuous because it is a willful moral choice rather than a limitation of ability. This framing forces the reader to confront the idea that the most damaging actors are often the most competent ones.

The intelligent enablers who understand exactly what they're empowering — and choose it for power and status — deserve more scorn than manifestly unqualified and honorless clowns from whom nothing could ever have been expected.

The Endemic Nature of Abuse

Drezner does not ignore the actions of lower-level officials, whom he terms "Stormtroopers." DeLong notes that Drezner documents how these career officials have enthusiastically embraced abusive tactics, citing a Wired report on Customs and Border Protection (CBP) officers misusing government databases for personal reasons. The report details cases where officers used trusted-traveler applications to ask people out or shared border-crossing data in "heated divorce" proceedings. DeLong points out that Drezner's analysis here is nuanced: while the Trump administration aggravates these behaviors, the abuse of surveillance power "spans administrations," with the Wired story covering the period from 2009 to 2022.

This historical context is vital. It suggests that the problem is not solely the current leadership but a structural rot within the bureaucracy that any administration can exploit. However, DeLong acknowledges that Drezner sees the current administration as a catalyst that has removed even the pretense of restraint. The administration's actions have turned what might have been hidden abuses into overt tools of political intimidation, such as the Department of Homeland Security investigating progressive organizations for "domestic terrorist financing" without evidence of criminal activity.

The Normalization of Scandal

Perhaps the most disturbing category Drezner identifies is the "Trump emulator." These are aspiring politicians who view the former president's behavior as a successful template for gaining power. DeLong cites Michelle Goldberg's observation that under the current administration, "misogyny and extremism among Republicans have become what journalists sometimes call a dog-bites-man story." This normalization allows candidates with disqualifying scandals to survive where they would have been purged in previous eras.

DeLong highlights the case of House Speaker Mike Johnson attending fundraisers for Brandon Herrera, a candidate accused of sexist and anti-Semitic remarks. The author notes that Drezner finds it infuriating that there is little public hand-wringing about such alliances. The administration has actively intervened to shield these figures, telling party members to close ranks. This creates a feedback loop where scandal is no longer a liability but a badge of loyalty. A counterargument worth considering is whether this strategy is purely cynical or if it reflects a genuine shift in the voter base that rewards aggression over traditional decorum. Drezner, however, remains focused on the agency of the enablers who facilitate this shift.

They absolutely have a choice. Furthermore, they keep making their choice in a way that empowers the worst elements of their party.

The Load-Bearing Structure of Party Identity

The core of Drezner's argument, as presented by DeLong, rests on the data provided by G. Elliott Morris. DeLong explains that Morris found a stark divergence in voter behavior: subgroups that retain a "Republican" identity stay loyal to the party by a margin of 37 points, even if they disapprove of the leader. Conversely, those without that identity defect to the Democrats. This suggests that the "pivotal enablers" are not the most extreme MAGA faithful, who are often marginalized, but the "normie" Republicans who believe they are merely "renting their souls temporarily."

DeLong draws a parallel to historical moments of institutional decay, noting that this dynamic mirrors the "Crisis of the Third Century" in Rome, where the rapid turnover of emperors and the collapse of central authority were driven by military and bureaucratic factions prioritizing their own survival over the state's integrity. Similarly, the current political class prioritizes party identity over policy or principle. DeLong writes that figures like Bill Cassidy and Mike Johnson are the "true load-bearing structure" of the coalition. They are the ones who keep the system functioning despite its moral compromises.

When I see someone like Brandon Herrera or Max Miller pop up in the news, it does not take long to conclude that there's no point condemning someone who has already lost their soul. Mike Johnson and Bill Cassidy, however, likely think that they have only rented theirs temporarily.

Bottom Line

DeLong's commentary on Drezner's typology offers a sobering assessment of modern political complicity: the greatest threat to democratic norms comes not from the incompetent, but from the capable who choose to enable them. The argument's strength lies in its refusal to be distracted by the spectacle of buffoonery, focusing instead on the quiet, calculated decisions of the establishment. Its vulnerability, however, may be an underestimation of how deeply the "Stormtrooper" mentality has permeated the bureaucracy, suggesting that the problem is not just a few bad actors but a systemic failure that transcends individual choices.
