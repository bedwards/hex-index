This piece from Works in Progress dismantles a comfortable assumption held by housing reformers: that if we just build beautiful enough, people will stop saying no. It argues that the rise of restrictive zoning and heritage conservation was not a reaction to ugly modernist buildings, but rather a political shift that happened decades before modernism ever took hold. The article's most striking claim is that the real barrier to development isn't aesthetics, but a specific type of distant opposition that mobilizes around the mere idea of change.

The Timing Mismatch

The core of the argument rests on a historical timeline that many housing advocates overlook. The piece notes that "restrictive zoning emerged in Germany and Austria-Hungary in the 1890s and spread quickly thereafter." This is a crucial pivot point. While modernist architecture became dominant in the 1950s, the legal tools to ban new construction were already in place by the turn of the century. As the editors observe, "NIMBYism triumphed long before it became true that, in most people's eyes, the average new building was uglier than the average old one."

This reframing is powerful because it suggests that making buildings prettier is a band-aid on a structural wound. The article points out that even when developers create "lovely neighborhoods" like I'On in Charleston, South Carolina, they still face "fevered opposition" from locals who fear disruption regardless of the architecture's quality. The piece argues that "ugliness is probably not a major driver of opposition to development by local people," citing concerns like construction noise and pressure on services as far more potent motivators.

Ugliness is probably not a major driver of opposition to development by local people. Local opponents have many other concerns that are at least as important, like disruption from construction, loss of green space, pressure on local services, and diminished social exclusivity.

Critics might argue that this historical analysis ignores the visceral emotional impact of modernist brutalism on post-war communities. However, the data on zoning dates suggests that the legal framework was built on a desire for control and exclusivity, not a sudden collective hatred of glass and steel.

The Rise of the Distant Opponent

The article introduces a fascinating distinction between two types of opponents. While "NIMBYs" (Not In My Backyard) are neighbors worried about immediate impacts, the piece identifies a second group: "NITBYs," or "Not In That Backyard." These are people who live far away but feel a moral or aesthetic duty to judge a development. The editors suggest that "in certain contexts, the ugliness of a development can summon into being a huge cloud of NITBYs, whose interest in the scheme may indeed be superficial, but who can nonetheless be collectively potent."

This dynamic is most visible in the history of heritage conservation. The piece challenges the standard narrative that conservation laws were a necessary response to the "destructiveness of postwar planners." Instead, it argues that the rise of conservationism coincided with the ascendancy of modernist architecture, which "most people demonstrably find relatively ugly." This aesthetic shift mobilized the NITBYs to freeze the character of neighborhoods, even those they never visited.

The evidence presented is stark. In Berlin, the demolition rate of pre-1919 buildings has plummeted to a near-zero "0.018 percent." The article calculates that at this rate, "it will take North Rhine-Westphalia 7,951 years to demolish its prewar stock entirely." This extreme protectionism, the piece contends, is less about saving history and more about a widespread reaction to the perceived ugliness of the new.

Conservationism was a reaction to postwar redevelopment, as its proponents claimed. But it was less a reaction to what the modernists destroyed, than to what they created.

This argument gains weight when considering historical precedents. The article notes that in the 19th century, cities like Paris were undergoing massive demolition without the same level of resistance, proving that destruction itself wasn't the trigger. The trigger was the style of the replacement. This helps explain why current reform efforts often fail in historic districts; the opposition is driven by a deep-seated cultural preference for the past that transcends local property lines.

The Limits of Aesthetic Reform

The piece concludes with a sobering reality check for housing advocates. While beautiful design might help, it is not a silver bullet. The editors note that "even the most beautiful neighborhood only emerges after several years of extreme ugliness, while the buildings are under construction." Furthermore, the fundamental friction of development—loss of light, increased density, and infrastructure strain—remains regardless of whether the building looks like a palace or a warehouse.

The article suggests that the focus on aesthetics distracts from the real political battle: overcoming the institutional power of local zoning boards and the mobilized NITBYs. "Removing the problem of ugliness may reduce NIMBY zeal a little, but the number of NIMBYs it satisfies entirely will be small." This is a necessary correction to the idea that architecture alone can solve the housing crisis.

Bottom Line

This is a vital correction to the housing reform narrative, proving that the fight for density is not a battle against bad taste, but against a deeply entrenched political desire for stasis. While the piece effectively debunks the idea that better design will unlock development, it leaves the reader with a harder question: if aesthetics aren't the barrier, what political mechanisms can actually override the NITBYs who freeze our cities in amber? The strongest part of the argument is the historical timeline of zoning, which exposes the fragility of the "ugly building" excuse; its biggest vulnerability is the lack of a concrete policy solution beyond identifying the problem.
